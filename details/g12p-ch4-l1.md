# Reflection and Plane and Curved Mirrors

A makeup mirror magnifies your face because it is a concave mirror used close up, while the mirror on the side of a car shows a wide view because it is convex. The way mirrors form images follows from one simple rule, the law of reflection, together with a single equation that locates the image. This lesson covers both, and it introduces the sign conventions that decide whether an image is real or virtual.

## Key ideas

The law of reflection states that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, with both angles measured from the normal, the line perpendicular to the surface. A flat mirror forms a virtual image that is the same size as the object, upright, and located behind the mirror at the same distance as the object is in front. The image is called virtual because the light only seems to come from it; no light actually passes through it, and it cannot be caught on a screen.

Curved mirrors are usually spherical. A concave mirror curves inward and brings parallel rays together at the focal point, whereas a convex mirror curves outward and spreads the rays apart. The focal length is half of the radius of curvature: f = R / 2. The relation between the object distance d_o, the image distance d_i and the focal length is the mirror equation: 1 / f = 1 / d_o + 1 / d_i. The magnification is m = -d_i / d_o, which is the ratio of the image height to the object height, and a negative value means that the image is inverted.

The signs tell the story. A positive image distance means that the image is real, formed in front of the mirror where light actually crosses, and it can be projected on a screen. A negative image distance means that the image is virtual, formed behind the mirror. A concave mirror has a positive focal length. When the object is farther from the mirror than the focal point, the image is real and inverted. When the object is closer than the focal point, as with a makeup mirror, the image is virtual, upright and magnified. A convex mirror has a negative focal length and always forms a virtual, upright and smaller image, which is why it gives a wide field of view.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Image position.** An object is 30 cm from a concave mirror of focal length 10 cm. Find the image distance.
1. Rearrange the mirror equation: 1 / d_i = 1 / f - 1 / d_o = 1 / 10 - 1 / 30 = 3 / 30 - 1 / 30 = 2 / 30.
2. So d_i = 15 cm.
3. The positive sign shows that the image is real, in front of the mirror.

**Example 2: Magnification.** For the same mirror and object, find the magnification.
1. Use m = -d_i / d_o = -15 / 30.
2. The magnification is m = -0.50.
3. The image is inverted and half the size of the object.

**Example 3: A close object.** An object is 5.0 cm from the same mirror. Find the image and the magnification.
1. 1 / d_i = 1 / 10 - 1 / 5.0 = 1 / 10 - 2 / 10 = -1 / 10, so d_i = -10 cm.
2. The negative sign shows a virtual image behind the mirror.
3. The magnification is m = -(-10) / 5.0 = +2.0, so the image is upright and twice as large.

## Common mistakes

Students often confuse the sign conventions for real and virtual images. Another common mistake is to use the radius of curvature in place of the focal length, which is half as large. Many forget the minus sign in the formula for magnification, which makes inverted images appear upright. Finally, do not forget that when you invert reciprocals, you must invert the whole sum, not each term separately.
