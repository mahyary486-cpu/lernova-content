# Uniform Circular Motion

A car driving around a circular track, a point on the rim of a spinning wheel and the Moon traveling around Earth all move in circles. Such motion is everywhere, from washing machines to planets, and it is described by a small number of simple relations connecting the radius of the circle, the time for one turn and the speed. In this lesson we meet these relations and, more importantly, the surprising fact that an object moving at constant speed in a circle is still changing its velocity.

## Key ideas

Uniform circular motion is motion along a circle at constant speed. The word uniform refers to the speed, not to the velocity. The speed stays the same, but the velocity is a vector, and its direction changes at every instant. The velocity is always tangent to the circle, meaning that it points along the line that just touches the circle at the object's position. As the object moves on, this tangent line keeps turning, so the velocity keeps changing even though its size does not. This is why we say that the velocity is not constant, and why the motion is accelerated, a point that the next lesson develops.

Two quantities describe how often the motion repeats. The period T is the time for one complete revolution, measured in seconds. The frequency f is the number of revolutions per second, measured in hertz (Hz). They are inverses of each other: f = 1 / T. A wheel with a period of 0.5 s makes two turns each second, so its frequency is 2 Hz.

In one revolution the object travels once around the circumference, a distance of 2 pi r. Dividing by the period gives the speed: v = 2 pi r / T, which can also be written v = 2 pi r f. A bigger circle or a shorter period means a larger speed. Sometimes it is more convenient to measure how fast the angle changes. The angular speed is omega = 2 pi / T, in radians per second, and it is related to the ordinary speed by v = omega r. One full revolution equals 2 pi radians, which is the same as 360 degrees. All points of a rotating wheel share the same angular speed, but points farther from the center move faster along the circle.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Speed from the period.** An object goes once around a circle of radius 5.0 m every 10 s. Find its speed.
1. Use v = 2 pi r / T = 2 x 3.14 x 5.0 / 10.
2. The numerator is 31.4, so v = 31.4 / 10.
3. The speed is 3.1 m/s, directed along the tangent at each point.

**Example 2: Frequency and angular speed.** A wheel has a period of 0.50 s. Find its frequency and its angular speed.
1. The frequency is f = 1 / T = 1 / 0.50 = 2.0 Hz.
2. The angular speed is omega = 2 pi / T = 6.28 / 0.50.
3. The result is omega = 12.6 rad/s.

**Example 3: A point on a wheel.** A wheel of radius 0.30 m turns at 5.0 revolutions per second. Find the speed of a point on its rim.
1. The frequency is f = 5.0 Hz, and the circumference path gives v = 2 pi r f = 2 x 3.14 x 0.30 x 5.0.
2. The speed is v = 9.4 m/s.
3. As a check, omega = 2 pi f = 31.4 rad/s and v = omega r = 31.4 x 0.30 = 9.4 m/s, the same result.

## Common mistakes

A very common mistake is to say that the velocity is constant because the speed is constant. Velocity includes direction, and the direction keeps changing. Another mistake is to forget the factor 2 pi and use only the radius, or to use the area of the circle instead of its circumference. Students also mix up the period and the frequency, which are inverses: a long period means a low frequency. Finally, when a problem gives revolutions per minute, convert to revolutions per second before using the formulas, or the speed will come out wrong by a factor of 60.
