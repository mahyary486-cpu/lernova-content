# Predicting Products and the Activity Series

Once you can recognize the type of a reaction, you can predict its products. The method is to identify the type, swap partners using the correct charges, write correct formulas for the new compounds and balance the equation. For single and double replacement there is one more question to answer first: does the reaction actually happen? Some mixtures simply do not react, and the activity series and the solubility rules are the tools that tell the difference.

## Key ideas

The activity series ranks metals from the most reactive to the least reactive: lithium, potassium, barium, calcium, sodium, magnesium, aluminum, zinc, iron, nickel, tin, lead, then hydrogen, copper, silver and gold. A more reactive metal loses electrons more easily, and that is why it can push a less reactive metal out of a compound. The series is a record of this tendency, so it is a prediction tool, and you do not need to remember a separate fact for each reaction.

The rule for single replacement is simple. A metal replaces another metal in a compound only if it is higher in the series. A metal above hydrogen also replaces the hydrogen of an acid and releases hydrogen gas, while a metal below hydrogen, such as copper, does not. If the free element is lower than the one in the compound, nothing happens. For example, copper metal placed in a zinc solution does not react, but zinc placed in a copper solution does.

The rule for double replacement is about the products. The reaction occurs only if one of the new compounds is a precipitate, a gas or water, because that removes ions from the solution and drives the change. To decide whether a product is a precipitate, use the solubility rules. All nitrates and all salts of group 1 metals are soluble. Most chlorides are soluble, except AgCl and PbCl2. Most sulfates are soluble, except BaSO4 and PbSO4. If every product stays dissolved, the ions simply remain mixed and there is no reaction.

After writing the products, always check the charges when writing their formulas, and balance the equation. Then the coefficients give the mole ratios that you can use for amounts.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Predict the products of Mg + HCl.**
1. Magnesium is above hydrogen in the series, so a reaction occurs.
2. Magnesium forms Mg 2+ and pairs with Cl-, which gives MgCl2, and hydrogen is released as H2.
3. The balanced equation is Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2.

**Example 2: Predict the products of Al + CuCl2.**
1. Aluminum is above copper, so aluminum replaces copper.
2. Al 3+ with Cl- gives AlCl3, and copper is released as the metal.
3. Balance with 6 Cl: 2Al + 3CuCl2 -> 2AlCl3 + 3Cu. Each side has 2 Al, 3 Cu and 6 Cl.

**Example 3: Predict the products of BaCl2 + Na2SO4.**
1. Swapping partners gives BaSO4 and NaCl.
2. Barium sulfate is one of the insoluble sulfates, so it is a precipitate, and the reaction occurs.
3. The balanced equation is BaCl2 + Na2SO4 -> BaSO4 + 2NaCl.

## Common mistakes

A common error is to predict a reaction when the free metal is lower in the activity series than the metal in the compound, since in that case nothing happens. Another is using wrong charges for the new formulas, such as AlCl2 instead of AlCl3. Students also forget to balance after writing the products. Finally, some predict a double replacement even though all the products are soluble. If potassium nitrate and sodium chloride solutions are mixed, the ions only mix together, and there is no reaction.
