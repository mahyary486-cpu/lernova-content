# Metals and nonmetals

This topic explains how elements are divided into metals and nonmetals and how their properties differ.

## Key ideas

Most elements in the periodic table are metals. Only about twenty are nonmetals, and a few elements in between are called metalloids because they show properties of both groups.

Typical properties of metals:
- They have a shiny surface (metallic luster) when freshly cut or polished.
- They are good conductors of heat and electricity.
- They are malleable (can be hammered into sheets) and ductile (can be drawn into wires).
- They are solid at room temperature, except mercury, which is a liquid.
- Most have high melting points and high density.

Typical properties of nonmetals:
- They have a dull surface, except iodine and graphite, which are slightly shiny.
- They are poor conductors of heat and electricity. Graphite is an important exception: it conducts electricity.
- Solid nonmetals are brittle and break into pieces when hit.
- They can be solids (carbon, sulfur), liquids (bromine) or gases (oxygen, nitrogen).
- They usually have lower melting points and lower density than metals.

The properties of a material decide how we use it. Copper is used in electric wires because it conducts well and can be drawn into thin wires. Aluminum is used in aircraft and drink cans because it is light and does not corrode easily. Gold is used in jewelry because it stays shiny and does not react with air. Sulfur is used to make sulfuric acid, and carbon in the form of diamond is used to cut glass because it is very hard.

Alloys are mixtures of a metal with other elements. Steel (iron with a little carbon) is stronger than pure iron. Brass (copper and zinc) is harder than pure copper.

## Examples

Example 1: Choosing a material for a cooking pot.
- The pot must conduct heat well, so a metal is better than a nonmetal such as plastic.
- The handle must not conduct heat, so a nonmetal material such as plastic or wood is used.
- Result: the pot body is aluminum or steel and the handle is plastic.

Example 2: Using density to find mass.
- Iron has a density of about 7.87 g/cm^3. A block of iron has a volume of 50 cm^3.
- Formula: mass = density × volume.
- Mass = 7.87 g/cm^3 × 50 cm^3 = 393.5 g, which is about 0.39 kg.
- A 50 cm^3 piece of sulfur (density about 2.07 g/cm^3) has a mass of 2.07 × 50 = 103.5 g. The metal is almost four times heavier.

Example 3: Deciding whether an unknown solid is a metal.
- A student has a gray solid. It is shiny, bends without breaking, and makes a bulb light in a simple circuit.
- Shiny, malleable and conducting electricity are all metal properties.
- Conclusion: the solid is very likely a metal.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking all metals are hard and heavy. Sodium and potassium are soft enough to cut with a knife, and they float on water.
- Thinking all nonmetals are gases. Carbon and sulfur are solids, and bromine is a liquid.
- Saying that nonmetals never conduct electricity. Graphite, a form of carbon, conducts well.
- Forgetting that mercury is a metal even though it is a liquid at room temperature.

## Remember

- Metals are shiny, malleable, ductile and good conductors; nonmetals are usually dull, brittle and poor conductors.
- The use of a material depends on its properties.
- Metalloids, such as silicon, are between metals and nonmetals.
