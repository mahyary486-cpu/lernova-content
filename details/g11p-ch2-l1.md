# Density and Pressure

A person who stands on one foot presses harder on the ground than the same person standing on two feet, because the same weight acts on a smaller area. A sharp knife cuts better than a blunt one for the same reason. This lesson introduces two quantities that describe matter and the way forces spread over surfaces: density and pressure. They are the foundation for everything that follows about liquids and gases.

## Key ideas

Density tells how much mass is packed into a given volume. It is defined as rho = m / V, and its SI unit is kilograms per cubic meter (kg/m^3). Density is a property of the material, not of the size of the object: a small iron nail and a large iron beam have the same density. Water has a density of 1000 kg/m^3, which is a handy reference. Materials with a density greater than that of water, like iron, sink in it, and those with a smaller density, like wood or oil, float. Gases have very small densities, about 1 kg/m^3 for air.

Pressure tells how concentrated a force is. It is the force acting perpendicular to a surface divided by the area of the surface: P = F / A. The SI unit is the pascal (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N/m^2. One pascal is a very small pressure, so pressures are often given in kilopascals. The formula shows that for the same force, a smaller area gives a greater pressure. This explains why snowshoes keep you from sinking, why nails have sharp points, and why a tractor with wide tires does not sink into soft ground.

The air around us also has weight and exerts pressure. At sea level, atmospheric pressure is about 1.01 x 10^5 Pa. It decreases as altitude increases, because there is less air above. We do not feel this pressure because the fluids inside our bodies push outward with the same pressure. Engineers often use gauge pressure, which is the pressure measured above atmospheric pressure, as in a tire gauge. The absolute pressure is the gauge pressure plus the atmospheric pressure.

When using P = F / A, the force must be in newtons, so a mass in kilograms must first be changed to a weight, W = m g. The area must be in square meters, which is a common source of mistakes when the area is given in square centimeters.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Pressure from force and area.** A force of 200 N acts perpendicular to a surface of area 0.50 m^2. Find the pressure.
1. Use P = F / A = 200 / 0.50.
2. The pressure is P = 400 Pa.
3. If the same force acted on 0.050 m^2, the pressure would be ten times larger, 4000 Pa.

**Example 2: A person standing.** A 60 kg person stands on two feet with a total area of 0.040 m^2. Use g = 9.8 m/s^2. Find the pressure on the floor.
1. First find the force, which is the weight: F = 60 x 9.8 = 588 N.
2. Then P = F / A = 588 / 0.040.
3. The pressure is 14 700 Pa. Standing on one foot (half the area) would double it to 29 400 Pa.

**Example 3: Density of a block.** A block has a mass of 600 kg and a volume of 0.20 m^3. Find its density and say whether it floats in water.
1. Use rho = m / V = 600 / 0.20.
2. The density is 3000 kg/m^3.
3. This is greater than 1000 kg/m^3, so the block sinks in water.

## Common mistakes

The most common error is to put a mass in kilograms into P = F / A instead of the weight in newtons. Another is to confuse pressure with force; a large force can produce a small pressure if it is spread over a large area. Students also forget to convert square centimeters to square meters: 1 m^2 equals 10 000 cm^2, not 100 cm^2. Finally, do not think that density depends on the amount of material; a drop of water and a lake have the same density.
