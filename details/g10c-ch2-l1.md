# Organization of the Periodic Table

The periodic table arranges all known elements so that their properties can be predicted from their position. Understanding how it was built explains why it looks the way it does.

## Key ideas
- **Dobereiner (1829)** noticed triads, groups of three similar elements in which the middle element has an atomic mass close to the average of the other two.
- **Mendeleev (1869)** arranged elements by increasing atomic mass, placed similar elements in the same column, and left gaps for undiscovered elements. He correctly predicted the properties of germanium.
- **Moseley (1913)** showed that elements should be ordered by atomic number (the number of protons), not atomic mass. This fixed pairs such as tellurium and iodine.
- **Periodic law:** when elements are arranged by increasing atomic number, their properties repeat in a regular, periodic pattern.
- **Layout:** rows are called **periods** (7 in total) and columns are called **groups** (18 in total). Period lengths are 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32 and 32 elements.
- **Blocks:** the s-block (groups 1 and 2), p-block (groups 13 to 18), d-block (groups 3 to 12) and f-block (the two rows below the main table) are named for the sublevel being filled.

## Worked examples
**Example 1: Testing a triad.** Calcium has mass 40.08 amu and barium 137.33 amu. Predict the mass of strontium.
1. The middle element should be near the average.
2. (40.08 + 137.33) / 2 = 88.71 amu.
3. The real mass of strontium is 87.62 amu, so the triad rule works well.

**Example 2: Finding a position from atomic number.** Where is the element with Z = 16?
1. Periods 1 and 2 hold 2 + 8 = 10 elements, so period 3 holds Z = 11 to 18.
2. Z = 16 is the 6th element of period 3, and the p-block begins at group 13, so it is in group 13 + 3 = 16.
3. It is in period 3, group 16: sulfur.

**Example 3: Counting elements.** How many elements are in periods 1 to 4?
1. 2 + 8 + 8 + 18 = 36.
2. The last element is krypton, Z = 36.

## Common mistakes
- Saying the modern table is ordered by atomic mass. It is ordered by atomic number.
- Mixing up periods (rows) and groups (columns).
- Thinking Mendeleev's gaps were mistakes. They were predictions that were later confirmed.
- Forgetting that the f-block elements belong inside periods 6 and 7, even though they are drawn below the table.
