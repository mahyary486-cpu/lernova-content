# Organization of the Periodic Table

The periodic table arranges all the known elements so that their properties can be predicted from their position. It looks simple, but it is the result of more than a century of work, and knowing how it was built explains why it has its shape. In this lesson we follow the main steps of its history and then learn how the modern table is laid out.

## Key ideas

Early chemists noticed patterns among the elements. In 1829 Dobereiner found triads, groups of three similar elements in which the middle element has an atomic mass close to the average of the other two, such as calcium, strontium and barium. In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the elements by increasing atomic mass and placed those with similar properties in the same column. His great insight was to leave gaps for elements that had not yet been found, and to predict their properties. He described the missing element that we now call germanium, and when it was discovered, its properties matched his predictions closely. This success convinced scientists that the table reflects a real pattern.

Mendeleev's order by atomic mass had a few flaws, for instance tellurium and iodine seemed to be in the wrong order. In 1913 Henry Moseley, studying X-rays from atoms, showed that the elements should be ordered by atomic number, the number of protons, and this removed the problems. The modern periodic law states that when the elements are arranged by increasing atomic number, their properties repeat in a regular, periodic pattern.

The modern table has 7 horizontal rows, called periods, and 18 vertical columns, called groups. The periods have lengths of 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32 and 32 elements. Elements in the same group have similar properties, because they have the same number of valence electrons. The table is also divided into blocks named after the sublevel that is being filled: the s-block (groups 1 and 2), the p-block (groups 13 to 18), the d-block (groups 3 to 12), and the f-block, the two rows that are usually drawn below the main table. The f-block elements really belong inside periods 6 and 7, but they are drawn separately to keep the table compact.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Testing a triad.** Calcium has an atomic mass of 40.08 amu and barium has 137.33 amu. Predict the mass of strontium, which lies between them.
1. The middle element should be close to the average of the other two.
2. The average is (40.08 + 137.33) / 2 = 88.71 amu.
3. The real mass of strontium is 87.62 amu, so the triad rule works well.

**Example 2: Position from the atomic number.** Where is the element with Z = 16?
1. Periods 1 and 2 hold 2 + 8 = 10 elements, so period 3 holds Z = 11 to 18.
2. Period 3 begins with Na (Z = 11) in group 1, Mg (Z = 12) in group 2, then Al (Z = 13) in group 13. Counting on, Z = 16 falls in group 16.
3. The element is in period 3, group 16, and it is sulfur.

**Example 3: Counting elements.** How many elements are in periods 1 to 4?
1. Add the lengths of the periods: 2 + 8 + 8 + 18 = 36.
2. The last element of period 4 has Z = 36, which is krypton.

## Common mistakes

Students often say that the modern table is ordered by atomic mass, when it is ordered by atomic number. Another mistake is to mix up periods, which are rows, and groups, which are columns. Some think that Mendeleev's gaps were errors, but they were predictions that were later confirmed. Finally, remember that the f-block elements belong inside periods 6 and 7, even though they are drawn below the table.
