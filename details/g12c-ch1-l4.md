# Buffers and the Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation

A buffer is like a shock absorber for pH. When a small amount of acid or base is added to pure water, the pH changes by several units, but the same addition to a buffer changes it only a little. Living things depend on this. Blood must stay near pH 7.4, and a change of only a few tenths of a unit is dangerous. Buffers also make laboratory work, medicines and food production possible, since they keep the pH steady while reactions go on.

## Key ideas

A buffer is a solution that contains a weak acid and its conjugate base, in comparable amounts. The pair works because each member neutralizes one kind of disturbance. When acid is added, the conjugate base reacts with it: A- + H+ -> HA. When base is added, the weak acid reacts with it: HA + OH- -> A- + H2O. In both cases, a strong acid or base is replaced by a weak one, so the pH changes only slightly. A buffer has a limit, called its capacity, and when one of the two components is used up, the pH changes quickly. A buffer with larger concentrations has a larger capacity.

The pH of a buffer is found with the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: pH = pKa + log([A-] / [HA]). It is a rearranged form of the Ka expression. Two consequences follow. When the concentrations are equal, the logarithm of 1 is zero, so pH = pKa. And a ratio of 10 to 1 shifts the pH by one unit. For this reason, a buffer works best within about one unit of its pKa, and the acid for a given pH is chosen with a pKa close to that pH. Since both concentrations refer to the same volume, the volume cancels, and the ratio of moles can be used in place of the ratio of concentrations. This is convenient after an addition of acid or base, when the moles change but the volume is almost constant.

Blood is a natural example. The H2CO3 and HCO3- pair holds the pH near 7.4, and the lungs and kidneys adjust the amounts of the two components. A buffer cannot stop pH change completely. It only reduces it.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Find the pH of a buffer.** The solution is 0.20 M acetic acid and 0.40 M acetate, with pKa = 4.74.
1. Substitute into the equation: pH = 4.74 + log(0.40 / 0.20) = 4.74 + log 2.
2. The logarithm of 2 is 0.30, so pH = 4.74 + 0.30 = 5.04.

**Example 2: Add acid to a buffer.** To 1.0 L of a buffer containing 0.20 mol HA and 0.20 mol A- (pKa = 4.74), 0.02 mol of HCl is added.
1. The added H+ reacts with A-, so A- falls to 0.18 mol and HA rises to 0.22 mol.
2. pH = 4.74 + log(0.18 / 0.22) = 4.74 + log 0.818 = 4.74 - 0.09 = 4.65.
3. The pH changed by only 0.09 units. The same amount of HCl in 1.0 L of pure water would give a pH of 1.7.

**Example 3: Choose a buffer for pH 5.** Which acid is better, acetic acid with pKa = 4.74 or formic acid with pKa = 3.75?
1. The best buffer has a pKa within one unit of the target pH.
2. Acetic acid differs from 5 by 0.26 units, and formic acid by 1.25 units.
3. Acetic acid is the better choice.

## Common mistakes

A frequent error is to turn the ratio upside down and use [HA] / [A-], which gives the wrong sign for the correction. Another is to forget that moles, and not only concentrations, can be used in the ratio, since both are in the same volume. Students also believe that a buffer stops any change in pH. It does not. It limits the change, and a large addition of acid or base will use it up.
