# Conservation of Energy

A roller coaster car climbs slowly to the top of a hill, then rushes down and up the next hill. All along the ride, energy changes from one form to another, but there is one thing that never changes: the total amount of energy. This is the law of conservation of energy, one of the most basic laws of nature.

## Key ideas

The law of conservation of energy says that energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only change from one form to another or be transferred from one object to another. The total amount of energy in a closed system stays constant.

For an object that moves under gravity without friction, the sum of its kinetic and potential energy, called the mechanical energy, is constant: KE + PE = constant. This explains the motion of a falling object. As it falls, its height decreases, so its potential energy decreases, but its speed increases, so its kinetic energy increases by the same amount. On the way up, the opposite happens. At the top of a swing or of a hill, the potential energy is greatest and the kinetic energy is least; at the bottom, the kinetic energy is greatest.

In real situations, friction and air resistance are present. They change some of the mechanical energy into thermal energy and sometimes sound. This is why a bouncing ball does not return to the same height and a swing slowly comes to rest. The energy is not destroyed; it is spread out as heat in the air and the surfaces, where it is no longer useful for doing work. For this reason, people sometimes say that energy is "lost", but it would be more accurate to say that it is transformed into a less useful form.

Using conservation of energy is often easier than using forces. If you know the initial and final energies, you can find the speed at the bottom of a hill without knowing the details of the path.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Rolling down a hill.** A ball rolls down a smooth hill with no friction. What happens to its speed?
1. The ball loses height, so its potential energy decreases.
2. By conservation of energy, the lost potential energy turns into kinetic energy.
3. Since the kinetic energy increases, the ball speeds up.

**Example 2: A roller coaster.** A car at the top of the first hill has 50,000 J of potential energy and almost no kinetic energy. Ignore friction. What is its kinetic energy at the bottom, where the height is zero?
1. The total mechanical energy is 50,000 J and stays constant.
2. At the bottom the potential energy is zero.
3. So all of the energy is kinetic: KE = 50,000 J.

**Example 3: A bouncing ball.** A ball does not bounce back to its original height. Why?
1. At each bounce, some of the ball's energy becomes thermal energy and sound.
2. Less mechanical energy remains for the next rise.
3. So the ball rises to a lower height each time, although the total energy of the ball and its surroundings is unchanged.

## Common mistakes

Some students say that energy is "used up" or destroyed, but it only changes form. Another mistake is to forget the thermal energy that friction produces when a problem has friction. A third error is to add potential and kinetic energy from different objects without checking which system is being considered.
