# Reference Points and Speed

Everything in the universe is moving in some way, so to describe motion we always compare an object with something else. A passenger sitting in a train is at rest compared with the train, yet moving quickly compared with the ground outside. That comparison object is called the reference point, and a statement about motion makes sense only when we know which reference point is being used. Once we agree on that, we can ask how fast something moves, and the answer is its speed.

## Key ideas

Motion is a change in position relative to a reference point. If the position of an object compared with the reference point does not change, the object is at rest in that frame. When you say that a car is "moving at 60 km/h", you almost always mean relative to the road.

Speed tells how much distance an object covers in a certain time. It is calculated as speed = distance / time, or v = d / t. The SI unit is meters per second (m/s), but kilometers per hour (km/h) is common on roads. The same equation can be rearranged to find the distance, d = v x t, or the time, t = d / v. Choose the form that has the unknown quantity alone on one side.

Two kinds of speed are used. Instantaneous speed is the speed at one particular moment, like the reading on a car's speedometer. Average speed is the total distance divided by the total time for the whole trip, including stops and slow parts. For a trip made of several parts, you must add all the distances and add all the times, and then divide. You may not just average the speeds of the parts, because the object spends different amounts of time at each speed.

Speed is a scalar quantity. It has a size but no direction, so "20 m/s" is a complete answer for speed. The next lesson explains how adding direction gives velocity.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Average speed.** A car travels 150 km in 2 hours. What is its average speed?
1. Write the formula: v = d / t.
2. Substitute the distance and the time: v = 150 km / 2 h.
3. The average speed is 75 km/h. The car may have gone faster or slower at different moments, but this is its average over the trip.

**Example 2: Distance from speed.** A cyclist rides at a steady 6 m/s for 40 s. How far does the cyclist go?
1. Rearrange the formula to find the distance: d = v x t.
2. Substitute the numbers: d = 6 m/s x 40 s.
3. The seconds cancel and the distance is 240 m.

**Example 3: A trip in two parts.** A runner runs 100 m in 20 s and then another 100 m in 30 s. What is the average speed for the whole run?
1. The total distance is 100 m + 100 m = 200 m.
2. The total time is 20 s + 30 s = 50 s.
3. The average speed is 200 m / 50 s = 4 m/s.
4. Notice that the speeds of the two parts were 5 m/s and 3.33 m/s. Their simple average is 4.17 m/s, which is wrong, because the runner spent more time at the slower speed.

## Common mistakes

The most common error is to average two speeds instead of dividing the total distance by the total time. Another frequent mistake is mixing units, such as using kilometers for distance and seconds for time in the same formula without converting first. Some students also believe that an object at rest in one reference frame is at rest in every frame, but a person sitting on a train is at rest relative to the train and moving relative to the station.
