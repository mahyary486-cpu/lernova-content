# Parts of the Atom

Everything around you is made of atoms, and atoms are themselves made of even smaller particles. The way these particles are arranged decides which element an atom belongs to and how it behaves in chemical reactions. This lesson describes the three main particles, where each one is found, and how scientists came to the modern picture of the atom.

## Key ideas

At the center of every atom is the nucleus, a tiny region that contains two kinds of particles. Protons carry a positive electric charge, and neutrons carry no charge at all. Around the nucleus, in a region called the electron cloud, move the electrons, which carry a negative charge. The charge of an electron is equal in size and opposite in sign to that of a proton. Electrons are held near the nucleus by the attraction between opposite charges.

The three particles differ greatly in mass. A proton and a neutron have almost the same mass, while an electron is about 1800 times lighter. For this reason the nucleus, with its protons and neutrons, contains almost all of the mass of the atom. At the same time, the nucleus is extremely small compared to the whole atom. If an atom were the size of a stadium, the nucleus would be about the size of a pea at the center, and the rest would be mostly empty space where the electrons move. An atom is therefore not a solid ball.

A neutral atom has the same number of protons and electrons, so the positive and negative charges cancel and the total charge is zero. When the numbers are different, the atom becomes an ion, which is the subject of the next lesson.

Our picture of the atom has changed as scientists found new evidence. Dalton pictured atoms as solid, indivisible spheres. Thomson discovered the electron and imagined an atom as positive material with electrons embedded in it. Rutherford fired positive particles at a thin gold foil and found that most went straight through, but a few bounced back, so he concluded that the positive charge and most of the mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus. Bohr then proposed that electrons move in certain energy levels around the nucleus. The modern model describes an electron cloud, in which electrons are found in regions with a certain probability.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Charges.** An atom has 6 protons and 6 electrons. What is its overall charge?
1. The 6 protons give a charge of +6, and the 6 electrons give a charge of -6.
2. The charges add to zero, so the atom is neutral.

**Example 2: Which particle?** Which particle is found in the nucleus and has no charge?
1. Protons are in the nucleus but are positive, and electrons are negative and found outside the nucleus.
2. Neutrons are in the nucleus and have no charge, so the answer is the neutron.

**Example 3: Where is the mass?** Where is most of the mass of an atom found?
1. Electrons are about 1800 times lighter than protons or neutrons, so they add very little mass.
2. Protons and neutrons are in the nucleus, so the nucleus holds almost all of the mass, even though it takes up a tiny fraction of the volume.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to think that electrons are inside the nucleus. They move outside it, in the electron cloud. Another is to picture the atom as a solid ball, when it is mostly empty space. Students also think that neutrons carry a charge, but they are neutral, as the name suggests. Finally, remember that the number of protons, not the number of neutrons, defines the element.
