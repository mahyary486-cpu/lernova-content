# Ecosystems and food chains

An ecosystem is a community of living things interacting with each other and with the nonliving parts of their environment.

## Key ideas

- **Biotic and abiotic factors.** Biotic factors are living things: plants, animals, fungi and bacteria. Abiotic factors are nonliving: sunlight, water, air, soil, temperature and minerals. Both kinds affect which organisms can live in a place.
- **Population and community.** A population is all the organisms of one species in an area. A community is all the populations living together in that area. A community plus its abiotic factors is an ecosystem. Examples are a pond, a forest, a desert or a farm field.
- **Producers.** Plants, algae and some bacteria make their own food by photosynthesis, using light energy, water and carbon dioxide. Nearly all energy in an ecosystem starts with the Sun and enters through producers.
- **Consumers.** Consumers cannot make their own food. Herbivores eat plants, carnivores eat other animals and omnivores eat both. A primary consumer eats producers, a secondary consumer eats primary consumers, and so on.
- **Decomposers.** Fungi and bacteria break down dead plants and animals and waste. They return nutrients to the soil, where producers can use them again.
- **Food chains and food webs.** A food chain shows one path of energy, with arrows pointing from the food to the eater. Most animals eat more than one kind of food, so chains connect into a food web.
- **Energy flow.** Energy is lost as heat at every level, because organisms use energy for life activities. About 10% of the energy is passed on to the next level. This is why a food chain usually has only 4 or 5 levels and why there are fewer carnivores than herbivores.

## Examples

**Example 1: A grassland food chain.** Grass -> grasshopper -> frog -> snake -> eagle. Grass is the producer. The grasshopper is the primary consumer, the frog is the secondary consumer, the snake is the tertiary consumer and the eagle is the top consumer. The arrows show energy moving from grass toward the eagle.

**Example 2: Energy in a chain.** Suppose the grass in a field holds 10 000 kJ of energy. Using the 10% rule:
1. Grasshoppers receive about 10% of 10 000 = 1000 kJ.
2. Frogs receive about 10% of 1000 = 100 kJ.
3. Snakes receive about 10% of 100 = 10 kJ.
So very little energy is left for the top level. This is why a field can support thousands of grasshoppers but only a few snakes.

**Example 3: Removing one organism.** In a pond, algae are eaten by small fish, and small fish are eaten by larger fish. If a disease kills most of the small fish, algae can grow too much because fewer are eaten, and larger fish may starve or move away. This shows that all members of a food web depend on each other.

## Common mistakes

- Pointing the arrow the wrong way. The arrow points from the organism that is eaten to the organism that eats it, because it shows the flow of energy.
- Thinking that decomposers are not part of the food chain. They are always present and recycle nutrients.
- Believing that energy is recycled in an ecosystem. Nutrients are recycled, but energy flows in one direction and is lost as heat.
- Confusing a population with a community. A population has one species; a community has many species.

## Remember

- Producers capture the Sun's energy; consumers and decomposers pass it on or recycle the nutrients.
- Only about 10% of energy moves to the next level, so higher levels have fewer organisms.
- A food web is a group of connected food chains.
