# Photons and the Photoelectric Effect

A solar panel works because particles of light, called photons, give their energy to electrons in a single interaction. This idea, which Einstein introduced in 1905 to explain the photoelectric effect, was one of the starting points of quantum physics. The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a metal surface when light shines on it, and its details could not be explained by treating light only as a wave.

## Key ideas

Light comes in packets of energy called photons. A photon of frequency f has the energy E = h f = h c / lambda, where h = 6.63 x 10^-34 J s is Planck's constant. A photon of blue light carries more energy than one of red light, because its frequency is higher. Photon energies are tiny in joules, so they are often given in electron-volts, with 1 eV = 1.6 x 10^-19 J.

In the photoelectric effect, a photon is absorbed by a single electron in the metal. The electron needs a minimum energy to escape, called the work function, phi, which depends on the metal. If the photon energy is larger than phi, the electron leaves with the remaining energy as kinetic energy: KE_max = h f - phi. The threshold frequency f0 is the lowest frequency that can eject electrons, found from phi = h f0. Below this frequency no electrons are emitted, however bright the light.

Three observations could not be explained by the wave theory. First, below the threshold frequency nothing happens, even for very intense light. A wave should eventually deliver enough energy. Second, above the threshold the electrons come out immediately, with no delay. Third, the maximum kinetic energy depends on the frequency of the light and not on its intensity. In the photon picture, increasing the intensity means more photons per second, so more electrons are emitted, but each photon still has the same energy, and the maximum energy of the electrons stays the same.

The maximum kinetic energy can be measured with a stopping potential, V_s, the voltage that just stops the fastest electrons from reaching the collector: e V_s = KE_max. A graph of the stopping potential against frequency is a straight line, whose slope gives h / e. This confirmed Einstein's theory, and he received the Nobel Prize for it.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Photon energy.** Light has a wavelength of 500 nm. Find the energy of one photon.
1. Use E = h c / lambda = 6.63 x 10^-34 x 3.00 x 10^8 / 5.0 x 10^-7.
2. The numerator is 1.99 x 10^-25, so E = 3.98 x 10^-19 J.
3. Dividing by 1.6 x 10^-19 gives E = 2.5 eV.

**Example 2: Maximum kinetic energy.** Photons of energy 4.0 eV hit a metal with a work function of 2.5 eV. Find the maximum kinetic energy of the electrons.
1. Use KE_max = E - phi = 4.0 - 2.5.
2. The maximum energy is KE_max = 1.5 eV.
3. In joules this is 1.5 x 1.6 x 10^-19 = 2.4 x 10^-19 J.

**Example 3: Stopping potential.** Find the stopping potential for the electrons of the previous example.
1. Use e V_s = KE_max = 1.5 eV.
2. Dividing by e gives V_s = 1.5 V.
3. A reverse voltage of 1.5 V is enough to stop all of the electrons.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that brighter light can eject electrons below the threshold frequency; it only provides more photons of the same low energy. Another is mixing joules and electron-volts in the same formula. Many also use the wavelength in nanometers without converting to meters, or use the frequency in the wrong unit. Finally, remember that increasing the intensity increases the number of electrons but not their maximum energy.
