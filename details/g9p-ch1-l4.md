# Unit Conversions

Real problems seldom give every quantity in the same unit. A road sign shows kilometers per hour while a physics formula needs meters per second, and a recipe may use milliliters while a bottle is labeled in liters. Converting units correctly is therefore one of the most useful skills in physical science, and there is a reliable method for it called dimensional analysis.

## Key ideas

The method is based on conversion factors. A conversion factor is a fraction whose top and bottom are equal amounts written in different units, for example 1000 m / 1 km. Because the top and bottom are the same length, the fraction equals one, and multiplying by one does not change the quantity; it only changes the unit in which it is written. The important rule is to place the factor so that the unit you want to get rid of sits in the denominator, where it cancels with the unit in the number you started with. After all the cancelling, only the unit you want remains.

Useful facts to remember are 1 km = 1000 m, 1 m = 100 cm, 1 kg = 1000 g, 1 L = 1000 mL, 1 h = 60 min = 3600 s and 1 in = 2.54 cm. For speed, going from km/h to m/s means multiplying by 1000 / 3600, which is the same as dividing by 3.6; going back from m/s to km/h means multiplying by 3.6.

Some conversions need two or more steps. You simply chain the factors in one line and cancel unit by unit, for example hours to minutes and then minutes to seconds. Always write the unit with every number during the calculation. When the units cancel the way you expect, you have good evidence that the setup is correct.

Finally, check whether the answer is sensible. A larger unit needs fewer of them to express the same quantity, so converting from meters to kilometers should give a smaller number, and converting from hours to seconds should give a larger number. If the result looks far too large or too small, a conversion factor was probably written upside down.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Kilometers to meters.** Convert 5.2 km to meters.
1. We want to remove kilometers, so km must be in the denominator of the factor: 1000 m / 1 km.
2. Multiply: 5.2 km x (1000 m / 1 km). The kilometers cancel.
3. The result is 5200 m. This is a bigger number than 5.2, as expected when we change to a smaller unit.

**Example 2: Speed.** Convert 72 km/h to m/s.
1. Write the speed and then two factors, one for the distance and one for the time: 72 km/h x (1000 m / 1 km) x (1 h / 3600 s).
2. The km cancels and the h cancels, leaving m/s.
3. Calculate 72 x 1000 / 3600 = 72,000 / 3600 = 20 m/s. This agrees with the shortcut 72 / 3.6 = 20.

**Example 3: Hours to seconds.** How many seconds are in 3.5 hours?
1. Convert hours to minutes with 60 min / 1 h, and minutes to seconds with 60 s / 1 min: 3.5 h x (60 min / 1 h) x (60 s / 1 min).
2. Hours and minutes cancel, leaving seconds.
3. The result is 3.5 x 3600 = 12,600 s.

## Common mistakes

The most common error is to put the conversion factor upside down so that the units do not cancel; always check the units first. Another trap is converting squared units as if they were linear: 1 m^2 equals 10,000 cm^2, not 100 cm^2, because both length directions are converted. Some students drop the units during the calculation and then cannot tell whether the setup is right. Others never check whether the size of the answer makes sense, so an upside-down factor goes unnoticed.
