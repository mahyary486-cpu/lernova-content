# Properties of ionic compounds

Ionic compounds are crystal solids made of ions, and their strong attractions between ions explain their typical physical properties.

## Key ideas

In an ionic crystal every ion is held by strong forces from many neighbours of opposite charge. This single fact explains most of their properties.

- High melting and boiling points. A lot of energy is needed to break the strong attractions. Sodium chloride melts at about 801 °C.
- Hard but brittle. The crystal is hard because ions are locked in place. It is brittle because a blow shifts one layer of ions. Then ions of the same charge sit next to each other, repel, and the crystal splits.
- Solid state does not conduct electricity. The ions cannot move, and there are no free electrons.
- Molten or dissolved ionic compounds conduct electricity. In liquid or in solution the ions are free to move and carry charge.
- Many ionic compounds dissolve in water. Water molecules pull ions out of the lattice. However, some ionic compounds, such as calcium carbonate (limestone), dissolve very little.
- They are usually crystalline with regular shapes. Table salt crystals are small cubes.

How to test: connect a battery and a bulb to two electrodes. A salt crystal does not light the bulb. A salt solution does.

Uses linked to properties:

- Table salt (NaCl) is used in food and for melting ice on roads.
- Calcium carbonate (CaCO3) is used in building materials such as cement and plaster.
- Magnesium oxide (MgO) has a very high melting point (about 2850 °C), so it is used to line furnaces.
- Electrolytes (ionic solutions) are used in batteries.

## Examples

Example 1: Why does solid salt not conduct but salt water does?
- In solid NaCl, Na+ and Cl- are fixed in the lattice. No charge can move.
- When dissolved, the ions separate and move freely.
- Moving charged particles form an electric current, so the solution conducts.

Example 2: Why does a salt crystal break when hit with a hammer?
- The hit moves one layer of ions by one position.
- Now Na+ lies beside Na+ and Cl- beside Cl-. Like charges repel.
- The repulsion pushes the layers apart and the crystal cracks.

Example 3: Compare melting points.
- Sodium chloride melts at about 801 °C. Water (a covalent compound) melts at 0 °C.
- Difference = 801 - 0 = 801 °C.
- The ionic attractions are far stronger than the forces between water molecules.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that all ionic compounds dissolve in water. Some, like limestone, hardly dissolve.
- Saying that solid ionic compounds conduct because they contain ions. The ions must be free to move.
- Confusing hard with tough. Ionic crystals are hard but brittle.
- Thinking that molten salt contains molecules. It contains free ions.

## Remember

- Strong ionic attractions give high melting points and hard, brittle crystals.
- Ionic compounds conduct only when molten or dissolved.
