# Newton's Third Law

You cannot push on something without being pushed back. If you lean on a wall, the wall pushes on you; if you push a friend on a skateboard, you start to roll backward yourself. Newton's third law describes this and says that forces always come in pairs.

## Key ideas

Newton's third law says that whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object exerts a force of equal size and opposite direction on the first. These two forces are called an action-reaction pair. Which one is called the action and which the reaction does not matter; they exist together and at the same time.

The pair has four important properties. The forces are equal in size, they are opposite in direction, they exist at the same moment, and they act on different objects. The last property is the key to understanding the law. Because the two forces act on different objects, they can never cancel each other. To find out if an object accelerates, you add only the forces acting on that object, and the reaction force acts on another one.

The law explains many everyday events. A rocket pushes hot gas downward, and the gas pushes the rocket upward. A swimmer pushes the water backward and the water pushes the swimmer forward. When you walk, your foot pushes backward on the ground, and the ground pushes you forward. A good way to name a pair is "A pushes on B" and "B pushes on A".

Equal forces do not mean equal accelerations. Each object's acceleration is found from its own mass using a = F / m, so the lighter object accelerates more. When a small car and a large truck collide, the force on each is the same size, but the car's velocity changes much more.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: A rocket.** Why does a rocket rise?
1. The rocket engine pushes hot gas downward and out of the nozzle.
2. By the third law, the gas pushes the rocket upward with a force of the same size.
3. This upward force acts on the rocket, so it can accelerate. Space around the rocket is not needed for the push, because the rocket pushes on its own exhaust.

**Example 2: Two skaters.** Skater A has a mass of 60 kg and skater B has a mass of 40 kg. They push apart, and each feels a force of 120 N. Compare their accelerations.
1. The forces on the two skaters are equal in size, 120 N, and opposite in direction.
2. For A, a = 120 / 60 = 2 m/s^2.
3. For B, a = 120 / 40 = 3 m/s^2.
4. The lighter skater accelerates more, although the forces are equal.

**Example 3: Walking.** How do you move forward when you walk?
1. Your foot pushes backward on the ground.
2. The ground pushes forward on your foot with an equal force.
3. This forward force acts on you, so you accelerate forward. On slippery ice the ground cannot push back well, so walking is hard.

## Common mistakes

The biggest mistake is to think that action and reaction cancel each other. They do not, because they act on different objects. Another mistake is to think that a bigger object exerts a bigger force in a collision; the forces are equal, but the effects on the objects can be different. A third error is to confuse a pair with two forces acting on the same object, for example the weight of a book and the push of the table on it. Those two forces have the same size but are not a third-law pair.
