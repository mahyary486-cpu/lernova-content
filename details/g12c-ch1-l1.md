# Acid-Base Theories and Conjugate Pairs

Lemon juice, soap, stomach fluid and blood all owe their behavior to acids and bases. In the first years of chemistry, an acid was defined by its sour taste and a base by its slippery feel, but these descriptions explain nothing. A modern definition has to say what actually happens at the level of particles. The most useful one for reactions in water is that an acid-base reaction is a transfer of a proton, and every such transfer creates a pair of related substances called a conjugate pair.

## Key ideas

In the Bronsted-Lowry theory, an acid is a proton donor and a base is a proton acceptor. A proton here means a hydrogen ion, H+, which is a hydrogen atom without its electron. In water, the free proton is never alone. It attaches to a water molecule and forms the hydronium ion, H3O+. For this reason, acid-base reactions are written with a pair of substances: one gives up a proton and the other takes it.

When an acid donates a proton, what remains is its conjugate base, and when a base accepts a proton, it becomes its conjugate acid. The two members of a conjugate pair differ by exactly one H+ and nothing else. This is the point students often miss, because they think the difference is an OH group, but it is always a single proton. In every acid-base reaction there are two conjugate pairs, one on each side of the equation.

The strength of a conjugate pair is linked. The stronger an acid is, the more easily it gives up its proton, and the weaker its conjugate base is, because the base has little tendency to take the proton back. Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid, so the chloride ion is an extremely weak base and does not react with water. The reverse is also true, since a weak acid has a conjugate base that is noticeably basic.

Some substances can act as both an acid and a base, depending on what they react with. They are called amphoteric, or amphiprotic when the behavior involves protons. Water is the best-known example. It accepts a proton from an acid, and it donates one to a base. Even by itself, water undergoes a small amount of self-ionization: 2 H2O <-> H3O+ + OH-. Another example is the hydrogen carbonate ion, HCO3-, which can lose a proton to become CO3 2- or gain one to become H2CO3.

The Lewis theory is wider. A Lewis acid is an electron-pair acceptor and a Lewis base is an electron-pair donor. Every Bronsted base is also a Lewis base, but the Lewis theory includes reactions without any proton, such as BF3 accepting the lone pair of NH3 to form a bond.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Identify the conjugate pairs in NH3 + H2O <-> NH4+ + OH-.**
1. Ammonia accepts a proton, so it is the base, and the NH4+ that it forms is its conjugate acid.
2. Water donates a proton, so it is the acid, and the OH- that remains is its conjugate base.
3. The pairs are NH3 and NH4+, and H2O and OH-. In each pair the members differ by one H+.

**Example 2: What is the conjugate base of H2SO4?**
1. The conjugate base is formed by removing one proton from the acid.
2. H2SO4 minus H+ leaves HSO4-, which is the conjugate base.
3. Notice that HSO4- can still give up a second proton, so it is itself a weak acid.

**Example 3: Write HCO3- acting as an acid with water.**
1. As an acid, the ion donates one proton to the water.
2. The water becomes H3O+ and the ion becomes CO3 2-.
3. The equation is HCO3- + H2O <-> CO3 2- + H3O+, and the charges balance: -1 on the left and -2 + 1 = -1 on the right.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that conjugates differ by an OH group, but they differ by one H+. Another error is to call a substance amphoteric only when it reacts with water. In fact, the property is general, and HCO3- shows it too. The third mistake is to forget that the conjugate base of a strong acid is very weak, so that Cl- and NO3- do not make solutions basic.
