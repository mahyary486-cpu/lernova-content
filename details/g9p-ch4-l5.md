# Simple Machines and Efficiency

Using a ramp to load a heavy box onto a truck takes less effort than lifting it straight up. But there is a price: you must push the box over a longer distance. Machines help us do work more conveniently, and understanding what they can and cannot do is the main point of this lesson.

## Key ideas

A machine is a device that changes the size or the direction of a force, or the distance over which it acts. It does not create energy. The six classic simple machines are the lever, the pulley, the wheel and axle, the inclined plane, the wedge and the screw. Many complex machines, such as a bicycle or a pair of scissors, are combinations of them.

A machine can make a job easier by letting you use a smaller force. The effort force is the force that you apply, and the load is the force that the machine must overcome. The mechanical advantage is the load divided by the effort. A mechanical advantage of 5 means that the machine multiplies the force by five. But the machine does not give anything for free: when the force is smaller, the distance over which you must apply it is larger. A long ramp needs a small push over a long distance, and a short, steep ramp needs a large push over a short distance, but in both cases the work is about the same.

The work that a machine delivers can never be greater than the work that is put in. Part of the input work is always lost to friction as thermal energy, so the output is less than the input. The efficiency tells how much of the input work becomes useful output work: efficiency = (useful work out / work in) x 100%. A real machine always has an efficiency below 100%, and reducing friction by lubrication or by using wheels increases it.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Mechanical advantage.** A lever lifts a 100 N load with an effort of 20 N. What is the mechanical advantage?
1. Use MA = load / effort.
2. Substitute: MA = 100 / 20.
3. The mechanical advantage is 5. The lever multiplies the force by five, and the effort moves five times as far as the load.

**Example 2: Efficiency.** A machine takes in 400 J of work and delivers 300 J of useful work. What is its efficiency?
1. Use efficiency = (output work / input work) x 100%.
2. Substitute: (300 / 400) x 100%.
3. The efficiency is 75%. The other 100 J changes to thermal energy because of friction.

**Example 3: A ramp.** A worker raises a load with a ramp instead of lifting it straight up. What does the ramp change?
1. The ramp is longer than the vertical height.
2. So the force that is needed is smaller, but it has to act over a longer distance.
3. The work is about the same, apart from friction, so the ramp trades force for distance.

## Common mistakes

Students often believe that a machine can give more work out than the work put in, but that would violate conservation of energy. Another mistake is to report an efficiency above 100%; a real machine is always below that value. A third common error is to forget that using a smaller force always means moving over a greater distance.
