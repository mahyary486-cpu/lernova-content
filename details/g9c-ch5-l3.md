# Indicators

A strip of paper can tell you whether a liquid is acidic or basic, and it does so by changing color. This is how indicators work. They are used in laboratories, swimming pools and gardens, and they are the simplest way to find out the nature of a solution without tasting it or using electrical equipment. In this lesson we meet the most common indicators and learn what their colors tell us.

## Key ideas

An indicator is a substance that changes color depending on the pH of the solution it is in. Its molecules have one color in acidic conditions and another in basic conditions, and the color changes around a particular pH value. Different indicators change at different pH values, so the choice depends on what we want to know.

Litmus is the most familiar indicator, and it is used on paper strips. It is red in acid and blue in base. Blue litmus paper turns red in an acid, and red litmus paper turns blue in a base. In a neutral solution, the colors of the papers do not change. Litmus tells only whether a solution is an acid or a base, and not how strong it is. Phenolphthalein is colorless in acids and in neutral solutions, and turns pink in bases, from a pH of about 8.3. It is often used in titrations, because the sudden appearance of the pink color marks the end point very clearly.

Universal indicator is a mixture of several indicators, and it shows a whole range of colors that give an approximate pH value. The usual colors are red for strong acids, then orange and yellow for weak acids, green for neutral solutions, blue for weak bases and purple for strong bases. A color chart is matched to the result. This makes it much more informative than litmus.

Some indicators can be found at home. Red cabbage juice contains natural pigments that turn red in acids, purple in neutral solutions and blue or green in bases, and it can be used to test lemon juice, soap, baking soda and many other household liquids.

No indicator gives an exact pH, because the colors are judged by eye and the chart has limited steps. A pH meter, an electronic instrument with a probe, measures the pH accurately, to a fraction of a unit. It is used when the exact value matters, such as in food production, in water treatment and in medicine.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: A litmus test.** A piece of blue litmus paper is dipped in a solution and turns red. What does this show?
1. Blue litmus turns red only in an acid.
2. The solution is acidic, and its pH is below 7.

**Example 2: Phenolphthalein.** A few drops of phenolphthalein are added to a colorless solution and it turns pink. What does this show?
1. Phenolphthalein turns pink only in basic solutions.
2. The solution is basic, with a pH above about 8.

**Example 3: Universal indicator.** A solution turns universal indicator green. What can you say about it?
1. Green is the color for a neutral solution on the universal indicator chart.
2. The pH is about 7, so the solution is neither acidic nor basic.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to think that phenolphthalein turns pink in acids, but it is pink in bases and colorless in acids. Another is to think that an indicator shows the exact pH, when it only gives an approximate value, or just the type of solution in the case of litmus. Finally, students mix up the litmus colors. Remember that acid turns blue litmus red.
