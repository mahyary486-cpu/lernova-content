# Refraction, Lenses and Optical Instruments

Glasses, cameras, microscopes and the human eye itself all use lenses to form images. A lens works because light changes speed and direction when it enters glass from air. This bending is called refraction, and it is governed by Snell's law. Once we know how a single lens forms an image, we can understand how glasses correct vision and how instruments such as the microscope magnify small things.

## Key ideas

Light travels at its fastest in a vacuum, and more slowly in materials. The refractive index of a material is n = c / v, where c is the speed of light in vacuum and v the speed in the material. Snell's law describes the change of direction at a boundary: n1 sin(theta1) = n2 sin(theta2), where the angles are measured from the normal. A ray that enters a material with a higher index bends toward the normal, and one that enters a material with a lower index bends away from it.

A lens uses refraction at its two curved surfaces. A converging lens is thicker in the middle and brings parallel rays to a focus; it has a positive focal length. A diverging lens is thinner in the middle and spreads the rays; it has a negative focal length. For a thin lens, the relation between the object distance, the image distance and the focal length is the same as for mirrors: 1 / f = 1 / d_o + 1 / d_i, and the magnification is m = -d_i / d_o. A positive d_i means a real image, on the opposite side of the lens from the object, while a negative one means a virtual image on the same side as the object.

The power of a lens is P = 1 / f, with f in meters, and it is measured in diopters. A short focal length means a high power. When lenses are placed in contact, their powers add: P = P1 + P2, which is how opticians combine lenses.

The eye is a converging lens system. In short sight (myopia) the eye focuses the light in front of the retina, and a diverging lens corrects it. In long sight (hyperopia) the focus falls behind the retina, and a converging lens corrects it. A magnifying glass is a converging lens used with the object inside the focal length, giving a virtual, upright, enlarged image, and a microscope uses two lenses in sequence to magnify even more.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Image from a lens.** An object is 20 cm from a converging lens with f = 10 cm. Find the image.
1. 1 / d_i = 1 / 10 - 1 / 20 = 2 / 20 - 1 / 20 = 1 / 20, so d_i = 20 cm.
2. The magnification is m = -20 / 20 = -1.
3. The image is real, inverted and the same size as the object.

**Example 2: The power of a lens.** A lens has a focal length of 25 cm. Find its power.
1. Convert the focal length to meters: f = 0.25 m.
2. P = 1 / f = 1 / 0.25.
3. The power is 4.0 diopters.

**Example 3: A diverging lens.** An object is 30 cm from a lens with f = -15 cm. Find the image.
1. 1 / d_i = 1 / f - 1 / d_o = -1 / 15 - 1 / 30 = -2 / 30 - 1 / 30 = -3 / 30.
2. So d_i = -10 cm.
3. The image is virtual and upright, and the magnification m = -(-10) / 30 = +0.33 shows that it is smaller than the object.

## Common mistakes

Students often use centimeters for the focal length when calculating the power in diopters, which gives an answer that is wrong by a factor of 100. Another is to forget the negative sign of the focal length of a diverging lens. Many also mix up the corrections for short sight and long sight: short sight needs a diverging lens, and long sight needs a converging lens. Finally, check the sign of the image distance before saying whether an image is real or virtual.
