# Covalent Bonds and Lewis Structures

When two nonmetal atoms meet, neither can take electrons from the other, because both attract electrons strongly. Instead, they share them. A covalent bond is formed when two atoms share a pair of electrons, and the group of atoms bonded this way is called a molecule. Lewis structures are diagrams that show how the shared pairs and the unshared pairs are placed, and they are the first step toward predicting the shape and the properties of a molecule.

## Key ideas

A single bond shares one pair of electrons, a double bond shares two pairs, and a triple bond shares three. The more pairs that are shared, the shorter and stronger the bond. Typical energies are 347 kJ/mol for C-C, 614 kJ/mol for C=C and 839 kJ/mol for a carbon-carbon triple bond. Valence pairs that are not shared are called lone pairs, and they belong to one atom only.

There is a standard procedure for drawing a Lewis structure. First, add up all the valence electrons, adding one for each negative charge and subtracting one for each positive charge. Second, place the least electronegative atom, but never hydrogen, in the center, and join the other atoms to it with single bonds. Third, complete the octets of the outer atoms with lone pairs. Fourth, put any leftover electrons on the central atom. Fifth, if the central atom has less than an octet, move lone pairs from the outer atoms into the bonds, making double or triple bonds. Hydrogen is always an outer atom, because it forms only one bond, and it holds only 2 electrons.

Formal charge helps to choose between possible structures. It is calculated as the valence electrons of the free atom, minus the nonbonding electrons, minus the number of bonds. In NH4+, nitrogen has 5 valence electrons, no lone pairs and 4 bonds, so its formal charge is 5 - 0 - 4 = +1. The best structure is usually the one in which the formal charges are as close to zero as possible. When more than one valid structure can be drawn, the molecule shows resonance. In nitrate, NO3-, the double bond can be placed on any of the three oxygen atoms, and the real structure is an average of the three, with all three bonds identical.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Lewis structure of CO2.** Draw the structure.
1. The electrons are 4 + 2 x 6 = 16.
2. Place carbon in the center and join the oxygens with single bonds: O-C-O uses 4 electrons, so 12 remain.
3. Giving each oxygen three lone pairs leaves carbon with only 4 electrons, so move lone pairs into bonds to make two double bonds: O=C=O. Each oxygen keeps 2 lone pairs, and every atom has an octet.

**Example 2: Valence electrons in NO3-.** Count the electrons to be drawn.
1. Nitrogen has 5, and three oxygens have 3 x 6 = 18.
2. The negative charge adds 1.
3. The total is 5 + 18 + 1 = 24 electrons, and the structure has three resonance forms.

**Example 3: Energy to break bonds.** How much energy is needed to break 3.0 mol of H-H bonds, if the bond energy is 436 kJ/mol?
1. Energy = moles x bond energy = 3.0 mol x 436 kJ/mol.
2. The energy is 1308 kJ, which is about 1.3 x 10^3 kJ.

## Common mistakes

Students often forget to adjust the electron count for an ion. Another mistake is to make hydrogen the central atom, but it forms only one bond. Some give hydrogen an octet, while it holds only 2 electrons. Finally, a Lewis structure shows the connections and not the three-dimensional shape of the molecule.
