# Groups, Periods and Families

The position of an element in the periodic table tells you its electron arrangement, and the electron arrangement controls its chemical behavior. This is why the table is so useful: once you know where an element sits, you can say how many valence electrons it has, what ion it is likely to form and which other elements it resembles. This lesson shows how to read these facts from the table and how to use them to write formulas.

## Key ideas

For the main-group elements, the group number tells the number of valence electrons, the electrons in the outermost energy level. Group 1 has 1 valence electron, group 2 has 2, and groups 13 to 18 have 3 to 8, so for these groups the number of valence electrons equals the group number minus 10. The period number is equal to the highest occupied energy level, n. Elements in the same group therefore have the same valence electrons but different numbers of energy levels, and this is why they behave similarly but not identically.

Several groups are called families and have names. Group 1 contains the alkali metals, which are very reactive and form 1+ ions. Group 2 contains the alkaline earth metals, which form 2+ ions. Group 17 contains the halogens, which are reactive nonmetals that form 1- ions. Group 18 contains the noble gases, which have a full outer level and are very unreactive. Groups 3 to 12 are the transition metals. Hydrogen is a nonmetal, even though it is placed above group 1.

The charge of the ion that an element forms can be predicted from its group. Metals lose their valence electrons to reach the configuration of a noble gas, so groups 1, 2 and 13 form 1+, 2+ and 3+ ions. Nonmetals gain electrons to fill their outer level, so group 15 forms 3- ions, group 16 forms 2- ions and group 17 forms 1- ions.

These charges give the formula of an ionic compound, because the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge. To find the formula, find the lowest common multiple of the two charges, and use enough ions of each kind to reach it. This is the same as criss-crossing the charges and reducing the result.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Position from the configuration.** An atom has the configuration 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p3. Find its period, group and name.
1. The highest level is n = 3, so the atom is in period 3.
2. The valence electrons are 3s2 3p3, which makes 5 electrons.
3. For the p-block, the group is 10 + 5 = 15. The atom has 15 electrons in total, so it is phosphorus.

**Example 2: Formula of magnesium nitride.** Magnesium is in group 2 and nitrogen is in group 15.
1. Magnesium forms Mg 2+ and nitrogen forms N 3-.
2. The lowest common multiple of 2 and 3 is 6, so use 3 Mg (a total of +6) and 2 N (a total of -6).
3. The formula is Mg3N2.

**Example 3: Formula of aluminum oxide.** Aluminum is in group 13 and oxygen is in group 16.
1. Aluminum forms Al 3+ and oxygen forms O 2-.
2. The lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6, so use 2 Al (+6) and 3 O (-6).
3. The formula is Al2O3.

## Common mistakes

Students often use the period number as the number of valence electrons, when the group tells that. Another mistake is to write a formula such as MgN without balancing the charges. Some think hydrogen is an alkali metal because it is above group 1, but it is a nonmetal. Finally, elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons but a different number of energy levels, so they are similar and not identical.
