# What fossils are and how they form

A fossil is the preserved remains or traces of a living thing that lived a very long time ago, usually more than about 10,000 years ago.

## Key ideas

- Fossils are found mostly in sedimentary rock. Sediments are small pieces of rock, sand and mud that settle in layers. Heat and pressure in igneous and metamorphic rock usually destroy living remains.
- Most living things never become fossils. The body must be protected quickly from scavengers, air and bacteria that cause decay.
- Hard parts such as shells, bones and teeth fossilize much more often than soft parts such as skin, muscles and leaves.
- The main steps of fossil formation are:
  1. A plant or animal dies, often in water, a lake or a river delta.
  2. Mud, sand or volcanic ash quickly covers the body.
  3. Over time, more layers pile up on top. The lower layers are pressed and slowly turn into rock.
  4. Water with dissolved minerals moves through the buried remains. Minerals fill small spaces or slowly replace the original material.
  5. Millions of years later, movement of Earth's crust and erosion bring the rock to the surface, where people can find the fossil.
- Good places for fossil formation are quiet seas, lakes, swamps and river floodplains, because sediment settles there often and quickly.
- Paleontologists are scientists who study fossils. The word comes from old words meaning "ancient" and "being".
- Fossils form slowly. A fossil is usually at least several thousand years old, and many are millions of years old.

## Examples

**Example 1: A fish in a quiet lake.** A small fish dies and sinks to the bottom of a calm lake. There is little oxygen at the bottom, so few bacteria and scavengers can reach it. Fine mud settles over the body within weeks. As the years pass, the mud becomes shale. The fish's bones are kept in the rock as a fossil. If the same fish had died on dry land and stayed in the open air, it would have been eaten or would have decayed and left no fossil.

**Example 2: A shell on a seafloor.** A sea snail dies and its hard shell falls to the seafloor. Sand covers the shell. Water carrying dissolved minerals seeps in and fills the empty spaces inside the shell. The minerals harden. After millions of years, the sea dries up and the rock is lifted into a mountain. A hiker later finds a stone shell high above sea level. This case also shows that the place was once under the sea.

**Example 3: Why soft animals are rare as fossils.** A jellyfish has no bones or shell, and its body is mostly water. After it dies, it decays in a few days. Fossil jellyfish are very rare and form only in very special conditions, such as burial in very fine mud. A clam has a hard shell, so many fossil clams are found. This explains why the fossil record is not complete: it shows mostly animals with hard parts.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking every dead animal becomes a fossil. In fact, only a very small number of living things are preserved.
- Thinking a fossil is the original bone that stayed unchanged. Often the original material has been replaced by minerals, so the fossil is partly or fully stone.
- Thinking fossils are found in all kinds of rock. They are found mainly in sedimentary rock.
- Thinking fossils form quickly. Burial may be fast, but turning into a fossil takes a very long time.

## Remember

- A fossil is preserved evidence of ancient life, found mostly in sedimentary rock.
- Quick burial and hard body parts greatly increase the chance of fossil formation.
