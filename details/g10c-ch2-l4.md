# Ionization Energy and Electronegativity

Two trends describe how strongly atoms hold on to electrons. Ionization energy tells how hard it is to remove an electron from an atom, and electronegativity tells how strongly an atom in a bond attracts the shared electrons. Both follow the same pattern across the periodic table, and both help to predict what kind of bond two atoms will form.

## Key ideas

The first ionization energy (IE1) is the energy needed to remove the outermost electron from a gaseous atom: A -> A+ + e-. It is usually given in kJ/mol. IE1 generally increases across a period, because the nuclear pull is stronger and the atom is smaller, so the outer electron is held more tightly. It decreases down a group, because the outer electron is farther away and shielded. Small dips occur from group 2 to group 13, where the electron comes from a higher-energy p subshell, and from group 15 to group 16, where electron pairing in an orbital adds some repulsion.

Successive ionization energies are the energies needed to remove the second, the third and further electrons. Each is larger than the one before, because the remaining electrons are held by the same nuclear charge, with less repulsion. A very large jump between two successive values shows that the next electron comes from an inner level, so the jump reveals the number of valence electrons. If the big jump comes after the second ionization energy, the element has 2 valence electrons, and it belongs to group 2.

Electronegativity (EN) is the ability of an atom to attract the shared electrons in a chemical bond. It follows the same trend as the ionization energy, rising across a period and falling down a group. Fluorine has the highest value, 4.0, and francium is among the lowest. The noble gases are usually left out, since they hardly form bonds. The difference in electronegativity between two bonded atoms predicts the type of bond. A difference below about 0.4 gives a nonpolar covalent bond, a difference from 0.4 to 1.7 gives a polar covalent bond, and a difference above 1.7 gives an ionic bond.

It should not be forgotten that the ionization energy is a property of a free gaseous atom, while electronegativity is a property of an atom inside a bond. Electron affinity is a third, different quantity: the energy change when an atom gains an electron.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Energy for a sample.** The first ionization energy of sodium is 496 kJ/mol. What energy is needed to ionize 0.250 mol of sodium atoms?
1. Energy = moles x IE1.
2. Energy = 0.250 mol x 496 kJ/mol.
3. The energy is 124 kJ.

**Example 2: Energy for one atom.** The first ionization energy of lithium is 520 kJ/mol. Find the energy needed for one atom.
1. Convert to joules: 520 kJ = 5.20 x 10^5 J.
2. Divide by Avogadro's number: 5.20 x 10^5 / 6.022 x 10^23.
3. The energy is 8.63 x 10^-19 J per atom.

**Example 3: Bond type in water.** Decide the type of the O-H bond in water, with EN of H = 2.1 and EN of O = 3.5.
1. The difference is 3.5 - 2.1 = 1.4.
2. This lies between 0.4 and 1.7, so the bond is polar covalent.
3. Oxygen carries the partial negative charge, because it attracts the shared electrons more strongly.

## Common mistakes

Students often confuse ionization energy, which is absorbed to remove an electron, with electron affinity, the change when an electron is gained. Another mistake is to forget to convert kJ to J when working with a single atom. Some say that the ionization energy decreases across a period, but it generally increases. Finally, noble gases are normally given no electronegativity value, so do not assume that they have a high one.
