# Parametric Equations

Parametric equations describe a curve by writing both x and y as functions of a third variable, the **parameter**, usually t. As t changes, the point (x(t), y(t)) moves along the curve, so the equations also show direction and speed of motion.

## Key ideas
- Plot points by choosing values of t and calculating x and y.
- **Eliminating the parameter** gives an equation in x and y only. Solve one equation for t and substitute into the other, or use an identity.
- Useful identity: cos^2 t + sin^2 t = 1.
  - x = r cos t, y = r sin t gives x^2 + y^2 = r^2 (a circle).
  - x = a cos t, y = b sin t gives x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 = 1 (an ellipse).
  - x = h + r cos t, y = k + r sin t gives (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2.
- A line through (x1, y1) and (x2, y2): x = x1 + (x2 - x1)t, y = y1 + (y2 - y1)t. At t = 0 you are at the first point and at t = 1 at the second.
- The domain of t can restrict the curve. Eliminating the parameter may add points the curve never reaches.
- Many different parametrizations give the same graph.

## Worked examples

**Example 1. Eliminate t from x = t^2, y = t + 1.**
1. Solve y = t + 1 for t: t = y - 1.
2. Substitute: x = (y - 1)^2.
3. This is a parabola opening to the right with vertex (0, 1).

**Example 2. Eliminate t from x = 4 cos t, y = 2 sin t.**
1. cos t = x/4 and sin t = y/2.
2. Use cos^2 t + sin^2 t = 1: x^2/16 + y^2/4 = 1.
3. This is an ellipse. As t goes from 0 to 2 pi, the point moves counterclockwise starting at (4, 0).

**Example 3. Parametrize the segment from (1, 2) to (4, 8).**
1. Changes: 4 - 1 = 3 and 8 - 2 = 6.
2. x = 1 + 3t, y = 2 + 6t for 0 <= t <= 1.
3. Check: t = 1 gives (4, 8).

## Real-world use
The path of a thrown ball is x = (v cos a)t, y = (v sin a)t - 16t^2 in feet. Time is the natural parameter.

## Common mistakes
- Forgetting to restrict the domain after eliminating the parameter.
- Dividing by the wrong number: x = 4 cos t gives cos t = x/4, so it becomes x^2/16.
- Treating the parameter as an axis. It does not appear on the graph.
- Substituting t into only one equation when finding a point.
