# Rate Laws and Reaction Order

The rate of a reaction depends on the concentrations of the reactants, but the dependence cannot be guessed from the balanced equation. It must be found by experiment. The rate law is an equation that states exactly how the rate depends on each concentration, and it is the main result of a kinetic study. With it, we can predict the rate for any concentrations, find how long a reaction will take, and learn something about the steps through which the reaction proceeds.

## Key ideas

For a reaction with reactants A and B, the rate law has the form rate = k [A]^m [B]^n. The number k is the rate constant, which depends on the temperature and on the reaction, but not on the concentrations. The exponents m and n are the orders of the reaction in A and in B, and they are found from experiments. The overall order is the sum m + n. Note that the exponents are not the coefficients of the balanced equation, except by coincidence, because a reaction often happens in several steps, and the rate law reflects those steps.

The order is found by comparing experiments in which one concentration is changed and the others are kept constant. If doubling [A] has no effect on the rate, the reaction is zero order in A. If doubling [A] doubles the rate, it is first order. If doubling [A] makes the rate four times as large, it is second order. In general, if the concentration is multiplied by a factor and the rate is multiplied by a factor f, the order is the exponent that connects the two.

The units of k depend on the overall order, since the rate has the units M/s. For a first-order reaction, k has the units s^-1, and for a second-order reaction, L/(mol s). The units of k are a useful check on the order.

For a first-order reaction, there is a simple result for the half-life, the time needed for half of the reactant to be used up: t(1/2) = 0.693 / k. The half-life of a first-order reaction does not depend on the starting concentration, so after each half-life the amount left is half of what it was. This is the behavior of radioactive decay, and it is also used to describe how drugs are removed from the body.

Many reactions take place in a sequence of elementary steps. The slowest step, called the rate-determining step, controls the speed of the overall process, in the same way that the slowest worker on an assembly line controls the output.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Finding the order.** Doubling the concentration of A changes the rate from 2.0 M/s to 8.0 M/s.
1. The rate is 8.0 / 2.0 = 4 times larger, while the concentration is 2 times larger.
2. We need 2^m = 4, so m = 2.
3. The reaction is second order in A.

**Example 2: Using a rate law.** The rate law is rate = k [A]^2, with k = 0.50 L/(mol s), and [A] = 0.20 M.
1. Substitute the values: rate = 0.50 x (0.20)^2.
2. (0.20)^2 = 0.04, so rate = 0.50 x 0.04 = 0.020 M/s.

**Example 3: Half-life.** A first-order reaction has k = 0.0693 per minute.
1. Use t(1/2) = 0.693 / k.
2. t(1/2) = 0.693 / 0.0693 = 10 min.
3. After 20 minutes, two half-lives, one quarter of the reactant is left.

## Common mistakes

The most frequent error is to take the orders from the coefficients of the balanced equation. They must come from experimental data. Another is to use the wrong units for k, which should be checked against the overall order. Students also believe that the half-life always depends on the concentration, but for a first-order reaction it is constant. Finally, do not confuse the rate with the rate constant: the rate changes as the reactants are consumed, and the rate constant does not, as long as the temperature is the same.
