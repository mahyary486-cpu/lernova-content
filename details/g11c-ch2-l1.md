# Kinetic Molecular Theory of Gases and Pressure

A car tire, a balloon and a can of spray paint all hold gases, and the way each of them behaves follows from one simple picture: gases are made of particles that are always moving. The kinetic molecular theory turns this picture into a set of clear assumptions, and from them we can explain pressure, temperature and the laws of gases that follow in this chapter. Understanding the model first makes the later equations feel natural, because each one is a direct consequence of it.

## Key ideas

The kinetic molecular theory rests on a few assumptions. The particles of a gas are very small compared with the distances between them, so most of the volume of a gas is empty space. The particles move randomly in straight lines, and they collide with each other and with the walls of the container. These collisions are elastic, which means that no kinetic energy is lost. In an ideal gas, the particles do not attract or repel each other except during the collisions. The average kinetic energy of the particles is proportional to the absolute temperature, which is the temperature in kelvins. So at the same temperature, all gases have the same average kinetic energy, and heavier particles simply move more slowly.

Pressure is the force of the collisions on the walls, divided by the area. More collisions, or harder collisions, mean higher pressure. This explains why a gas in a smaller container, or at a higher temperature, has a higher pressure. The common units are connected by 1 atm = 101.3 kPa = 760 mmHg. A conversion between them is a simple proportion, so you can use any of the three pairs of values.

Because gases change so much with temperature and pressure, a standard reference is needed. Standard temperature and pressure (STP) means 273 K and 1 atm. Temperatures in gas problems must always be in kelvins, and the conversion is K = C + 273. The kelvin scale starts from absolute zero, so a temperature in kelvins is directly proportional to the energy of the particles, and the Celsius scale is not.

No real gas is perfectly ideal, but many behave almost ideally. The approximation is best at high temperature and low pressure. At high temperature the particles move so fast that attractions between them are unimportant, and at low pressure the particles are so far apart that their own volume is negligible.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Pressure units.** Convert 380 mmHg to atmospheres.
1. The relation is 760 mmHg = 1 atm, so the conversion factor is 1 atm / 760 mmHg.
2. 380 mmHg x 1 atm / 760 mmHg = 0.5 atm.
3. In kilopascals this is 0.5 x 101.3 = 50.7 kPa.

**Example 2: Temperature.** Convert 25 degrees C to kelvins.
1. Add 273 to the Celsius value: K = C + 273.
2. K = 25 + 273 = 298 K.

**Example 3: Kinetic energy.** The temperature of a gas doubles from 300 K to 600 K. What happens to the average kinetic energy?
1. The average kinetic energy is proportional to the temperature in kelvins.
2. The temperature in kelvins doubles, so the average kinetic energy doubles.
3. Note that it would not double if the temperature went from 20 to 40 degrees C, because those are 293 K and 313 K.

## Common mistakes

The most serious mistake in gas problems is to use Celsius temperatures. A temperature of 0 degrees C does not mean zero energy, and it gives wrong results or even division by zero. Another is to mix the pressure units, such as using mmHg in an equation whose constant is written for atm. Finally, some students think that gas particles attract each other strongly. In the ideal model they do not, and that is why a gas fills any container completely.
