# Newton's Second Law

Newton's first law tells us what happens when the net force is zero. The second law tells us what happens when it is not. It connects three quantities, force, mass and acceleration, and it lets us predict exactly how an object will move when we know the forces acting on it. For this reason, the second law is the central tool of mechanics, and it is used in problems from falling objects to rockets.

## Key ideas

The second law says that the acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to its mass. In symbols, a = F_net / m, or equivalently F_net = ma. The acceleration points in the same direction as the net force. Because the law uses the net force, all forces on the object must first be added as vectors. A single large force can produce no acceleration at all if another force balances it.

The units match naturally: 1 newton is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s^2, so 1 N = 1 kg x 1 m/s^2. Two proportions follow immediately from the law. Doubling the net force doubles the acceleration, and doubling the mass halves it. A heavy object is harder to accelerate because its inertia is larger, and this agrees with the first law.

A reliable method solves almost every problem. First, draw a free-body diagram of the object. Second, choose a positive direction. Third, add the forces with their signs to find the net force. Fourth, apply F_net = ma and solve. When several objects are pushed together, treat them as a single system with the total mass to find the common acceleration, and then apply F = ma to one part to find the force between the parts.

Newton's second law often works together with kinematics. Once the acceleration is known, the equations of motion give the velocity and displacement. In reverse, if the change in velocity and the time are known, the acceleration comes from a = (change in velocity) / time, and the law then gives the net force. This two-way link between forces and motion is what makes the law so useful.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Car acceleration.** An 800 kg car has a net forward force of 2400 N. Find its acceleration.
1. Use a = F_net / m = 2400 / 800.
2. The acceleration is 3.0 m/s^2.
3. Its direction is the same as the direction of the net force, forward.

**Example 2: Pushing against friction.** A 25 kg box is pushed across the floor with a 100 N force, while friction of 30 N opposes the motion. Find the acceleration.
1. The net force is the push minus friction: 100 - 30 = 70 N.
2. Apply the law: a = 70 / 25.
3. The acceleration is 2.8 m/s^2 in the direction of the push.

**Example 3: Force from a change in speed.** A 1000 kg car slows from 20 m/s to rest in 5.0 s. Find the net force on it.
1. The acceleration is a = (0 - 20) / 5.0 = -4.0 m/s^2.
2. The net force is F_net = ma = 1000 x (-4.0) = -4000 N.
3. The size of the force is 4000 N, and the minus sign shows that it points opposite to the motion.

## Common mistakes

A very common error is to use the applied force instead of the net force; you must subtract friction and any other opposing forces first. Students also write a = m / F, putting the mass in the numerator, when the mass belongs in the denominator. Another mistake is to think that the velocity points along the net force. The acceleration does, and the velocity need not: a ball thrown upward moves up while the net force points down. Finally, forgetting to convert grams to kilograms gives an answer that is wrong by a factor of 1000.
