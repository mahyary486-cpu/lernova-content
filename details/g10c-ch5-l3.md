# Percent Composition and Empirical Formulas

The formula of a compound shows its atoms, but an experiment rarely gives a formula directly. What a laboratory usually measures is mass. Burning a sample or decomposing it tells how many grams of each element it contained. Percent composition and empirical formulas are the tools for moving between mass data and the formula of a compound, in both directions, and they are the way new compounds are first identified.

## Key ideas

Percent composition gives the percent by mass of each element in a compound. For one element it is calculated as the mass of that element in one mole of the compound, divided by the molar mass of the compound, multiplied by 100. The percentages of all the elements must add up to 100 percent, which is a useful check on the work. Percent composition does not depend on the size of the sample, because a gram or a kilogram of water has the same proportion of hydrogen.

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms in a compound. The molecular formula is the true number of atoms in a molecule, and it is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. Glucose has the molecular formula C6H12O6, but its empirical formula is CH2O. To get from one to the other, divide the molar mass of the compound by the mass of the empirical formula, and call the result n. Then the molecular formula equals the empirical formula multiplied by n.

Finding an empirical formula from data follows four steps. First, treat the percentages as grams, which is the same as assuming a 100 g sample. Second, convert each mass to moles by dividing by the molar mass of the element. This step is essential, because formulas count atoms, and not grams. Third, divide all the mole values by the smallest one, which makes the smallest value equal to 1. Fourth, if a ratio is not close to a whole number, such as 1.5, multiply every value by a small integer, here 2, until they are all whole. Do not round 1.5 to 2, because that would change the formula.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Percent composition.** Find the percent of hydrogen in H2O, whose molar mass is 18.02 g/mol.
1. The mass of hydrogen in one mole is 2 x 1.008 = 2.016 g.
2. The percent is 2.016 / 18.02 x 100 = 11.2 percent.
3. The oxygen therefore makes up the other 88.8 percent.

**Example 2: Empirical formula.** A compound is 40.0 percent C, 6.7 percent H and 53.3 percent O by mass.
1. In 100 g there are C: 40.0 / 12.01 = 3.33 mol, H: 6.7 / 1.008 = 6.65 mol and O: 53.3 / 16.00 = 3.33 mol.
2. Divide by the smallest, 3.33: C = 1.00, H = 2.00 and O = 1.00.
3. The empirical formula is CH2O.

**Example 3: Molecular formula.** The empirical formula is CH2 and the molar mass of the compound is 56.1 g/mol.
1. The empirical formula mass is 12.01 + 2 x 1.008 = 14.03 g/mol.
2. The factor is n = 56.1 / 14.03 = 4.0, which is 4.
3. The molecular formula is C4H8.

## Common mistakes

The most frequent error is to use the percentages as if they were moles, without dividing by the atomic masses. Another is rounding too early, or rounding a ratio such as 1.5 to 2 instead of multiplying every value by 2. Students also report the empirical formula when the question asks for the molecular formula, and the molar mass in the question is the signal that the second step is needed. Finally, for a hydrate, remember that the water is part of the total mass, so it must be included in the calculation of the percent.
