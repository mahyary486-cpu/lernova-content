# Entropy, Gibbs Free Energy and Spontaneity

Ice melts above 0 degrees C but not below it, and iron rusts by itself without any help. Why do some processes happen on their own, while others never do? Energy alone is not the answer, because some spontaneous processes absorb energy, and melting ice is one of them. The missing idea is entropy, a measure of disorder, and the two ideas are combined in the Gibbs free energy, which predicts the direction of change at a given temperature.

## Key ideas

A spontaneous process is one that happens without continuous outside help. It does not mean fast. A diamond turns into graphite spontaneously, but so slowly that it never happens in a lifetime. Speed is a question of kinetics, and spontaneity a question of thermodynamics.

Entropy, S, is a measure of the disorder of a system, or the number of ways in which its energy and particles can be arranged. Gases have more entropy than liquids, and liquids more than solids, because the particles in a gas can be arranged in many more ways. The entropy rises when a solid dissolves, when a substance is heated, and when a reaction produces more moles of gas. The second law of thermodynamics states that in any spontaneous process the entropy of the universe increases.

The Gibbs free energy combines the two effects of energy and disorder in one quantity: delta G = delta H - T delta S. Here T must be in kelvins, and the units of delta H and delta S must agree, normally kJ and kJ/K. If delta G is negative, the process is spontaneous. If delta G is zero, the system is at equilibrium. If delta G is positive, the process is not spontaneous in the forward direction, but its reverse is.

The signs of delta H and delta S give four cases. If delta H is negative and delta S positive, delta G is always negative, and the process is spontaneous at all temperatures. If delta H is positive and delta S negative, it is never spontaneous. In the two other cases, temperature decides. A process with positive delta H and positive delta S, such as melting, is spontaneous only at high temperature, and a process with negative delta H and negative delta S, such as freezing, is spontaneous only at low temperature. The dividing temperature is where delta G = 0, so T = delta H / delta S.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Gibbs free energy.** A reaction has delta H = -100 kJ and delta S = -0.2 kJ/K at 300 K.
1. delta G = delta H - T delta S = -100 - 300 x (-0.2).
2. delta G = -100 + 60 = -40 kJ.
3. The value is negative, so the reaction is spontaneous at this temperature.

**Example 2: The crossover temperature.** A process has delta H = +40 kJ and delta S = +0.1 kJ/K. When is it spontaneous?
1. At the dividing point delta G = 0, so T = delta H / delta S = 40 / 0.1 = 400 K.
2. Because delta S is positive, higher temperatures make delta G more negative, so the process is spontaneous above 400 K.

**Example 3: Predicting the entropy change.** Does the entropy increase in 2 H2O(l) -> 2 H2(g) + O2(g)?
1. The reactants contain no gas, and the products contain 3 mol of gas.
2. More gas means more disorder, so the entropy increases and delta S is positive.

## Common mistakes

The most frequent mistake is to use Celsius for the temperature in delta G. Another is to confuse spontaneous with fast, but a spontaneous reaction can be extremely slow. Students also mix joules and kilojoules, because delta H is usually given in kJ and delta S in J/K, so one of them must be converted before the two are combined. Finally, do not conclude that a process with positive delta H can never happen. If delta S is positive and the temperature is high enough, it can be spontaneous.
