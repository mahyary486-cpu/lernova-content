# Work Done by a Force

In physics, work is done only when a force moves an object. Pushing on a wall that does not move does no work, however tired you feel. Work measures the energy transferred to or from an object by a force.

## Key ideas
- For a constant force: **W = F d cos(theta)**, where theta is the angle between the force and the displacement.
- The SI unit is the joule: 1 J = 1 N m.
- Work is a scalar. It is positive if the force helps the motion (theta less than 90 degrees), zero if the force is perpendicular (theta = 90 degrees), and negative if it opposes the motion (theta greater than 90 degrees).
- Net work is the sum of the work done by each force, or the net force times the displacement.
- The area under a force versus displacement graph equals the work done, even if the force varies.

## Worked examples
**Example 1: Force at an angle.** A suitcase is pulled 15 m by a 40 N strap that makes 30 degrees with the floor.
1. W = F d cos(theta).
2. W = 40 N x 15 m x cos 30 = 40 x 15 x 0.866.
3. W = 520 J (about 519.6 J).

**Example 2: Negative work.** A 12 N friction force acts on a box that slides 5 m.
1. Friction is opposite to the displacement, so theta = 180 degrees and cos 180 = -1.
2. W = 12 x 5 x (-1) = -60 J.

**Example 3: Lifting at constant speed.** A student lifts a 3 kg bag 2 m at constant speed. Use g = 9.8 m/s^2.
1. The lifting force equals the weight: F = 3 x 9.8 = 29.4 N.
2. Work by the student: 29.4 x 2 = 58.8 J.
3. Work by gravity: -58.8 J, so the net work is 0 J and the speed does not change.

## Common mistakes
- Forgetting the cosine and using W = F d for every force.
- Using the angle with the vertical instead of the angle between force and displacement.
- Saying that a force which does no work must be zero. A perpendicular force can be large and still do no work.
- Forgetting that friction usually does negative work.
