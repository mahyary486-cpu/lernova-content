# Work Done by a Force

In everyday language, work means any effort, mental or physical. In physics it has a precise and narrower meaning: work is done only when a force moves an object. If you push on a wall with all your strength and the wall does not move, you may become tired, but you do no work on the wall. Work measures the energy that a force transfers to or from an object, and it is the first step toward the idea of energy conservation.

## Key ideas

For a constant force acting on an object that moves a distance d, the work is W = F d cos(theta), where theta is the angle between the force and the displacement. Only the part of the force along the motion matters, and cos(theta) picks out exactly that part. The SI unit of work is the joule (J), where 1 J = 1 N m, the work done by a force of 1 N over a distance of 1 m.

Work is a scalar, but it has a sign. When the force has a component along the motion (theta less than 90 degrees), the work is positive: the force adds energy to the object. When the force is perpendicular to the motion (theta equal to 90 degrees), the work is zero, because cos(90) = 0. This is why the normal force on a sliding box and the tension of a string that swings a stone in a circle do no work. When the force has a component against the motion (theta greater than 90 degrees), the work is negative and the force takes energy away. Friction is the usual example, with theta = 180 degrees.

If several forces act, the net work is the sum of the work done by each of them, which equals the net force times the displacement when the forces are constant. This is the quantity that will change the speed of the object, as the next lesson shows. For forces that vary, there is a graphical method: the area under a graph of force against displacement equals the work done, even when the force changes along the way.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: A force at an angle.** A suitcase is pulled 15 m along the floor by a 40 N strap that makes 30 degrees with the floor. Find the work done by the strap.
1. Use W = F d cos(theta).
2. Substitute: W = 40 x 15 x cos(30) = 40 x 15 x 0.866.
3. The work is W = 520 J (more precisely 519.6 J).

**Example 2: Negative work.** A box slides 5.0 m along the floor while a 12 N friction force acts on it. Find the work done by friction.
1. Friction points opposite to the displacement, so theta = 180 degrees and cos(180) = -1.
2. W = 12 x 5.0 x (-1) = -60 J.
3. The negative sign shows that friction removes 60 J of energy from the box.

**Example 3: Lifting at constant speed.** A student lifts a 3.0 kg bag straight up through 2.0 m at constant speed. Use g = 9.8 m/s^2. Find the work done by the student and by gravity.
1. Constant speed means the lifting force equals the weight: F = 3.0 x 9.8 = 29.4 N.
2. The work by the student is 29.4 x 2.0 = 58.8 J.
3. The work by gravity is -58.8 J, because the weight points opposite to the motion. The net work is 0 J, which agrees with the speed staying the same.

## Common mistakes

A frequent mistake is to forget the cosine and use W = F d for every force, even when the force does not point along the motion. Another is to use the angle with the vertical, or the angle with a slope, instead of the angle between the force and the displacement. Students also believe that a large force which does no work must be zero, when a perpendicular force can be large and still do no work. Finally, remember that friction usually does negative work, because it opposes the motion and removes energy from the object.
