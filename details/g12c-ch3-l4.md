# Isomerism and Organic Reactions

Two molecules can have the same formula, such as C4H10, and be completely different substances. They are called isomers. Isomerism is one of the main reasons for the huge number of organic compounds, and it matters in practice, because isomers can differ in boiling point, smell and even effect on the body. Alongside isomerism, this lesson reviews the main types of organic reaction, so that you can describe what happens when a compound with a certain functional group reacts.

## Key ideas

Isomers have the same molecular formula but different structures. In structural isomers, the atoms are joined in a different order. There are three kinds. Chain isomers differ in the branching of the carbon skeleton, such as butane and 2-methylpropane. Position isomers have the same group at a different position, such as propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol. Functional-group isomers have different groups, such as ethanol and dimethyl ether, which both have the formula C2H6O.

Stereoisomers have the same order of atoms but a different arrangement in space. Cis-trans isomers arise from restricted rotation around a double bond. Optical isomers arise when a carbon atom is bonded to four different groups, which is called a chiral center. The molecule and its mirror image then cannot be placed on top of each other, like left and right hands. The two forms are almost identical in physical properties, but they rotate polarized light in opposite directions, and they can act differently in the body.

The main types of reaction are as follows. In substitution, one atom or group replaces another, as when a halogen replaces a hydrogen in an alkane. In addition, atoms are added across a double bond. In elimination, a small molecule, often water, is removed and a double bond forms. An alcohol heated with concentrated sulfuric acid loses water and gives an alkene. Oxidation, as we saw in the previous lesson, converts alcohols to aldehydes, ketones or acids. In hydrolysis, a compound is split by reaction with water, as when an ester is split into an acid and an alcohol.

Saponification is the hydrolysis of a fat with a strong base such as NaOH. It gives glycerol and the sodium salts of the fatty acids, which are soap. This is the oldest industrial organic reaction, and the soap is made because the long chain gives a nonpolar end that attaches to grease, and the ionic end dissolves in water.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: How many structural isomers does C4H10 have?**
1. One isomer is the straight chain, CH3-CH2-CH2-CH3, which is butane.
2. The other has a branch: CH3-CH(CH3)-CH3, which is 2-methylpropane.
3. No other arrangement of four carbons is possible, so there are 2 isomers.

**Example 2: Is CH3-CHBr-CH2CH3 chiral?**
1. Look at the second carbon, the one carrying the bromine.
2. It is attached to four groups: H, Br, CH3 and CH2CH3.
3. All four are different, so the carbon is a chiral center, and the molecule is chiral.

**Example 3: What happens when ethanol is heated with concentrated H2SO4?**
1. The acid removes an -OH group from one carbon and an H from the neighboring carbon.
2. These leave as a molecule of water, and a double bond forms between the two carbons.
3. The equation is C2H5OH -> C2H4 + H2O, and the product is ethene.

## Common mistakes

Students sometimes think that isomers have different molecular formulas, but the formula is the same, and only the structure is different. Another error is to miss a chiral center, so that a carbon with four different groups is not recognized. The third mistake is to mix up hydrolysis and condensation. Condensation joins two molecules and releases water, and hydrolysis uses water to split one molecule.
