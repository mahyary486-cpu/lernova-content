# Bond Energies and Energy Changes

Every chemical reaction breaks some bonds and forms others. Breaking bonds costs energy, because the atoms are held together by attraction, and forming bonds gives energy back. The balance between the two decides whether a reaction releases heat or absorbs it. This gives a way to estimate the enthalpy change of a reaction without any experiment, using only a table of bond energies and the structures of the molecules.

## Key ideas

Breaking a bond absorbs energy, and forming a bond releases energy. The bond energy is the energy needed to break one mole of a particular bond in the gas phase, and it is a positive number. A higher bond energy means a stronger bond. For example, H-H is 436 kJ/mol, Cl-Cl is 243 kJ/mol and H-Cl is 431 kJ/mol. Double and triple bonds between the same atoms have higher energies than single bonds, so a carbon-carbon triple bond is stronger than a double bond.

The enthalpy change of a reaction is estimated by delta H = sum of the energies of the bonds broken - sum of the energies of the bonds formed. If more energy is released in forming the new bonds than is absorbed in breaking the old ones, the result is negative and the reaction is exothermic. If the opposite is true, the reaction is endothermic. The order of the subtraction is the main thing to remember: broken minus formed. A good way to remember it is that the first step in any reaction is to break the bonds, and the energy spent on it comes with a positive sign.

There are two cautions. First, all the bonds must be counted, one by one, which means that you should draw the structures of the molecules. In water, each molecule has two O-H bonds, so 2 H2O has four O-H bonds. Second, tabulated bond energies are averages taken over many compounds, because the energy of a bond depends slightly on its surroundings. The result of the calculation is therefore an estimate, and it can differ from the measured value by several percent. It is also only valid for gases, since energy is also needed to change the state of a substance.

This method also explains why some molecules are more reactive than others. Reactions that replace weak bonds with strong bonds release a lot of energy, and this is why fuels burn so readily and why explosives contain weak bonds.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Formation of HCl.** Find delta H for H2 + Cl2 -> 2 HCl, with H-H = 436, Cl-Cl = 243 and H-Cl = 431 kJ/mol.
1. The bonds broken are one H-H and one Cl-Cl: 436 + 243 = 679 kJ.
2. The bonds formed are two H-Cl: 2 x 431 = 862 kJ.
3. delta H = 679 - 862 = -183 kJ, so the reaction is exothermic.

**Example 2: Formation of water.** Find delta H for 2 H2 + O2 -> 2 H2O, with H-H = 436, O=O = 498 and O-H = 463 kJ/mol.
1. The bonds broken are two H-H and one O=O: 2 x 436 + 498 = 1370 kJ.
2. The bonds formed are four O-H: 4 x 463 = 1852 kJ.
3. delta H = 1370 - 1852 = -482 kJ.

**Example 3: Deciding the sign.** A reaction needs 1000 kJ to break its bonds and releases 1200 kJ when new bonds form.
1. delta H = 1000 - 1200 = -200 kJ.
2. The sign is negative, so the reaction is exothermic.

## Common mistakes

The most common error is to reverse the subtraction, writing formed minus broken, which gives the opposite sign. Another is to forget some of the bonds, especially when a molecule has several identical ones, or to ignore the coefficients of the equation. Students also treat bond energies as exact numbers, although they are averages, and the answer is only an estimate. Finally, remember that breaking bonds always costs energy, so it cannot be an exothermic step.
