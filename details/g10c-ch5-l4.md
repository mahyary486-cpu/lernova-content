# Mole Ratios and Stoichiometry Calculations

Stoichiometry is the use of a balanced chemical equation to calculate how much of one substance reacts with, or forms from, another. It answers the practical questions of chemistry: how much oxygen is needed to burn a fuel, and how much product a reaction can give. The key fact is that the coefficients of the equation give the ratio of moles. They never give the ratio of grams, and that distinction is the center of this lesson.

## Key ideas

A mole ratio compares the coefficients of two substances in a balanced equation. In N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3, the ratio of hydrogen to ammonia is 3 mol H2 to 2 mol NH3. Written as a fraction, it becomes a conversion factor, and it can be turned upside down depending on which substance you want. The equation must be balanced first, because an unbalanced equation gives the wrong ratios.

A mole-to-mole problem needs only one step, which is to multiply the known moles by the mole ratio, with the wanted substance on top so that the units cancel. Most problems in the laboratory are mass-to-mass problems, because we measure masses. These use a path of three steps: grams of A to moles of A using the molar mass of A, then moles of A to moles of B using the mole ratio, and finally moles of B to grams of B using the molar mass of B. The molar masses are used for the gram and mole steps, and the mole ratio is used only for the step from A to B.

The mass of the products is not equal to the mass of each reactant taken one at a time, but the total mass is conserved. When magnesium burns, the solid gains the mass of the oxygen that joins it. The product is heavier than the metal you started with, and the difference is exactly the mass of oxygen that reacted. This is the law of conservation of mass at work, and it is a good way to test whether a result is sensible.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Mole to mole.** For 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O, how many moles of water form from 3.0 mol of O2?
1. The ratio is 2 mol H2O for every 1 mol O2.
2. Moles of H2O = 3.0 mol O2 x (2 / 1) = 6.0 mol.

**Example 2: Mass to mass.** For 2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO, find the mass of MgO that forms from 12.15 g of Mg. The molar masses are Mg 24.31 g/mol and MgO 40.31 g/mol.
1. Moles of Mg = 12.15 / 24.31 = 0.4998 mol.
2. The ratio of MgO to Mg is 2 to 2, so moles of MgO = 0.4998 mol.
3. Mass of MgO = 0.4998 x 40.31 = 20.1 g, which is about 8.0 g more than the magnesium, and this is the oxygen that joined it.

**Example 3: Reactant needed.** For CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O, how many grams of O2 burn 8.0 g of CH4, whose molar mass is 16.04 g/mol?
1. Moles of CH4 = 8.0 / 16.04 = 0.499 mol.
2. Moles of O2 = 0.499 x (2 / 1) = 0.998 mol.
3. Mass of O2 = 0.998 x 32.00 = 31.9 g.

## Common mistakes

The most basic mistake is to use an unbalanced equation, which gives the wrong ratio. Another is to apply the coefficient ratio directly to grams, which is wrong because the coefficients count moles, and one mole of different substances has different masses. Students also put the ratio upside down, and the cure is to write it so that the unit you want is on top. Finally, skipping the molar mass step leaves units that never cancel, which is a clear sign that a step is missing.
