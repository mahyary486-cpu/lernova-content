# Orbits and Kepler's Laws

Planets, moons and artificial satellites all obey the same laws. Understanding them lets us predict where a satellite will be, how long it takes to go around, and how fast it must move to stay in its path. Long before Newton, Johannes Kepler found three rules for planetary motion by studying careful observations. Newton's law of gravitation later explained why those rules hold, and together they give a complete picture of orbits.

## Key ideas

A satellite in orbit is in free fall around the planet. It is falling toward the planet all the time, but it also moves sideways fast enough that the ground curves away beneath it, so it never lands. Gravity is the only force, and it plays the role of the centripetal force. Setting G M m / r^2 equal to m v^2 / r gives the orbital speed v = sqrt(G M / r). This formula contains no mass of the satellite, so any object at that distance has the same orbital speed. It can also be written v = sqrt(g r), with g the field strength at the orbit. A higher orbit has a larger r and therefore a lower speed.

Kepler's first law states that the orbit of each planet is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. Most planetary orbits are nearly circular, so the circular formulas work well. Kepler's second law says that a line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. The consequence is that a planet moves fastest when it is closest to the Sun and slowest when it is farthest. Kepler's third law says that the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the average orbital radius: T^2 is proportional to r^3. For planets around the Sun, if T is in years and r is in astronomical units (AU, the Earth-Sun distance), the law becomes T^2 = r^3.

The practical meaning is that higher orbits take longer. A satellite closer to Earth moves faster and completes each turn sooner, while a distant one moves slowly and takes a long time. A geostationary satellite orbits at a special height where the period is exactly 24 hours, so it stays above the same point on the equator. Satellites do not need engines to stay in orbit; engines are used only to change the orbit or to correct small disturbances.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Orbital speed.** At a distance r = 8.0 x 10^6 m from the center of the Earth, the field strength is g = 4.9 m/s^2. Find the orbital speed.
1. Use v = sqrt(g r) = sqrt(4.9 x 8.0 x 10^6).
2. The value under the root is 3.92 x 10^7.
3. The speed is v = 6.3 x 10^3 m/s, about 6.3 km/s.

**Example 2: Kepler's third law for planets.** A planet has a period of 8.0 years. Find its average distance from the Sun in AU.
1. For the Sun, T^2 = r^3 when T is in years and r in AU.
2. Then r^3 = 8.0^2 = 64.
3. The radius is r = 4.0 AU, since 4.0^3 = 64.

**Example 3: Comparing two orbits.** A satellite at radius r has a period of 90 minutes. Another satellite orbits the same planet at radius 4r. Find its period.
1. Kepler's third law gives T^2 proportional to r^3, so the period changes by a factor of 4^(3/2) = 8.
2. The new period is 8 x 90 = 720 minutes.
3. This is 12 hours, and the second satellite also moves more slowly, at half the speed of the first.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that a satellite needs engines to stay in orbit, but gravity alone keeps it there. Another widespread belief is that higher orbits are faster; they are slower. Many forget to square the period or cube the radius in Kepler's third law, or they use the planet's surface height instead of the distance from its center. Finally, remember that Kepler's law in the form T^2 = r^3 works only with years and AU.
