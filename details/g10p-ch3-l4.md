# Newton's Third Law and Weight

Forces never appear alone. Whenever one object pushes or pulls another, the second object pushes or pulls back. When you push a wall, the wall pushes your hand. When a swimmer pushes water backward, the water pushes the swimmer forward. Newton's third law describes this pairing exactly. This lesson also uses it to clarify the difference between mass and weight, two ideas that are often confused in everyday speech.

## Key ideas

The third law says that if object A exerts a force on object B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. The two forces are called an interaction pair. A crucial point is that the forces of a pair act on different objects. For this reason, they never cancel each other in a free-body diagram, because a free-body diagram shows only the forces on one object. The forces cancel only when they act on the same object.

Equal forces do not mean equal effects. Each object accelerates according to the second law, a = F / m, using its own mass. When a small car and a large truck collide, the force on each is the same, but the small car has the larger acceleration because its mass is smaller. This explains why the lighter object in a collision is usually thrown around more.

Mass and weight are different. Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms, and it does not change with location. Weight is the gravitational force on the object, W = mg, measured in newtons. On Earth g is 9.8 m/s^2, and on the Moon it is about 1.6 m/s^2, so an astronaut has the same mass in both places but a smaller weight on the Moon.

The normal force is the contact force perpendicular to a surface, and it does not always equal the weight. In an elevator that accelerates upward, the second law gives N - mg = ma, so N = m(g + a). If the elevator accelerates downward, N = m(g - a). A bathroom scale reads the normal force, which explains why you feel heavier when an elevator starts upward and lighter when it starts downward.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Weight on two worlds.** An astronaut has a mass of 75 kg. Find the weight on Earth and on the Moon.
1. On Earth: W = mg = 75 x 9.8 = 735 N.
2. On the Moon: W = 75 x 1.6 = 120 N.
3. The mass stays 75 kg in both places, because mass does not depend on location.

**Example 2: Skaters pushing apart.** A 40 kg skater and a 60 kg skater push each other with 120 N on frictionless ice. Find the acceleration of each.
1. By the third law, each skater feels a force of 120 N, in opposite directions.
2. The lighter skater has a = 120 / 40 = 3.0 m/s^2.
3. The heavier skater has a = 120 / 60 = 2.0 m/s^2, a smaller acceleration from the same force.

**Example 3: An elevator scale.** A 60 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator that accelerates upward at 1.5 m/s^2. Find the scale reading, and then the reading for a downward acceleration of 1.5 m/s^2.
1. For upward acceleration, N = m(g + a) = 60 x (9.8 + 1.5) = 60 x 11.3 = 678 N.
2. For downward acceleration, N = m(g - a) = 60 x (9.8 - 1.5) = 60 x 8.3 = 498 N.
3. At rest the reading would be 60 x 9.8 = 588 N, so the person feels heavier in the first case and lighter in the second.

## Common mistakes

Students often claim that action and reaction cancel; they do not, because they act on different objects. Another common belief is that the larger object pushes harder in a collision, but the forces are equal. Many also confuse mass and weight, giving weight in kilograms or saying that mass changes on the Moon. Finally, do not assume that the normal force always equals mg; it changes with an acceleration, with an extra vertical push, and on a slope.
