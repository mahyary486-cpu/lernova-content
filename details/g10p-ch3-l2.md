# Newton's First Law and Inertia

Newton's first law explains what happens when there is no unbalanced force. For many centuries people believed that a force was needed to keep an object moving, because everyday objects stop when we stop pushing them. The truth is more subtle: a force is needed only to change motion. Things stop in daily life because friction and air resistance act on them. If those forces disappeared, a moving object would keep going forever.

## Key ideas

The first law says that an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving at constant velocity in a straight line, unless a net external force acts on it. The words constant velocity include both the speed and the direction, so a moving object neither slows down nor turns without a cause. This law is sometimes called the law of inertia.

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist any change in its motion. It is not a force; it is a property of matter. The measure of inertia is mass: the larger the mass, the harder it is to start the object, stop it or turn it. A loaded truck is harder to stop than an empty one, and a bowling ball is harder to throw than a tennis ball, for exactly this reason.

If the net force on an object is zero, the object is in equilibrium. Equilibrium does not mean that the object is at rest; it means that the acceleration is zero, so the object is either at rest or moves at constant velocity. Balanced forces cancel when added as vectors, while unbalanced forces leave a nonzero net force that changes the velocity. For equilibrium on a vertical line, the upward forces equal the downward forces. A hanging object at rest has a tension equal to its weight, T = mg, with g = 9.8 m/s^2.

Everyday experiences show inertia clearly. Passengers lurch forward when a bus brakes, because their bodies tend to keep moving at the old velocity while the bus slows down. A tablecloth can be yanked quickly from under dishes, because the dishes tend to stay at rest. In each case, the object simply keeps what it already had.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: A hanging lamp.** A 5.0 kg lamp hangs at rest from a cord. Find the tension.
1. The lamp is at rest, so the net force is zero and the tension must equal the weight.
2. The weight is mg = 5.0 x 9.8 = 49 N.
3. The tension in the cord is 49 N, directed upward.

**Example 2: A car at constant velocity.** A car drives at a steady 25 m/s. Air drag and rolling resistance together total 700 N backward. Find the forward force of the road.
1. Constant velocity means a = 0, so the net force is zero.
2. The forward force must cancel the backward 700 N, so it is 700 N.
3. If the forward force were larger than 700 N, the car would speed up instead.

**Example 3: A crate on a rope.** A 30 kg crate is lifted at a constant 0.5 m/s by a rope. Find the tension.
1. Constant velocity means zero net force, so the tension equals the weight.
2. T = mg = 30 x 9.8 = 294 N.
3. The speed of 0.5 m/s does not appear in the answer, because only a change in velocity requires a net force.

## Common mistakes

Many students believe that a moving object needs a net force to keep moving; it needs a net force only to change its velocity. Another error is to call inertia a force, when it is a property measured by mass. A third mistake is to think that "no motion" is the only kind of equilibrium, but constant velocity is equilibrium too. Finally, remember that two forces must be equal and opposite and act on the same object to cancel; a single force alone can never give zero net force.
