# Pascal's Principle

A garage jack lets one person lift a car that weighs more than a ton. The secret is a fluid: a small force pushing on a small piston is changed into a large force on a large piston. This is possible because of Pascal's principle, which describes how pressure moves through a fluid in a closed container. Hydraulic brakes, car lifts, excavators and dentist chairs all use it.

## Key ideas

Pascal's principle states that a pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of the fluid and to the walls of the container. If you push on one piston and raise the pressure there by some amount, the pressure rises by the same amount at every other point, including under a second piston. The principle works well with liquids because they are almost incompressible: their volume hardly changes under pressure, so the effect is passed on at once.

Applying the principle to two pistons gives the key equation. The pressure under the small piston is F1 / A1, and the pressure under the large piston is F2 / A2. Since the two pressures are equal, F1 / A1 = F2 / A2, or F2 = F1 (A2 / A1). The output force is the input force multiplied by the ratio of the areas. A large piston with 25 times the area gives a force that is 25 times greater.

This does not mean that something is gained for free. The fluid has a fixed volume, so when the small piston moves down by a distance d1, the volume A1 d1 moves to the large piston, which rises by d2 with A2 d2 = A1 d1. Hence d1 / d2 = A2 / A1: the distances are in the inverse ratio of the areas. The large piston moves a much smaller distance than the small one. The work done on the input piston, F1 d1, equals the work done by the output piston, F2 d2, in an ideal machine. The jack multiplies force at the cost of distance, and energy is not created.

The area ratio is often given through radii or diameters. Since the area of a circular piston is pi r^2, the ratio of areas is the square of the ratio of the radii. This is a place where many mistakes happen.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: A hydraulic press.** The pistons of a press have areas 0.010 m^2 and 0.50 m^2. A force of 100 N acts on the small piston. Find the force on the large piston.
1. Use F2 = F1 x A2 / A1 = 100 x 0.50 / 0.010.
2. The area ratio is 50.
3. The output force is F2 = 5000 N.

**Example 2: Distances.** The large piston has 20 times the area of the small piston. The small piston moves down by 10 cm. How far does the large piston move?
1. The volume moved is the same on both sides, so A1 d1 = A2 d2.
2. Therefore d2 = d1 x A1 / A2 = 10 / 20.
3. The large piston rises only 0.50 cm, which is the price paid for the 20 times larger force.

**Example 3: A jack.** The pistons of a jack have radii 2.0 cm and 10 cm. What force on the small piston lifts a load of 2500 N?
1. The area ratio is the square of the radius ratio: (10 / 2.0)^2 = 5^2 = 25.
2. The input force is F1 = F2 / 25 = 2500 / 25.
3. A force of 100 N is enough.

## Common mistakes

Students often use the ratio of the radii instead of the ratio of the areas, which gives a force multiplication of 5 instead of 25 in the jack. Another mistake is to think that a hydraulic machine creates energy; the work output equals the work input at best. A third mistake is to forget that the distance moved is inversely proportional to the area, so the big piston moves less. Finally, remember that the principle applies to a closed fluid; if there is a leak or an air bubble, the pressure is not transmitted well.
