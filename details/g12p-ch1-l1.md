# Electric Charge and Coulomb's Law

A balloon rubbed on hair sticks to a wall, and a plastic comb can lift tiny pieces of paper. These effects come from electric forces between charges. Electric force is one of the fundamental forces of nature, and it holds atoms together, gives materials their strength and makes all electrical devices work. Coulomb's law tells exactly how strong this force is, and it is the starting point of the whole subject of electricity.

## Key ideas

There are two kinds of electric charge, called positive and negative. The rule of interaction is simple: like charges repel, and unlike charges attract. Matter is normally neutral because it contains equal amounts of positive and negative charge. Rubbing two materials transfers electrons from one to the other, leaving one positive and the other negative. Only electrons move in this way; the positive protons stay in the nuclei.

Charge has two important properties. It is quantized: every charge is a whole-number multiple of the elementary charge e = 1.60 x 10^-19 C, the charge of a proton, with the electron having the same size but a negative sign. It is also conserved: the total charge of an isolated system never changes. The SI unit of charge is the coulomb (C), a very large amount. Typical static charges are microcoulombs (10^-6 C) or nanocoulombs (10^-9 C).

Coulomb's law gives the size of the force between two small charged objects, which we treat as point charges: F = k q1 q2 / r^2, where r is the distance between them and k = 9 x 10^9 N m^2/C^2. The force acts along the straight line joining the two charges, and by Newton's third law it is equal and opposite on the two objects. Like gravity, it is an inverse-square law: doubling the distance cuts the force to one quarter. Unlike gravity, it can be attractive or repulsive. When using the formula, put in the magnitudes of the charges and decide the direction from the rule about like and unlike charges.

When more than two charges are present, use the principle of superposition: the net force on a charge is the vector sum of the forces from each of the other charges, taken one at a time. Finally, materials differ in how they treat charge. Conductors, such as metals, let charge move freely, while insulators, such as rubber and plastic, hold charge in place.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Force between charges.** Two charges of 2.0 x 10^-6 C and 3.0 x 10^-6 C are 0.30 m apart. Find the force.
1. Use F = k q1 q2 / r^2 = 9 x 10^9 x 2.0 x 10^-6 x 3.0 x 10^-6 / 0.30^2.
2. The numerator is 9 x 10^9 x 6.0 x 10^-12 = 0.054, and the denominator is 0.090.
3. The force is F = 0.60 N, repulsive because both charges are positive.

**Example 2: Changing the distance.** The force between two charges is 8.0 N. The distance between them is doubled. Find the new force.
1. The force is proportional to 1 / r^2, so doubling r divides the force by 2^2 = 4.
2. The new force is F = 8.0 / 4 = 2.0 N.

**Example 3: Counting electrons.** A body has a charge of -3.2 x 10^-9 C. How many extra electrons does it carry?
1. The number is n = Q / e = 3.2 x 10^-9 / 1.6 x 10^-19.
2. n = 2.0 x 10^10 extra electrons, a large number even for such a small charge.

## Common mistakes

Students often forget to square the distance, which makes the force wrong by a large factor. Another frequent error is leaving the charges in microcoulombs; they must be converted to coulombs, where 1 microcoulomb is 10^-6 C. Many add forces from several charges as plain numbers, but forces are vectors and must be added with their directions. Finally, do not use the signs of the charges inside the formula and then forget that the sign of the result only tells whether the force is attractive or repulsive, not a direction on its own.
