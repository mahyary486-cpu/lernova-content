# Addition Polymers

Plastic bags, water pipes, food wrap and non-stick pans are all made of addition polymers, very long molecules built from small alkene molecules. A polymer is a molecule made of many repeating units, and the small molecules that join to make it are called monomers. The word comes from Greek and means many parts. This lesson shows how a double bond makes it possible to link thousands of monomers into one chain, and why the structure of the chain decides the properties of the plastic.

## Key ideas

Monomers that contain a C=C double bond can undergo addition polymerization. In this process, one bond of each double bond opens, and the monomers join end to end, so that the chain is made of single bonds. No atoms are lost, so the polymer has the same composition as the monomer, and the repeat unit has the same atoms as the monomer. This is the difference from condensation polymerization, which we meet in the next lesson.

The common examples follow one pattern. Ethene, CH2=CH2, gives poly(ethene), whose repeat unit is -(CH2-CH2)n-, where n is a very large number. Chloroethene, CH2=CHCl, gives poly(chloroethene), known as PVC, which is used for pipes and window frames. Tetrafluoroethene, CF2=CF2, gives PTFE, which is the slippery coating on non-stick pans. Propene gives poly(propene), which is used in ropes and containers. To draw the repeat unit, open the double bond and show the two free bonds at the ends, and to find the monomer from a polymer, close the bond of the repeat unit again.

The properties of a plastic depend on the structure of its chains. Polymers whose chains are branched cannot pack tightly, so they have a low density and are flexible. They are used in plastic bags. Polymers with linear chains pack closely, so they are denser, stronger and have a higher melting point, as in rigid containers. Plastics are also classified by what happens when they are heated. A thermoplastic softens on heating and can be reshaped many times, because the chains are held by weak forces between molecules. A thermosetting plastic has cross-links of strong covalent bonds between chains, so it does not soften and it decomposes when overheated.

The carbon-carbon bonds in these polymers are strong and nonpolar, so microorganisms cannot break them down. Most addition polymers are therefore not biodegradable, which causes a waste problem. Recycling, incineration with energy recovery and the design of plastics that degrade are three responses.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Draw the repeat unit of poly(propene).**
1. Propene is CH2=CH-CH3, with a methyl group on one carbon of the double bond.
2. Open the double bond, and the two carbons each have a free bond.
3. The repeat unit is -(CH2-CH(CH3))n-, and the methyl group stays as a side group.

**Example 2: What monomer gives PVC?**
1. PVC is poly(chloroethene), so the repeat unit is -(CH2-CHCl)n-.
2. Put the double bond back between the two carbons of the repeat unit.
3. The monomer is chloroethene, CH2=CHCl.

**Example 3: What is the molar mass of a chain of 1000 ethene units? Use 28 g/mol for C2H4.**
1. No atoms are lost, so the mass of the chain is the sum of the masses of the monomers.
2. Mass = 1000 x 28 = 28,000.
3. The molar mass of the chain is 28,000 g/mol.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that atoms are lost in addition polymerization, but the polymer has the same atoms as the monomers, and nothing is released. Another error is to confuse a thermoplastic with a thermosetting plastic. The first can be melted again and the second cannot. The third mistake is to forget that the double bond is opened, so that the repeat unit is drawn with a double bond still present.
