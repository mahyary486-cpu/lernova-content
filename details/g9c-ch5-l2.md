# The pH Scale

A small change in pH is a big change in acidity. This is why a solution with pH 3 is much more acidic than one with pH 5, and not just a little more. The pH scale is a convenient way of expressing the concentration of hydrogen ions with small, easy numbers, and it is used in everything from swimming pools and soil testing to medicine and food production.

## Key ideas

The pH scale usually runs from 0 to 14. A solution with a pH below 7 is acidic, a pH of exactly 7 is neutral, and a pH above 7 is basic. The lower the pH, the more acidic the solution, and the higher the pH, the more basic it is. Typical values are about 2 for lemon juice, 7 for pure water, 9 for baking soda solution and 11 for household ammonia.

The scale is defined from the concentration of hydrogen ions, written [H+] and measured in mol/L: pH = -log [H+]. When the concentration is written as a power of ten, the pH is simply the exponent without its minus sign. If [H+] = 1 x 10^-5 mol/L, then pH = 5. Because the scale is logarithmic, each step of one pH unit is a change by a factor of 10 in the hydrogen ion concentration. A solution with pH 3 has 10 times more H+ than a solution with pH 4, and 100 times more than one with pH 5. A difference of two pH units means a factor of 100, and three units means 1000.

In pure water at 25 degrees C, a very small number of water molecules split into ions, so [H+] = [OH-] = 1 x 10^-7 mol/L. That is why neutral water has pH 7. In acidic solutions the hydrogen ions outnumber the hydroxide ions, and in basic solutions the hydroxide ions are more numerous. We can also describe the hydroxide concentration with the pOH, defined as pOH = -log [OH-]. At 25 degrees C, the two are linked by pH + pOH = 14, so if one is known, the other follows by subtraction.

It is important not to treat the pH scale as an ordinary linear scale. The numbers look close together, but they hide very large differences in concentration, and this is why a drop of pH in a lake from 7 to 5 is a serious change, a hundredfold increase in hydrogen ions, and can harm fish and plants.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Comparing.** How much more acidic is a solution of pH 2 than one of pH 5?
1. The difference is 5 - 2 = 3 pH units.
2. Each unit is a factor of 10, so the factor is 10 x 10 x 10 = 1000. The pH 2 solution has 1000 times more hydrogen ions.

**Example 2: Finding pH.** A solution has [H+] = 1 x 10^-5 mol/L. What is its pH, and is it acidic?
1. pH = -log (1 x 10^-5) = 5.
2. The pH is below 7, so the solution is acidic.

**Example 3: pH from pOH.** A solution has [OH-] = 1 x 10^-3 mol/L. Find the pH.
1. pOH = -log (1 x 10^-3) = 3.
2. pH = 14 - pOH = 14 - 3 = 11, so the solution is basic.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that pH 4 is twice as acidic as pH 8, when it is a difference of 4 units, or 10 000 times. Another common mistake is to forget that a lower pH means a more acidic solution. Finally, remember that the pH scale is not linear: each unit is a factor of 10.
