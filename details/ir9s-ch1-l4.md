# The periodic table

This topic explains how the elements are arranged in the periodic table and what the position of an element tells us.

## Key ideas

The periodic table arranges elements in order of increasing atomic number. Dmitri Mendeleev made an early version in 1869, ordered by atomic mass, and he left gaps for elements that were not yet discovered. He predicted the properties of these missing elements, and the later discoveries matched his predictions well.

The table has horizontal rows called periods and vertical columns called groups.

- Periods: There are 7 periods. The period number equals the number of electron shells that contain electrons in the atom.
- Groups: There are 18 groups. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of valence electrons.
- For main groups: group 1 has 1 valence electron, group 2 has 2, and groups 13 to 18 have 3 to 8 (so the number of valence electrons is the group number minus 10). Helium in group 18 has 2.

Some important groups:
- Group 1, the alkali metals (lithium, sodium, potassium): soft, very reactive, and reactivity increases going down the group.
- Group 2, the alkaline earth metals (magnesium, calcium): less reactive than group 1.
- Group 17, the halogens (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine): reactive nonmetals with 7 valence electrons.
- Group 18, the noble gases (helium, neon, argon): very unreactive because their outer shell is full.

Metals are on the left and in the middle of the table. Nonmetals are on the right. A zigzag line separates them, and metalloids lie along this line. Between group 2 and group 13 are the transition metals, such as iron, copper and zinc.

Trends: Going down a group, atoms get larger because they have more shells. Metals become more reactive going down a group, while halogens become less reactive going down.

## Examples

Example 1: Magnesium (Z = 12).
- Electron arrangement: 2,8,2.
- Three shells are used, so it is in period 3.
- It has 2 valence electrons, so it is in group 2.
- It is a metal and is similar to calcium.

Example 2: Sulfur (Z = 16).
- Arrangement: 2,8,6, so period 3.
- Six valence electrons: group 16 (16 - 10 = 6).
- It is on the right side, so it is a nonmetal.

Example 3: Potassium (Z = 19).
- Arrangement: 2,8,8,1.
- Four shells: period 4. One valence electron: group 1.
- It is in the same group as sodium, so it reacts with water to give hydrogen and a hydroxide, and it is even more reactive than sodium.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing periods and groups. Periods are rows; groups are columns.
- Thinking the group number always equals the number of valence electrons. This works directly only for groups 1 and 2; for groups 13 to 18 subtract 10.
- Saying that reactivity increases going down every group. It increases down group 1 but decreases down group 17.
- Believing the table is ordered by mass today. It is ordered by atomic number.

## Remember

- Period number = number of shells; group tells the valence electrons.
- Elements in a group have similar properties.
- Metals are on the left, nonmetals on the right, noble gases at the far right.
