# Acids and Bases in Everyday Life

From the sour taste of a lemon to the cleaning of a kitchen, acids and bases are part of daily life. They help to digest our food, to keep our homes clean and to grow our crops, and they can also damage the environment when released in the wrong amounts. This lesson gathers the most important applications and also the safety rules for handling these chemicals.

## Key ideas

Our own bodies depend on acids and bases. The stomach makes hydrochloric acid, a strong acid, which helps to digest food and kills bacteria. When there is too much acid, we feel indigestion or heartburn. Antacid tablets contain bases, such as magnesium hydroxide or calcium carbonate, which neutralize part of the acid and relieve the discomfort. This is a direct use of neutralization.

Many foods contain weak acids. Citric acid is found in oranges and lemons, acetic acid in vinegar, and lactic acid in yogurt and sour milk. These acids give the sour taste and also help to preserve food, because most bacteria cannot grow well in an acidic environment. Bases are found in many cleaning products. Soaps, detergents, ammonia cleaners and drain cleaners are basic, because bases dissolve fats and grease and break them into pieces that wash away. Household bleach is also strongly basic. Some cleaners, such as those for toilets and rust, are acids, which dissolve mineral deposits, so it is wrong to think that all cleaners are bases.

In agriculture, plants grow best in soil of a certain pH. If the soil is too acidic, farmers add lime, which is calcium hydroxide or calcium carbonate, to neutralize it. In industry, acids and bases are among the most important chemicals. Sulfuric acid is made in larger amounts than any other chemical, and it is used for fertilizers, batteries and many other products.

Not every effect is useful. Acid rain forms when sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, released by burning coal, oil and gasoline, dissolve in rain water and form acids. Normal rain is already slightly acidic, with a pH of about 5.6, because carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in it. Acid rain is more acidic, with pH values that can be below 4.5. It damages forests, harms fish in lakes and slowly dissolves marble statues and buildings. Reducing the burning of fossil fuels and cleaning the exhaust gases are the main solutions.

Safety is essential. Never taste or smell unknown chemicals directly, wear goggles and gloves, and always add acid slowly to water, never water to acid. Adding water to a concentrated acid can release so much heat that the mixture boils and splashes.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Indigestion.** Why does an antacid tablet help with indigestion?
1. The stomach has too much hydrochloric acid, which irritates its lining.
2. The tablet contains a base that neutralizes some of the acid, forming a salt and water, and the pH rises.

**Example 2: Acid rain.** What causes acid rain?
1. Burning fuels releases sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides into the air.
2. These gases dissolve in rain water and make acids, so the rain has a lower pH than normal.

**Example 3: Soil.** A farmer's soil is too acidic for the crops. What can be done?
1. A base is needed to neutralize the extra acid.
2. Lime, calcium hydroxide or calcium carbonate, is spread on the soil, and the soil pH rises toward neutral.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to add water to concentrated acid instead of adding the acid slowly to water. Another is to think that all cleaners are acids or that all of them are bases, since both types are used. Finally, many students think that rain water is neutral, but it is naturally slightly acidic because of dissolved carbon dioxide.
