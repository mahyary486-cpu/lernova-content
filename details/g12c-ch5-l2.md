# Half-Life, Dating and Radiation Safety

The nuclear clock in carbon-14 lets us date objects up to about 50,000 years old. Radioactive decay is random for any single atom, but for a large sample it follows a very regular pattern, and that regularity makes it a reliable timekeeper. The same pattern determines how long a medical isotope stays active, and how long radioactive waste remains dangerous.

## Key ideas

The half-life, T(1/2), is the time needed for half of the nuclei in a sample to decay. It is a constant for each isotope and does not depend on the amount, the temperature or the chemical form. After one half-life, half of the sample is left. After two, a quarter is left, and after three, an eighth. The general formula is N = N0 x (1/2)^(t / T(1/2)), where N0 is the starting amount and t is the elapsed time. The time and the half-life must be in the same unit. The half-life is not the time for all of the sample to decay, since the amount keeps halving and never reaches zero.

The decay constant, lambda, measures the chance of decay per unit time, and it is related to the half-life by lambda = 0.693 / T(1/2). A short half-life means a large lambda and a fast decay. The activity of a sample is the number of decays per second, measured in becquerels, where 1 Bq is 1 decay per second. The absorbed dose, which measures the effect on tissue, is measured in sieverts for the equivalent dose.

Carbon dating uses carbon-14, which has a half-life of 5730 years. It forms in the atmosphere, and living plants and animals take it in, so the proportion of C-14 in a living organism stays constant. After death, no more is taken in, and the C-14 decays. Measuring the remaining fraction gives the age. The method works for about 10 half-lives, since after that too little is left to measure. For older rocks, isotopes with longer half-lives, such as uranium-238, are used.

Radiation is useful but dangerous, because it ionizes atoms and damages cells. The three rules of protection are time, distance and shielding. Reduce the time of exposure, increase the distance from the source, since the intensity falls quickly as one moves away, and place a suitable barrier in between. Radioisotopes are used as tracers in medicine, in radiotherapy to destroy tumors, in dating, and in sterilizing food and medical equipment.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Half-lives.** A 40 g sample has a half-life of 3 days. How much is left after 12 days?
1. The number of half-lives is 12 / 3 = 4.
2. N = 40 x (1/2)^4 = 40 / 16.
3. N = 2.5 g remains.

**Example 2: Carbon dating.** A bone has 25 percent of its original C-14. How old is it?
1. A fraction of 25 percent is 1/4, which equals (1/2)^2, so 2 half-lives have passed.
2. The age is 2 x 5730 years.
3. The bone is 11,460 years old.

**Example 3: The decay constant.** Find lambda for an isotope with a half-life of 6 hours.
1. Use lambda = 0.693 / T(1/2) = 0.693 / 6 hours.
2. lambda = 0.116 per hour, or about 11.6 percent of the nuclei per hour.

## Common mistakes

Students often treat the half-life as the time for the whole sample to decay, when it is the time for half of it. Another error is to use minutes for the time and hours for the half-life, which gives the wrong number of half-lives. The third mistake is to think that the half-life depends on the amount of the sample. It is a property of the isotope, and a large sample and a small sample have the same half-life.
