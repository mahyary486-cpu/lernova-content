# Atomic Number, Mass Number and Isotopes

Carbon atoms all have 6 protons, but some have 6 neutrons and others have 8. They are atoms of the same element, yet they are slightly different in mass. These different versions are called isotopes. To describe an atom exactly, chemists use two numbers, the atomic number and the mass number, and with them they can count the particles inside any atom or ion.

## Key ideas

The atomic number, written Z, is the number of protons in the nucleus. It is the number that defines the element: every atom with 6 protons is carbon, and every atom with 8 protons is oxygen, no matter what else it contains. The elements on the periodic table are listed in order of their atomic number. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so the atomic number also tells us the number of electrons.

The mass number, written A, is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus, which together are called nucleons. Electrons are not counted, because their mass is tiny. The number of neutrons is found by subtraction: neutrons = A - Z. The mass number is always a whole number, and it appears in names such as carbon-14, where 14 is the mass number.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element, which means the same Z, that have different numbers of neutrons and therefore different mass numbers. Carbon-12 has 6 neutrons and carbon-14 has 8. Isotopes of an element have nearly the same chemical behavior, because chemistry depends mostly on electrons, but they can differ in properties that depend on mass, and some isotopes are radioactive. The atomic mass shown on the periodic table is an average of the masses of all the natural isotopes, weighted by how common each one is. Chlorine, for example, is about 75 percent chlorine-35 and 25 percent chlorine-37, so its average mass is 0.75 x 35 + 0.25 x 37 = 35.5. For this reason the table shows decimals and not whole numbers.

An atom can also gain or lose electrons, and then it becomes an ion. The number of protons never changes in this process, because that would change the element. If an atom loses electrons, it has more positive charge than negative, and it becomes a positive ion. If it gains electrons, it becomes a negative ion. The charge of the ion equals the number of protons minus the number of electrons.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Counting neutrons.** An atom has an atomic number of 11 and a mass number of 23. How many protons, electrons and neutrons does the neutral atom have?
1. The atomic number is 11, so there are 11 protons, and in a neutral atom there are 11 electrons.
2. The neutrons are A - Z = 23 - 11 = 12.
3. The atom is sodium-23.

**Example 2: Isotopes.** Carbon-14 has an atomic number of 6. Why is it an isotope of carbon-12?
1. The neutrons in carbon-14 are 14 - 6 = 8.
2. Carbon-12 has 12 - 6 = 6 neutrons. Both have 6 protons but different numbers of neutrons, so they are isotopes of the same element.

**Example 3: An ion.** An ion has 12 protons and 10 electrons. What is its charge?
1. The charge is (+12) + (-10) = +2, written 2+.
2. With 12 protons the element is magnesium, so this is the ion Mg 2+. It was formed by the loss of two electrons.

## Common mistakes

Students often confuse the atomic number with the mass number. The atomic number counts protons only, and the mass number counts protons and neutrons. Another mistake is to think that isotopes are different elements, but they are the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Finally, when an atom becomes an ion, only the number of electrons changes. A change in the number of protons would produce a different element.
