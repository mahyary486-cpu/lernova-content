# Pressure in Fluids

Divers feel the pressure on their ears increase as they go deeper, and dams are built thicker at the bottom than at the top. Both facts have the same cause: in a fluid at rest, the pressure grows with depth. This lesson gives the simple formula for that growth and shows how it connects to atmospheric pressure and to the barometer, the instrument used to measure it.

## Key ideas

A fluid is a liquid or a gas. At any depth, the fluid below pushes up and the fluid above pushes down, and the pressure at that point is caused by the weight of the column of fluid above it. For a fluid of density rho at a depth h, the pressure due to the fluid is P = rho g h. This formula follows directly from P = F / A, because the weight of a column of fluid with base area A is rho x A x h x g, and dividing by A leaves rho g h.

Three features of this formula deserve attention. First, the pressure at a point acts equally in all directions: up, down and sideways. A diver feels the same pressure on every side. Second, the pressure depends only on the depth, the density and g. It does not depend on the shape or the width of the container. A narrow tube and a wide lake have the same pressure at the same depth, which surprises many students. Third, the pressure increases in a regular way: for water, about 98 000 Pa for every 10 m of depth, which is nearly the same as one extra atmosphere.

The pressure given by rho g h is only the part due to the liquid. A real point in a lake also feels the air pressure acting on the surface, which is transmitted through the water. The absolute pressure is therefore P = P_atm + rho g h. This is the quantity that acts on a diver or on the wall of a submarine.

A mercury barometer uses this idea to measure the atmosphere. A tube filled with mercury is turned upside down in a dish of mercury. The mercury falls until the pressure of the column, rho g h, equals the atmospheric pressure pushing on the dish. At sea level the column is about 0.76 m high, which is why pressure is sometimes quoted in millimeters of mercury. Mercury is used because its high density makes the column short enough to handle.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Pressure at depth.** Find the pressure due to water at a depth of 10 m. Use g = 9.8 m/s^2.
1. Use P = rho g h with rho = 1000 kg/m^3.
2. P = 1000 x 9.8 x 10.
3. The pressure is 98 000 Pa.

**Example 2: Absolute pressure.** Find the absolute pressure at the 10 m depth when the atmospheric pressure is 1.01 x 10^5 Pa.
1. Add the atmosphere to the pressure of the water: P = P_atm + rho g h = 101 000 + 98 000.
2. The absolute pressure is 199 000 Pa, or 1.99 x 10^5 Pa.
3. This is almost twice the pressure at the surface.

**Example 3: A mercury barometer.** What height of mercury (density 13 600 kg/m^3) balances an atmospheric pressure of 1.01 x 10^5 Pa?
1. Solve P = rho g h for the height: h = P / (rho g) = 101 000 / (13 600 x 9.8).
2. The denominator is 133 280.
3. The height is h = 0.76 m, or 760 mm.

## Common mistakes

Students often use the total volume of the liquid instead of the depth, but only the vertical depth counts. Another mistake is to forget atmospheric pressure when the problem asks for the absolute pressure. A third is to think that a wider container gives more pressure at the same depth; the width does not matter. Finally, measure the depth from the surface of the liquid and not from the bottom of the container.
