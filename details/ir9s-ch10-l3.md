# Constellations and finding directions

A constellation is a pattern of stars that people have given a name, and the stars can help us to find directions on Earth.

## Key ideas

- Long ago, people joined bright stars with imaginary lines to make shapes, such as animals, heroes and tools. These shapes help us to find our way around the sky.
- The International Astronomical Union has divided the whole sky into 88 official constellations. Examples are Ursa Major (the Great Bear), Cassiopeia, Orion and Scorpius.
- The stars in a constellation look close together, but they are usually at very different distances from Earth. They only look like a group when seen from our place in space.
- The Big Dipper is a group of seven bright stars in Ursa Major. It looks like a pot with a long handle.
- Earth rotates from west to east around its axis once every 24 hours. This makes the Sun, the Moon and the stars seem to move from east to west. They rise in the east and set in the west.
- The north end of Earth's axis points almost directly at Polaris, the North Star. For this reason, Polaris seems almost fixed in the sky, and the other stars seem to circle around it.
- To find Polaris, draw a line through the two stars at the end of the Big Dipper's pot, Dubhe and Merak, and follow it. You will reach Polaris. Cassiopeia is on the other side of Polaris.
- Polaris shows north. If you face it, east is on your right, west on your left and south behind you.
- In the northern hemisphere, the angle of Polaris above the horizon is equal to the latitude of the observer.
- Other methods: a compass shows north using Earth's magnetic field. In the daytime, a shadow stick can be used. Mark the tip of the shadow of a vertical stick twice, about 15 minutes apart. The line between the marks points roughly west to east.

## Examples

Example 1: Finding the altitude of Polaris in Tehran.
- The latitude of Tehran is about 35.7° N.
- The altitude of Polaris equals the latitude.
- So Polaris is about 36° above the northern horizon.
- In Bandar Abbas (latitude about 27° N), Polaris is lower, about 27° above the horizon.

Example 2: How far do the stars seem to turn in 3 hours?
- Earth turns 360° in 24 hours.
- Rate = 360 / 24 = 15° per hour.
- In 3 hours: 15 × 3 = 45°.
- A star near Polaris seems to circle around it by 45° in 3 hours.

Example 3: Finding north on a clear night.
- A traveler sees the Big Dipper low in the sky.
- She finds the two pointer stars at the end of the pot, then follows a line from the lower one through the upper one, and goes about five times the distance between them.
- She finds a medium-bright star there. This is Polaris, and the direction to it is north.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking that Polaris is the brightest star in the sky. It is only of medium brightness. Sirius is brighter.
- Thinking that the stars in a constellation are close to each other in space.
- Thinking that the stars move around Earth. The apparent motion is caused by Earth's rotation.
- Thinking that the Sun rises exactly in the east every day. It rises exactly in the east only on two days of the year, the equinoxes.

## Remember

- There are 88 official constellations.
- Earth turns 15° each hour, so stars seem to move from east to west.
- The altitude of Polaris equals the latitude of the observer.
