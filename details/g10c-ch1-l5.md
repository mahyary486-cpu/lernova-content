# Atomic Models and Light

Our picture of the atom changed several times as new evidence appeared. The most important evidence about the electrons came from light, because atoms emit light of particular colors when they are heated, and these colors carry information about the energy of the electrons. In this lesson we review the sequence of atomic models and then learn the two equations that connect wavelength, frequency and energy.

## Key ideas

The models follow one another in order. Dalton pictured atoms as tiny solid spheres that are different for each element. Thomson found electrons and proposed a positive sphere with electrons embedded in it. Rutherford discovered that the positive charge and most of the mass are concentrated in a small, dense nucleus. Bohr proposed that electrons move in fixed orbits with set energy levels, which explained the light from hydrogen. The quantum mechanical model, used today, says that electrons are found in orbitals, regions of space where an electron is likely to be, and it does not give them exact paths.

Light is an electromagnetic wave. Its wavelength, lambda, is the distance between two crests, and its frequency, f, is the number of waves per second, in hertz. They are related to the speed of light, c = 3.00 x 10^8 m/s, by c = lambda x f. A longer wavelength means a lower frequency. The energy of a photon, a packet of light, is E = h f, where h = 6.626 x 10^-34 J s is Planck's constant. Combining the two equations gives E = h c / lambda, so a shorter wavelength means a higher energy. In the visible spectrum, red light has the longest wavelength and the lowest energy, and violet has the shortest wavelength and the highest energy.

The link with atoms is this. Electrons can only have certain energies. When an atom is heated, an electron may jump to a higher level, which is absorption. When it falls back to a lower level, it emits a photon whose energy equals the difference between the two levels. Since only certain differences are possible, only certain colors appear, and the result is a line spectrum, a set of thin bright lines on a dark background. Each element has its own set of lines, which works like a fingerprint, and astronomers use it to find out what distant stars are made of.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Frequency of green light.** Green light has a wavelength of 500 nm. Find its frequency.
1. Convert to meters: 500 nm = 5.00 x 10^-7 m.
2. Use f = c / lambda = 3.00 x 10^8 / 5.00 x 10^-7.
3. The frequency is f = 6.00 x 10^14 Hz.

**Example 2: Energy of the same photon.** Find the energy of a photon of that frequency.
1. Use E = h f.
2. E = 6.626 x 10^-34 x 6.00 x 10^14.
3. The energy is E = 3.98 x 10^-19 J.

**Example 3: A 400 nm photon.** Find the energy of a photon of wavelength 400 nm.
1. Convert: 400 nm = 4.00 x 10^-7 m.
2. E = h c / lambda = (6.626 x 10^-34 x 3.00 x 10^8) / 4.00 x 10^-7.
3. The energy is E = 4.97 x 10^-19 J, which is larger than for green light, as expected for a shorter wavelength.

## Common mistakes

Students often forget to convert nanometers to meters, which makes the answer wrong by a factor of a billion. Another common mistake is to think that a longer wavelength means more energy, when it means less. Many confuse absorption, in which the electron moves up, with emission, in which the electron falls down and a photon leaves. Finally, do not mix up the Thomson model, with electrons inside a positive sphere, and the Rutherford model, with a small nucleus.
