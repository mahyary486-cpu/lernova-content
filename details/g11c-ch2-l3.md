# The Ideal Gas Law

The gas laws of the previous lesson each describe one change at a time and require the amount of gas to stay fixed. A single equation can do much more. The ideal gas law connects pressure, volume, temperature and the number of moles in one statement, and it can describe the air in a room, a balloon or a scuba tank. With it, you can find any one of the four quantities from the other three, and you can also find the density and the molar mass of an unknown gas.

## Key ideas

The ideal gas law is PV = nRT. Here P is the pressure, V the volume, n the amount of gas in moles and T the temperature in kelvins. The symbol R is the universal gas constant. Its value depends on the units used. For pressure in atmospheres and volume in liters, R = 0.0821 L atm/(mol K). For energy calculations, R = 8.31 J/(mol K). You must pick the value of R that matches your units, and convert the data if necessary. Volume must be in liters, so milliliters are divided by 1000 first.

The law contains the earlier ones. If n and T are constant, PV is constant, which is Boyle's law. If n and P are constant, V is proportional to T, which is Charles's law. It also explains the molar volume of 22.4 L at STP: for 1 mol at 273 K and 1 atm, V = nRT / P = 1 x 0.0821 x 273 / 1 = 22.4 L.

Rearranging the law gives useful formulas. The number of moles is n = PV / (RT). Because n = mass / M, the molar mass is M = m R T / (P V). Because density is mass divided by volume, the density of a gas is d = P M / (R T). The last formula shows why a hot gas is less dense than a cold one at the same pressure, which is the reason that a hot-air balloon rises. It also shows that, at the same conditions, a gas with a larger molar mass has a higher density.

The ideal gas law works best at low pressure and high temperature, where real gases behave almost ideally. It is still a very good approximation under ordinary conditions, and it is the main tool of gas calculations.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Moles of a gas.** How many moles of gas are in 5.0 L at 2.0 atm and 300 K, with R = 0.0821?
1. Solve for n: n = PV / (RT).
2. n = 2.0 x 5.0 / (0.0821 x 300) = 10 / 24.63.
3. n = 0.41 mol.

**Example 2: Pressure.** A container holds 2.0 mol of gas in 10 L at 300 K. Find the pressure.
1. Solve for P: P = nRT / V.
2. P = 2.0 x 0.0821 x 300 / 10 = 49.26 / 10.
3. P = 4.9 atm.

**Example 3: Molar mass.** A sample of 1.6 g of a gas occupies 1.0 L at 1.0 atm and 273 K.
1. The moles are n = PV / (RT) = 1.0 x 1.0 / (0.0821 x 273) = 1.0 / 22.41 = 0.0446 mol.
2. The molar mass is M = mass / n = 1.6 g / 0.0446 mol = 35.9 g/mol.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to use the wrong value of R for the units in the problem, such as 8.31 with pressure in atmospheres. Another is to forget to convert Celsius to kelvins. Students also leave the volume in milliliters, which makes the answer a thousand times too large or too small. A last check is worth doing every time: a gas at room conditions has roughly 0.04 mol per liter, and an answer very far from that should make you look for an error.
