# Diffusion, Effusion and Real Gases

The smell of perfume spreads across a room because of diffusion, and a helium balloon deflates faster than one filled with air because helium effuses faster. Both effects depend on how quickly gas particles move, and this speed depends on the mass of the particles. The lesson also asks when the ideal gas model fails, because no real gas is perfectly ideal, and knowing the limits of a model is part of using it well.

## Key ideas

Diffusion is the spreading of one gas through another until the mixture is uniform. Effusion is the escape of a gas through a very small hole into a vacuum. Both are faster for lighter gases, because at the same temperature all gases have the same average kinetic energy, so lighter particles must move faster. Graham's law states this quantitatively: the ratio of the rates of two gases is the square root of the inverse ratio of their molar masses, rate1 / rate2 = sqrt(M2 / M1). The gas with the smaller molar mass is the faster one.

The speed of the particles can be calculated from the root-mean-square speed, v_rms = sqrt(3 R T / M). In this formula the molar mass must be in kilograms per mole and R = 8.31 J/(mol K), so that the result comes out in meters per second. For instance, hydrogen molecules at room temperature move at nearly 2000 m/s, and this is the reason hydrogen escapes from the atmosphere of small planets. The formula also shows that the speed increases with the square root of the temperature, so doubling the temperature does not double the speed.

Real gases deviate from ideal behavior, and the deviations are largest at high pressure and low temperature. The ideal model assumes that the particles have no volume and no attractions. At high pressure the particles are squeezed close together, so their own volume is no longer negligible. At low temperature they move slowly, so the attractions between them have time to pull them together, and the gas can finally condense to a liquid. The van der Waals equation improves the ideal gas law with two corrections, one for the volume of the particles and one for the attractions between them. Gases with strong attractions, such as water vapor, deviate more than small nonpolar gases, such as helium.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Graham's law.** How many times faster does hydrogen (2 g/mol) effuse than oxygen (32 g/mol)?
1. The ratio is rate(H2) / rate(O2) = sqrt(M(O2) / M(H2)) = sqrt(32 / 2).
2. sqrt(16) = 4.
3. Hydrogen effuses 4 times faster than oxygen.

**Example 2: An unknown gas.** An unknown gas effuses 2 times more slowly than methane, CH4, whose molar mass is 16 g/mol. Find its molar mass.
1. The methane is faster, so rate(CH4) / rate(x) = 2 = sqrt(M / 16).
2. Squaring both sides gives M / 16 = 4.
3. M = 64 g/mol.

**Example 3: Real gases.** Under which conditions does a real gas behave least ideally?
1. Particle volume and attractions matter most when the particles are close together and slow.
2. This happens at high pressure and low temperature, near the point where the gas condenses.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to put the molar masses the wrong way round in Graham's law. The check is simple: the lighter gas must come out faster. Another is to use grams per mole in the formula for rms speed, where the units must be kilograms per mole. Some students also think that real gases are ideal at high pressure, but this is where the model fails most. Finally, remember that diffusion and effusion are different, since effusion needs a small hole, and diffusion needs no barrier.
