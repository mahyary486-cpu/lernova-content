# Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation

Newton realized that the force that pulls an apple down to the ground is the same force that keeps the Moon in orbit around Earth. Before him, the heavens and the Earth were thought to follow different rules. Newton showed that one law covers both: every object in the universe attracts every other object. The law is simple to state, but it governs the motion of planets, stars and galaxies, and it also explains why you feel your own weight.

## Key ideas

The law of universal gravitation says that two masses m1 and m2 attract each other with a force F = G m1 m2 / r^2, where r is the distance between them and G is a constant, the universal gravitational constant, G = 6.67 x 10^-11 N m^2/kg^2. The extremely small value of G explains why we do not notice the gravity between everyday objects. Two people standing next to each other attract each other with a force far too small to detect. Gravity becomes noticeable only when at least one of the masses is huge, such as a planet.

Gravity is always attractive. It never pushes. The force acts on both masses, and by Newton's third law the two forces are equal in size and opposite in direction. The Earth pulls on you with the same force with which you pull on the Earth; the reason you fall toward the Earth and not the reverse is that your mass is tiny, so the same force gives you a huge acceleration and the Earth a negligible one.

The force depends on the masses and on the distance in a specific way. It is proportional to each mass, so doubling one mass doubles the force, and doubling both masses multiplies the force by 4. It follows an inverse-square law with distance: if the distance doubles, the force falls to one quarter, and if the distance triples, the force falls to one ninth. This rapid weakening means that gravity is strong close to a planet and fades quickly far away.

One detail needs care. The distance r is measured between the centers of the two objects. For a person standing on the ground, r is the distance to the center of the Earth, about 6370 km, not the height above the ground. Using the height above the surface is a frequent source of errors.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Two small masses.** Two masses of 10 kg and 20 kg are 2.0 m apart. Find the gravitational force between them.
1. Use F = G m1 m2 / r^2 = 6.67 x 10^-11 x 10 x 20 / 2.0^2.
2. The numerator is 6.67 x 10^-11 x 200 and the denominator is 4.0.
3. The force is F = 3.3 x 10^-9 N, far too small to feel.

**Example 2: Changing the distance.** The gravitational force between two masses is 12 N. The distance between them is tripled. Find the new force.
1. By the inverse-square law, the force is multiplied by 1 / 3^2 = 1 / 9.
2. The new force is F = 12 / 9 = 1.33 N.

**Example 3: Doubling the masses.** The force between two masses is 12 N. Both masses are doubled with the distance unchanged. Find the new force.
1. The force is proportional to the product m1 x m2, so it is multiplied by 2 x 2 = 4.
2. The new force is F = 4 x 12 = 48 N.

## Common mistakes

Students often use the height above the surface of the planet instead of the distance from its center. Another error is to forget to square the distance, which makes the result far too large. A third is to think that the heavier object pulls with a larger force; the two forces are always equal, and only the accelerations differ. Finally, do not confuse G, the universal constant, with g, the free-fall acceleration, which depends on the planet.
