# Circular Motion and Gravitation

An object that moves in a circle at constant speed is always changing direction, so its velocity is always changing, and it is therefore accelerating. This surprises many students, because the speed does not change. Gravity can supply the inward force that keeps planets and satellites on curved paths, and the same ideas explain a car turning a corner or a stone swung on a string. This lesson links circular motion to Newton's law of gravitation.

## Key ideas

For motion in a circle of radius r, the speed is the circumference divided by the period T, the time for one full turn: v = 2 pi r / T. The velocity is always tangent to the circle, but its direction keeps changing. The resulting acceleration points toward the center and is called the centripetal acceleration: a_c = v^2 / r. A faster speed or a tighter circle gives a larger acceleration.

By Newton's second law, an inward acceleration needs an inward net force, called the centripetal force: F_c = m v^2 / r. It is not a new kind of force. It is simply the name for the net inward force, and it is supplied by something real: the tension of a string, the friction of tires on a road, the normal force of a track, or gravity. For this reason it should never be added as an extra arrow in a free-body diagram. If the inward force disappears, the object does not fly outward; it continues in a straight line along the tangent.

Newton's law of universal gravitation says that every two masses attract each other with a force F = G m1 m2 / r^2, where G = 6.67 x 10^-11 N m^2/kg^2 and r is the distance between the centers. Gravity is always attractive and follows an inverse-square law: doubling the distance makes the force one fourth as large. For a body near the surface of a planet, the same law gives g = G M / R^2, and for a circular orbit it gives the orbital speed v = sqrt(G M / r), because gravity supplies the centripetal force. Satellites stay in orbit because they are continually falling toward the Earth while moving sideways fast enough to keep missing it.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: A car on a curve.** A 1200 kg car rounds a curve of radius 50 m at 15 m/s. Find the centripetal acceleration and the required force.
1. The acceleration is a_c = v^2 / r = 225 / 50 = 4.5 m/s^2, directed toward the center of the curve.
2. The force is F_c = m a_c = 1200 x 4.5 = 5400 N.
3. On a flat road this force is supplied by friction between the tires and the road.

**Example 2: Gravity between two masses.** Two 1000 kg masses are 1.0 m apart. Find the gravitational force between them.
1. Use F = G m1 m2 / r^2 = 6.67 x 10^-11 x 1000 x 1000 / 1.0^2.
2. The force is F = 6.67 x 10^-5 N, a tiny force that is hard to notice.
3. If the distance were doubled, the force would fall to one fourth of this value.

**Example 3: The orbit of a satellite.** Find the orbital speed of a satellite at r = 6.77 x 10^6 m from Earth's center. Earth's mass is 5.97 x 10^24 kg.
1. G M = 6.67 x 10^-11 x 5.97 x 10^24 = 3.98 x 10^14 m^3/s^2.
2. Then G M / r = 3.98 x 10^14 / 6.77 x 10^6 = 5.88 x 10^7.
3. The speed is v = sqrt(5.88 x 10^7) = 7.7 x 10^3 m/s, about 7.7 km/s.
4. As a check of the method, g at the surface is 3.98 x 10^14 / (6.37 x 10^6)^2 = 9.8 m/s^2.

## Common mistakes

Many students think that centripetal force is an extra force to be added to the free-body diagram, when it is the name of the net inward force. Another error is to use v instead of v^2 in a_c = v^2 / r. Some say that there is no acceleration because the speed is constant, forgetting that the direction changes. Finally, in the law of gravitation, do not forget to square the distance, and measure r from the center of the planet, not from its surface.
