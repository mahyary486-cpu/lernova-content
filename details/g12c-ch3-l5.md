# Organic Synthesis and Analysis Methods

Chemists identify an unknown compound by combining several analytical techniques, each of which tells a different part of the story. In the same way, making a new compound rarely takes one step. A drug or a plastic is usually built through a series of reactions, with a purification after each. This lesson looks at the two sides of the work: how a synthesis is planned and judged, and how the product is checked.

## Key ideas

A synthesis is a sequence of reactions that turns a starting material into the target compound. Each step has its own percent yield, and the losses add up. The overall yield is the product of the yields of the individual steps, written as fractions. It is not the average. For this reason, a long synthesis gives a small overall yield even when every step is good. Ten steps with a yield of 90 percent each give only 35 percent in total.

After each reaction the product must be separated from the other substances. Distillation separates liquids with different boiling points. Recrystallization purifies a solid by dissolving it in a hot solvent and letting it crystallize on cooling, while the impurities stay in the solution. Chromatography separates a mixture because its components move at different speeds through a stationary phase.

Several instruments are used to identify the product. In mass spectrometry, the sample is ionized, and the peak of the molecular ion, which is the one with the highest mass, gives the molar mass. In infrared spectroscopy, each type of bond absorbs at a characteristic wavenumber. A broad band near 3300 cm^-1 shows an O-H group, and a strong sharp band near 1700 cm^-1 shows a C=O group. In nuclear magnetic resonance, NMR, the number of signals shows how many different environments there are for the hydrogen atoms, and the size of each signal shows how many hydrogens are in each environment. No single method is enough. A mass of 46 fits both ethanol and dimethyl ether, but the IR spectrum of ethanol has an O-H band and that of the ether does not.

Good practice also considers the purity of the final product. A sharp melting point, or a single spot in chromatography, suggests that a solid is pure.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Overall yield.** A two-step synthesis has yields of 80 percent and 50 percent.
1. Write the yields as fractions: 0.80 and 0.50.
2. Overall yield = 0.80 x 0.50 = 0.40.
3. The overall yield is 40 percent, which is lower than either step, and not the average of 65 percent.

**Example 2: Interpreting an infrared spectrum.** A compound shows a strong peak at 1700 cm^-1 and no O-H band.
1. A strong peak near 1700 cm^-1 indicates a C=O group.
2. The absence of an O-H band means that the compound is not an acid or an alcohol.
3. It could be an aldehyde, a ketone or an ester, and other data are needed to decide.

**Example 3: Interpreting a mass spectrum.** The molecular ion peak is at m/z = 46.
1. The molecular ion peak gives the molar mass, so M = 46 g/mol.
2. Ethanol, C2H6O, fits: 2 x 12 + 6 x 1 + 16 = 46.
3. Dimethyl ether has the same formula and mass, so the IR spectrum is needed to tell them apart.

## Common mistakes

A frequent error is to average the yields of the steps, when they must be multiplied. Another is to confuse the information of IR with that of NMR, because IR identifies groups and NMR shows the environments of the hydrogen atoms. The third is to rely on one technique, since several of them are needed to be sure of a structure.
