# Conservation of Mechanical Energy

Energy can change its form: a falling ball loses potential energy and gains kinetic energy, and a rising ball does the reverse. What stays constant in many situations is the sum of the two. Mechanical energy is the total of kinetic and potential energy, and when it is conserved, we can find speeds and heights directly from energy, without knowing the details of the path. This makes the principle one of the most powerful tools in physics.

## Key ideas

Mechanical energy is E = KE + PE. If only conservative forces do work, such as gravity and the spring force, the mechanical energy stays constant: KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f. Here the subscripts i and f mean initial and final, and the two points can be any two moments of the motion. The principle does not care about the shape of the path, whether it is a straight drop, a curved slide or a roller coaster loop. Only the start and the end matter.

A common application is free fall from rest through a height h. All the lost potential energy becomes kinetic energy, so m g h = 1/2 m v^2. The mass cancels from both sides, and the speed is v = sqrt(2 g h). This is the reason all objects fall equally fast when air resistance is ignored. In a throw upward, the process runs in reverse: the kinetic energy at the start becomes potential energy at the top, where the object is momentarily at rest.

When friction or air resistance acts, the situation changes. These forces are not conservative, and they turn mechanical energy into thermal energy, so the mechanical energy decreases. The energy is not destroyed: the total energy, including the heat produced, is still conserved. In practice, the energy lost to friction is found by E_lost = E_initial - E_final, and it is the work done by the friction force.

A reliable way to use the principle is to choose a reference level for the height, write the total energy at the start, write the total energy at the end, and set the two equal. If friction is present, add a term for the lost energy.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: A dropped ball.** A ball falls from rest from a height of 20 m. Ignore air resistance. Find its speed just before it lands.
1. Equate the potential energy lost to the kinetic energy gained: m g h = 1/2 m v^2, so v^2 = 2 g h.
2. Substitute: v^2 = 2 x 9.8 x 20 = 392.
3. Take the square root: v = 19.8 m/s.

**Example 2: Thrown upward.** A 0.20 kg ball is thrown straight up at 15 m/s. Find its maximum height.
1. At the top the speed is zero, so all kinetic energy has become potential energy: 1/2 m v^2 = m g h.
2. The mass cancels, so h = v^2 / (2 g) = 225 / 19.6.
3. The maximum height is h = 11.5 m.

**Example 3: A slide with friction.** A 50 kg child starts at rest at the top of a 4.0 m high slide and reaches the bottom at 5.0 m/s. How much energy is turned into heat?
1. The potential energy at the top is 50 x 9.8 x 4.0 = 1960 J, and the kinetic energy there is zero.
2. The kinetic energy at the bottom is 0.5 x 50 x 5.0^2 = 625 J.
3. The energy lost to friction is 1960 - 625 = 1335 J, which warms the slide and the child's clothes.

## Common mistakes

A common error is to use conservation of mechanical energy when friction acts, without adding the lost energy. Another is to think that a heavier object falls faster; without air resistance the mass cancels, as shown above. Students also use the total height of a structure instead of the height fallen between the two points being compared. Finally, many forget the square root when solving for the speed and give v^2 instead of v.
