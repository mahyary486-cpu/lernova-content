# Uses of fossils and index fossils

Fossils help scientists learn about past life, past environments and the age of rock layers, and special fossils called index fossils help match layers in different places.

## Key ideas

- **Learning about past life.** Fossils show that many organisms that lived long ago are now extinct. Fossil bones show the shape, size and diet of ancient animals. Fossil series show how groups of living things changed over time.
- **Learning about past environments and climates.** A fossil shell in a desert shows that the area was once under the sea. Fossil tropical plants in a cold region show that the climate there was once warm.
- **Finding resources.** Fossils of tiny sea organisms help oil companies find rock layers that may hold crude oil and natural gas. Coal is formed from the remains of ancient plants.
- **Relative age.** In undisturbed sedimentary rock, lower layers are older and upper layers are younger. Fossils in a layer are as old as the layer. The age found this way is relative: it says "older than" or "younger than", not a number of years.
- **Absolute age.** Scientists measure the decay of radioactive atoms in rock to find an age in years. This is called radiometric dating. It works best on igneous rock and ash layers that lie near the fossil-bearing layers. After one half-life, half of the radioactive atoms in a sample have decayed.
- **Index fossils.** An index fossil comes from an organism that:
  1. lived for a short geologic time,
  2. was found over a wide area, and
  3. was common, so many fossils were left.
  If the same index fossil is found in two places far apart, the rock layers there have about the same age.
- Examples of useful index fossils are trilobites and ammonites (marine animals with shells).

## Examples

**Example 1: Matching layers in two places.** In a mountain in Iran and in a quarry hundreds of kilometers away, scientists find the same species of ammonite in a limestone layer. Ammonite species of this kind lived only for a short time and were widespread, so they are index fossils. The two limestone layers formed at about the same time, even if they look a little different. This lets scientists build one time line for a large region.

**Example 2: Reading the age of a layer.** A rock section has three layers: A at the bottom, B in the middle and C at the top. Layer B holds an index fossil known to be 300 million years old. Layer A is older than B, so it is more than 300 million years old. Layer C is younger than B, so it is less than 300 million years old. We did not need to measure A or C directly to give limits for their ages.

**Example 3: A simple half-life calculation.** A radioactive atom in a rock has a half-life of 1 million years. A sample today has one-fourth of the original atoms left. Step 1: after 1 half-life, 1/2 is left. Step 2: after 2 half-lives, 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 is left. So 2 half-lives have passed. Step 3: age = 2 × 1 million years = 2 million years. The rock is about 2 million years old.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking any fossil is an index fossil. An index fossil must be short-lived, widespread and common.
- Thinking relative age gives a number of years. It only gives the order of events.
- Thinking fossils themselves are dated by their shape alone. The age comes from the layer and from radioactive dating of nearby rock.
- Forgetting that layers can be tilted or folded by Earth movements, which can change the simple order of "older at the bottom".

## Remember

- Fossils show past life, past environments and the order of rock layers.
- Index fossils are short-lived, widespread and common, so they help match layers in different places.
