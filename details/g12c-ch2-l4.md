# Electrolysis and Faraday's Laws

Aluminum, chlorine and sodium hydroxide are produced industrially by electrolysis, and so are the thin layers of chrome on car parts and the gold on jewelry. In electrolysis, electrical energy is used to force a reaction that would not occur by itself. It is the reverse of what a galvanic cell does, and the quantity of product depends directly on the amount of electric charge that passes. Faraday's laws turn this fact into a simple calculation.

## Key ideas

An electrolytic cell uses an external source of current, such as a battery, to drive a non-spontaneous reaction. The names of the electrodes follow the same rule as in the galvanic cell: oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction occurs at the cathode. The signs, however, are reversed. In an electrolytic cell the anode is connected to the positive terminal of the source, and the cathode to the negative one. Electrons are pushed to the cathode, where they reduce the positive ions, and they are removed at the anode, where negative ions or water are oxidized. A good way to avoid confusion between the two kinds of cell is to remember that the cell with a power source is the electrolytic one.

The amount of electric charge is Q = I t, with the current I in amperes and the time t in seconds. The moles of electrons are n_e = Q / F, where F = 96,500 C/mol. The half-reaction then shows how many electrons are needed for each mole of substance. Combining these gives the mass formula m = M I t / (n F), where M is the molar mass and n is the number of electrons per ion. The time must always be in seconds, so minutes and hours have to be converted.

The electrolysis of water gives hydrogen at the cathode and oxygen at the anode, in the volume ratio 2 to 1, as the formula 2 H2O -> 2 H2 + O2 shows. In electroplating, the object to be coated is the cathode, so that the metal ions in the solution are reduced and deposited on its surface. The anode is often a piece of the plating metal, which dissolves to replace the ions that are used. The electrolysis of a molten salt is the simplest case, since only the ions of the salt are present. In a solution, water can also react, and so the products may be different.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Mass deposited.** A current of 2.0 A flows for 3860 s through a solution of Cu 2+. Find the mass of copper, using M = 63.5 g/mol and n = 2.
1. The charge is Q = 2.0 x 3860 = 7720 C, and the moles of electrons are 7720 / 96,500 = 0.080 mol.
2. Each copper ion needs 2 electrons, so the moles of Cu = 0.080 / 2 = 0.040 mol.
3. The mass is 0.040 x 63.5 = 2.54 g.

**Example 2: Time needed.** How long does it take to deposit 1.08 g of silver, with M = 108 g/mol and n = 1, using a current of 0.50 A?
1. The moles of silver are 1.08 / 108 = 0.0100 mol, so the charge needed is Q = 0.0100 x 96,500 = 965 C.
2. The time is t = Q / I = 965 / 0.50 = 1930 s, which is about 32 minutes.

**Example 3: Products from molten NaCl.**
1. Sodium ions are reduced at the cathode: Na+ + e- -> Na.
2. Chloride ions are oxidized at the anode: 2 Cl- -> Cl2 + 2 e-.
3. The products are sodium metal at the cathode and chlorine gas at the anode.

## Common mistakes

Students often confuse the galvanic and the electrolytic cell. A galvanic cell produces current, while an electrolytic cell consumes it. Another error is to forget n in Faraday's law, so that the mass of copper would be double the correct value. The third is to put the time in minutes, when the formula needs seconds, which makes the charge 60 times too small.
