# Galvanic Cells and Standard Electrode Potentials

A battery is a galvanic cell, a device that turns the energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy. Instead of letting the electrons pass directly from one substance to another, the cell separates the two half-reactions, and forces the electrons to travel through a wire. This flow of electrons is an electric current, and it can run a lamp or a phone. The tendency of each half-reaction to occur is measured by a number called the electrode potential.

## Key ideas

A galvanic cell has two half-cells, each with an electrode in a solution. Oxidation takes place at the anode, and reduction takes place at the cathode. In a galvanic cell the anode is the negative electrode and the cathode is the positive one. Electrons flow in the wire from the anode to the cathode. A salt bridge, which holds an unreactive electrolyte, connects the two solutions. It lets ions move so that the charge in each half-cell stays balanced. Without it the current would stop quickly, because the anode solution would build up positive charge and the cathode solution would build up negative charge.

The standard electrode potential, E0, of a half-reaction is measured against the standard hydrogen electrode, which is given the value 0.00 V. Tables list these as reduction potentials. A more positive value means that the species is more easily reduced. The potential of a cell is E cell = E cathode - E anode, where both values are taken from the table of reduction potentials. The half-reaction with the higher reduction potential is the cathode. A positive E cell means that the reaction is spontaneous. Multiplying a half-reaction by a number does not change its potential, because potential measures the energy per unit of charge, and not the total.

The potential is related to the free energy by delta G = -n F E, where n is the number of electrons transferred and F is the Faraday constant, 96,500 C/mol. A positive E gives a negative delta G, which agrees with spontaneity. This relationship shows that a cell potential is the same kind of information as the free-energy change.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Cell potential.** The half-reactions are Zn 2+ + 2 e- -> Zn with E0 = -0.76 V, and Ag+ + e- -> Ag with E0 = +0.80 V. Find E cell.
1. Silver has the higher reduction potential, so it is reduced at the cathode, and zinc is oxidized at the anode.
2. E cell = E cathode - E anode = 0.80 - (-0.76) = 1.56 V.
3. The value is positive, so the cell is spontaneous.

**Example 2: Free energy of the Zn-Cu cell.** The cell has n = 2 and E = 1.10 V.
1. Use delta G = -n F E = -2 x 96,500 C/mol x 1.10 V.
2. delta G = -212,300 J/mol, which is -212 kJ/mol.
3. The negative sign confirms that the reaction is spontaneous.

**Example 3: Is Cu + 2 H+ -> Cu 2+ + H2 spontaneous?**
1. Here hydrogen is reduced, so the cathode is the hydrogen electrode with E0 = 0.00 V, and copper is oxidized at the anode with E0 = +0.34 V.
2. E = 0.00 - 0.34 = -0.34 V.
3. The potential is negative, so the reaction is not spontaneous, which is why copper does not dissolve in dilute acid.

## Common mistakes

Students often use oxidation potentials in the formula, which has no minus sign for the anode, so they get the wrong sign. Another error is to double the potential when a half-reaction is multiplied, but E0 does not depend on the amount. The third mistake is to mix up the anode and the cathode. The reminder is that oxidation happens at the anode, and that both words begin with a vowel.
