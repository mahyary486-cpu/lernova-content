# Solutions: Solute and Solvent

When sugar disappears into tea, it has not vanished. Its particles have spread out evenly among the water particles, and you can prove it by tasting the tea or by evaporating the water and finding the sugar again. Solutions are everywhere in daily life and in nature: sea water, soda, the air and even the blood in our bodies. This lesson explains the parts of a solution and why some substances dissolve in each other while others do not.

## Key ideas

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. It has two kinds of parts. The solute is the substance that is dissolved, and the solvent is the substance that does the dissolving. As a rule, the solvent is the component present in the larger amount. In salt water, salt is the solute and water is the solvent. In a solution of sugar in tea, the sugar is the solute, and the water in the tea is the solvent.

Solutions are not limited to solids in liquids. A solute can be a solid, a liquid or a gas, and so can the solvent. Air is a solution of gases, with nitrogen as the solvent. The fizz in soda is carbon dioxide gas dissolved in water. Alloys such as brass and steel are solid solutions, where one metal is dissolved in another. Water is the most common solvent, and it is often called the universal solvent, because it dissolves so many ionic and polar substances, including salts, sugars and many gases.

When a substance dissolves, the particles of the solute separate from each other and become surrounded by particles of the solvent. No new substance is made. For this reason dissolving is a physical change. The solute can be recovered unchanged: if the water of salt water is evaporated, the salt is left behind. The solute does not disappear, and its mass is added to the mass of the solution.

Whether one substance dissolves in another depends on the types of particles. The rule is like dissolves like. Polar substances, whose molecules have a slight positive and negative side, dissolve in polar solvents, such as sugar and salt in water. Nonpolar substances, such as oil, grease and wax, dissolve in nonpolar solvents, such as gasoline. Polar and nonpolar substances do not mix. This is why oil floats on water, why water does not clean a greasy pan well, and why soap, which has both polar and nonpolar parts, is useful.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Naming parts.** Salt is dissolved in water. Name the solute and the solvent.
1. Salt is the substance that is dissolved, so it is the solute.
2. Water does the dissolving and is present in the larger amount, so it is the solvent.

**Example 2: Recovering the solute.** How can salt be recovered from salt water?
1. Heat the solution gently so that the water evaporates.
2. The salt remains as a solid, which shows that dissolving is a physical change and the salt was not destroyed.

**Example 3: Oil and water.** Why does oil not dissolve in water?
1. Oil is nonpolar, while water is polar.
2. Like dissolves like, so the particles of oil and water are not attracted to each other, and the oil forms a separate layer.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to think that the solute disappears or is destroyed. It is still there, spread out among the solvent particles. Another is to think that the solvent must always be a liquid, but the solvent can be a gas or a solid too. Finally, dissolving is a physical change, not a chemical one, because the substances keep their identities.
