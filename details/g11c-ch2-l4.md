# Dalton's Law, Gas Mixtures and Gas Stoichiometry

The air we breathe is a mixture of about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen and small amounts of other gases. Each gas in the mixture behaves as if it were alone in the container, and each contributes its own share of the total pressure. This is the idea behind Dalton's law, and it allows us to work with mixtures, such as a gas collected over water in the laboratory, and to connect gas volumes to the stoichiometry of reactions.

## Key ideas

Dalton's law of partial pressures says that the total pressure of a mixture of gases is the sum of the partial pressures of the components: P_total = P1 + P2 + P3 + ... The partial pressure of a gas is the pressure it would exert if it alone occupied the container. The law holds because the particles of an ideal gas do not interact, so each kind of particle hits the walls independently of the others.

The partial pressure of a component is also found from its mole fraction. The mole fraction X is the moles of that component divided by the total moles of gas. Then the partial pressure is P_i = X_i x P_total. The mole fractions of all the components add up to 1, and the partial pressures add up to the total pressure. The fraction by volume of a gas is equal to its mole fraction, because equal volumes contain equal moles at the same conditions, but the fraction by mass is not.

A very common application is a gas collected over water. The gas bubbles through water and leaves with some water vapor mixed in, so the pressure inside the collecting vessel is the sum of two partial pressures. The pressure of the dry gas is therefore P_gas = P_total - P_water vapor. The vapor pressure of water depends only on the temperature, and it is looked up in a table.

For stoichiometry with gases, two tools work together. At the same temperature and pressure, gas volumes react in the ratio of the coefficients of the balanced equation, so no conversion to moles is needed. When the conditions are different, find the moles of the gas from the mass and the equation, and then use PV = nRT to get the volume.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Total pressure.** Three gases in a container have partial pressures 0.5 atm, 0.3 atm and 0.2 atm. Find the total pressure.
1. Dalton's law says to add the partial pressures.
2. P_total = 0.5 + 0.3 + 0.2 = 1.0 atm.

**Example 2: Partial pressure from composition.** Air at a total pressure of 1.0 atm is 21 percent oxygen by moles. Find the partial pressure of oxygen.
1. The mole fraction of oxygen is 0.21.
2. P(O2) = X x P_total = 0.21 x 1.0 atm = 0.21 atm.
3. At a mountain altitude where P_total is 0.5 atm, the oxygen pressure is 0.105 atm, which is why breathing is harder.

**Example 3: Gas collected over water.** The total pressure is 1.00 atm and the vapor pressure of water at that temperature is 0.03 atm. Find the pressure of the dry gas.
1. The total pressure is the sum of the gas and the water vapor.
2. P_gas = P_total - P_water vapor = 1.00 - 0.03 = 0.97 atm.

## Common mistakes

A frequent error is to add mole fractions instead of pressures, or to forget that the mole fractions must add up to 1. Another is to forget the water vapor correction when a gas is collected over water, which makes the amount of gas look larger than it is. Students also use the percent by mass of a gas in place of the mole percent, and the partial pressure depends on the number of moles. Finally, remember that the ratio of volumes follows the coefficients only when the temperature and the pressure are the same.
