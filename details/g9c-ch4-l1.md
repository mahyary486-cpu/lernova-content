# Types of Mixtures

Salt water, milk and muddy water are all mixtures, but they behave very differently. Salt water stays clear, milk looks cloudy but does not separate, and muddy water slowly settles when left standing. The difference comes from the size of the particles that are mixed in. By looking at how a mixture behaves, we can sort it into one of three types: solution, colloid or suspension.

## Key ideas

A solution is a homogeneous mixture in which the particles of one substance are spread out as individual atoms, molecules or ions among the particles of the other. These particles are extremely small, less than about one nanometer. A solution is clear and transparent, even if it has a color. It never settles, however long you wait, and its particles pass through any filter paper. A beam of light passes straight through without being visible from the side. Sugar water and salt water are solutions. A solution does not need to be a liquid: air is a solution of gases, and brass is a solid solution of zinc in copper.

A suspension is a heterogeneous mixture in which the particles are large, bigger than about 1000 nanometers. They can often be seen, and the mixture looks cloudy. If left standing, the large particles settle at the bottom, and they can be separated from the liquid by filtration. Muddy water, sand in water and some medicines that must be shaken before use are suspensions.

A colloid lies between the two. Its particles are of medium size, between about 1 and 1000 nanometers. They are too small to settle out or to be caught in a filter, so a colloid looks uniform and stays mixed. But they are large enough to scatter light. This is called the Tyndall effect, and it is seen when the beam of a flashlight becomes visible as it passes through fog, milk or smoke. The Tyndall effect is the standard test to tell a colloid from a true solution, which does not scatter light. Milk, fog, mayonnaise, gelatin and whipped cream are colloids.

In all three types, the substances are not chemically combined, so each keeps its own properties and the mixture can be separated by physical methods. What changes from one type to the next is the particle size, and with it the behavior of the mixture.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Classifying.** Classify sugar water, milk and muddy water.
1. Sugar water is clear, does not settle and does not scatter light, so it is a solution.
2. Milk looks cloudy, does not settle and scatters light, so it is a colloid.
3. Muddy water contains large particles that settle out, so it is a suspension.

**Example 2: The Tyndall test.** A flashlight beam is visible as it passes through a clear-looking liquid. What kind of mixture is it?
1. The beam is visible from the side, so the particles scatter the light.
2. Particles that scatter light but do not settle make it a colloid and not a true solution.

**Example 3: Settling.** A cloudy mixture becomes clear after standing for an hour and leaves a layer of material at the bottom. What type is it?
1. The particles are large enough to fall to the bottom under gravity.
2. A mixture whose particles settle out is a suspension, and it can be separated by filtration.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to think that a solution is always a liquid. Solutions can be gases, liquids or solids. Another is to think that colloids settle, but their particles stay spread out. Finally, students think that mixtures are chemically combined, when in every type of mixture the substances keep their own properties.
