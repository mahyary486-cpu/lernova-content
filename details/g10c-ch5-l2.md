# Molar Mass and Mole Conversions

In the laboratory nobody counts atoms. We weigh a sample on a balance and then need to know how many moles or particles it contains. The molar mass is the tool that makes this possible. It is the mass in grams of one mole of a substance, so it works as the bridge between the mass you measure and the amount of substance you want to know. With it, any piece of data in grams can be turned into moles, and from moles into particles.

## Key ideas

The molar mass of an element, in g/mol, has the same number as its atomic mass on the periodic table. Carbon is 12.01 g/mol and oxygen is 16.00 g/mol. The reason is the way the mole is defined: one mole of atoms has a mass in grams that matches the mass of one atom in atomic mass units. For a compound, the molar mass is the sum of the molar masses of all the atoms in its formula. Subscripts and numbers outside parentheses must be included, since each of them multiplies the number of atoms.

Two equations do most of the work. To find moles from mass, use moles = mass (g) / molar mass (g/mol). To find mass from moles, use mass (g) = moles x molar mass (g/mol). The units guide you: grams divided by grams per mole leaves moles, and moles multiplied by grams per mole leaves grams. If the units do not cancel, the setup is wrong.

When you need the number of particles from a mass, the path has two steps, grams to moles and then moles to particles. Moles are always the middle step, because the molar mass connects grams to moles, and Avogadro's number connects moles to particles. There is no direct conversion between grams and particles.

The values used in these examples are H = 1.008, C = 12.01, O = 16.00, Na = 22.99 and Cl = 35.45 g/mol, with N_A = 6.02 x 10^23 per mole. The number of significant figures in the answer follows the data given in the question, and the molar mass should have at least as many figures as the data.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Molar mass of a compound.** Find the molar mass of Ca(OH)2.
1. One calcium atom contributes 40.08 g/mol.
2. The subscript 2 outside the parentheses means two oxygen atoms and two hydrogen atoms: 2 x 16.00 = 32.00 and 2 x 1.008 = 2.016.
3. The total is 40.08 + 32.00 + 2.016 = 74.10 g/mol.

**Example 2: Mass to moles.** How many moles are in 36.0 g of water, whose molar mass is 18.02 g/mol?
1. The unit wanted is moles, so divide the mass by the molar mass.
2. 36.0 g / 18.02 g/mol = 2.00 mol.

**Example 3: Mass to molecules.** How many molecules are in 9.00 g of water?
1. First find the moles: 9.00 g / 18.02 g/mol = 0.499 mol.
2. Then multiply by Avogadro's number: 0.499 mol x 6.02 x 10^23 = 3.01 x 10^23 molecules.

## Common mistakes

A frequent error is to use the atomic number instead of the atomic mass, which gives a number that is far too small for heavier elements. Students also ignore subscripts or the number outside parentheses when adding up the atoms, which makes the molar mass wrong. Another mistake is to divide when the problem needs multiplication, and checking the units catches it at once. Finally, many students try to go straight from grams to particles. That cannot work, because the two quantities are connected only through moles.
