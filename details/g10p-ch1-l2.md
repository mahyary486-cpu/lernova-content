# Average Speed and Velocity

Speed tells us how fast something moves, and velocity tells us how fast and in which direction. Both are rates: each one is a quantity divided by the time needed. Rates are everywhere in physics, and learning to handle the first two of them carefully makes later topics such as acceleration and force much easier. This lesson shows how the idea of distance leads to speed, how the idea of displacement leads to velocity, and why the two can disagree.

## Key ideas

Average speed is the total distance traveled divided by the total time. Because distance is a scalar, speed is a scalar too: it has no direction and it is never negative. The word total is important. If a trip has several parts, you must add all the distances and all the times first and divide only once at the end. This is the only method that always works.

Average velocity is the displacement divided by the time, written v_avg = delta x / delta t. Because displacement is a vector, so is velocity, and its sign shows the direction of the motion. A negative velocity simply means that the object moves toward the negative side. Since displacement can be zero or small even when the distance is large, average velocity can be much smaller than average speed. When an object comes back to its starting point, its average velocity is exactly zero, although its average speed is not.

Both quantities are measured in meters per second (m/s) in SI units, but everyday life often uses kilometers per hour. To convert from km/h to m/s, divide by 3.6, because 1 km is 1000 m and 1 h is 3600 s. To convert the other way, multiply by 3.6. Doing this conversion before you mix the speed with meters and seconds avoids many wrong answers.

Instantaneous velocity is the velocity at one single moment, like the number on a car speedometer at a given instant. When the velocity is constant, the instantaneous velocity is the same as the average velocity at every moment, and the position at a later time follows x = x0 + vt, where x0 is the starting position. This formula says that the new position is the old position plus the displacement gained during time t.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Unit conversion.** Express a speed of 72 km/h in m/s.
1. Convert the distance and the time: 72 km = 72 000 m and 1 h = 3600 s.
2. Divide: 72 000 / 3600 = 20 m/s.
3. As a quick check, 72 / 3.6 gives the same result, 20 m/s.

**Example 2: A two-part trip.** A car drives 60 km at 30 km/h and then another 60 km at 60 km/h. Find its average speed for the whole trip.
1. The time for the first part is 60 / 30 = 2.0 h, and the time for the second part is 60 / 60 = 1.0 h.
2. The total distance is 60 + 60 = 120 km, and the total time is 2.0 + 1.0 = 3.0 h.
3. The average speed is 120 / 3.0 = 40 km/h. Notice that this is not 45 km/h, the plain average of 30 and 60, because the car spends more time at the slow speed.

**Example 3: Lap and position.** A runner completes one 400 m lap in 80 s and finishes at the starting line. Then a car starts at x = 5 m and moves with a constant velocity of +12 m/s for 6.0 s.
1. For the runner, the average speed is 400 / 80 = 5.0 m/s.
2. The runner's displacement is 0 m, so the average velocity is 0 / 80 = 0 m/s.
3. For the car, x = x0 + vt = 5 + 12 x 6.0 = 5 + 72 = 77 m after 6.0 s.

## Common mistakes

The most frequent error is averaging two speeds without weighting them by time. The correct method is always total distance divided by total time, as the two-part trip shows. A second error is to treat speed and velocity as the same thing; a negative velocity is a legitimate answer and does not mean a slower object. Another error is to forget the unit conversion, for example putting 90 km/h into a formula together with seconds. Finally, when finding where an object is at a later time, students sometimes give only how far it moved and forget to add the starting position x0.
