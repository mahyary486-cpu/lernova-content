# Centripetal Force and Acceleration

A car turning a corner needs a force that points toward the inside of the curve. If the road is icy and friction is too small, the car does not turn; it continues straight ahead. The same is true for a stone swung on a string or a satellite around a planet: something must always pull or push the object toward the center, or it would leave the circle. This lesson explains the acceleration and the force that keep an object on a circular path.

## Key ideas

An object moving in a circle at constant speed changes direction continuously, so it accelerates. This acceleration is called centripetal acceleration, and it points toward the center of the circle. Its size is a_c = v^2 / r, where v is the speed and r is the radius. A higher speed or a tighter curve means a larger acceleration. Notice that it is the radius and not the diameter that appears in the formula.

By Newton's second law, an acceleration requires a net force in the same direction. The net force toward the center is the centripetal force, F_c = m v^2 / r. It is important to understand that centripetal force is not a new kind of force that nature adds. It is the name for the net inward force, and it is always supplied by a real interaction: the tension of a string, the friction of the road, the normal force of a track, or gravity. When you draw a free-body diagram, you draw the real forces only, and the sum of the inward components equals m v^2 / r.

The formula shows how the force depends on the situation. Doubling the speed makes the force four times larger, because of the square, while doubling the radius cuts the force in half. This is why sharp turns at high speed are dangerous. For a car on a flat curve, friction provides the centripetal force, and the largest friction available is mu m g, so m v^2 / r = mu m g and the maximum speed is v_max = sqrt(mu g r). The mass cancels, so a heavy truck and a light car have the same limit on the same road.

If the inward force suddenly disappears, for example if a string breaks, the object does not fly outward. It moves in a straight line along the tangent, in the direction of its velocity at that moment, as the first law predicts. The feeling of being pushed outward in a turning car comes from your own inertia, not from a real outward force.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Acceleration.** A car moves at 10 m/s on a circle of radius 50 m. Find its centripetal acceleration.
1. Use a_c = v^2 / r = 10^2 / 50 = 100 / 50.
2. The acceleration is 2.0 m/s^2, directed toward the center of the circle.

**Example 2: Force on a ball.** A 2.0 kg ball moves at 3.0 m/s in a horizontal circle of radius 1.0 m on a string. Find the tension.
1. The tension is the inward force, so F = m v^2 / r = 2.0 x 9.0 / 1.0.
2. The tension is F = 18 N.
3. If the speed were doubled to 6.0 m/s, the tension would become four times larger, 72 N.

**Example 3: A flat curve.** A car rounds a flat curve of radius 100 m, and the coefficient of friction is mu = 0.50. Find the maximum speed. Use g = 9.8 m/s^2.
1. Use v_max = sqrt(mu g r) = sqrt(0.50 x 9.8 x 100).
2. The value under the root is 490.
3. The maximum speed is v_max = 22 m/s, about 80 km/h.

## Common mistakes

Students often treat centripetal force as an extra force on the free-body diagram, when it is only the sum of the real inward forces. Another error is to use the diameter instead of the radius in the formulas. Many also believe that an outward force acts on the object; the sense of being thrown outward is only inertia. Finally, remember that when the speed doubles, the force required becomes four times larger, not twice.
