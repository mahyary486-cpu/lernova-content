# Chemical Properties

Paper can burn and iron can rust. Each of these is a chemical property, because it describes how a substance can change into something new. Physical properties tell us what a substance is like, and chemical properties tell us what a substance can become when it meets other substances. Chemical properties explain why we store gasoline carefully, why we paint iron bridges and why gold jewelry stays shiny for centuries.

## Key ideas

A chemical property describes the ability of a substance to change into a new substance with different properties. Typical examples are flammability, which is the ability to burn; reactivity with oxygen, such as the rusting of iron; reactivity with acids, such as the fizzing of zinc; and the tendency to decompose when heated. Each of these involves a chemical reaction, in which the original substance is used up and new substances appear.

A chemical property can be observed only when the substance tries to react. You cannot learn that wood is flammable by looking at it, but only by seeing it burn, or by knowing from past experience. However, the substance has the property all the time, even when no reaction is taking place. A piece of wood in a cold room is still flammable. The property is a part of what the substance is, not an event that happens only during burning.

Substances differ widely in their chemical properties. Sodium reacts violently with water, while gold does not react with water or oxygen at all. The noble gases, such as helium and neon, have almost no chemical properties of this kind, because they do not react with other substances in normal conditions. A good way to compare is to ask what happens when the substance meets oxygen, water, an acid or heat.

It is important to keep chemical properties apart from physical ones, and properties apart from changes. A property describes what a substance can do, while a change is what actually happens. The flammability of paper is a property, but the burning of paper is a chemical change. Properties like color, density and boiling point can be measured without changing the substance, and for this reason they are physical.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Flammability.** Wood burns in air. What kind of property is this?
1. Burning changes the wood into ash, carbon dioxide, water vapor and heat. These are new substances.
2. The ability to burn requires a chemical reaction, so flammability is a chemical property.

**Example 2: Rusting.** An iron nail left in moist air slowly turns brown and flaky. What does this tell us?
1. Iron reacts with oxygen and water in the air and forms a new substance, rust.
2. The ability to rust is a chemical property of iron. The nail has this property even when it is kept in dry air and does not rust.

**Example 3: Telling properties apart.** Is the boiling point of water a physical or a chemical property?
1. When water boils, it turns from liquid to steam, but it is still water and its identity does not change.
2. The boiling point can be measured without changing the substance into a new one, so it is a physical property.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to think that a color change always shows a chemical property. Color is a physical property, and mixing two paints changes the color without making a new substance. Students also think that a chemical property exists only while the reaction happens; the substance has the property at all times. Finally, many students mix up properties and changes. Flammability is a property, and burning is the change that shows it.
