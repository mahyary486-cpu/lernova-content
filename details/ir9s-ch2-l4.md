# Covalent bonds

A covalent bond is formed when two atoms share one or more pairs of electrons so that each atom reaches a stable outer shell.

## Key ideas

Covalent bonds usually form between nonmetal atoms. Neither atom can easily lose electrons, so they share them instead.

- A shared pair of electrons is one single bond. We draw it as one line, for example H-H.
- Two shared pairs make a double bond (O=O). Three shared pairs make a triple bond (N≡N).
- The result is usually a molecule: a small group of atoms joined by covalent bonds. Examples: H2, O2, N2, Cl2, H2O, CO2, NH3, CH4.
- The number of bonds an atom makes depends on how many electrons it needs: hydrogen 1, oxygen 2, nitrogen 3, carbon 4, chlorine 1.
- Covalent compounds are usually gases, liquids, or soft solids with low melting and boiling points. This is because forces between separate molecules are weak.
- They generally do not conduct electricity, because they have no free ions or free electrons.
- Some covalent substances are giant structures with billions of atoms bonded together. Diamond and silicon dioxide (sand) are examples. They are very hard and have very high melting points.

Comparing the two kinds of bonding:

- Ionic: electrons transferred, metal + nonmetal, high melting point, conducts when molten or dissolved.
- Covalent: electrons shared, nonmetal + nonmetal, usually low melting point, usually does not conduct.

## Examples

Example 1: Water, H2O.
- Oxygen has 6 outer electrons and needs 2 more.
- Each hydrogen has 1 electron and needs 1 more.
- Oxygen shares one pair with each of two hydrogen atoms.
- Result: O has 6 + 2 = 8 outer electrons, each H has 2. Formula H2O, with two single bonds.

Example 2: Carbon dioxide, CO2.
- Carbon has 4 outer electrons and needs 4 more. Each oxygen needs 2 more.
- Carbon shares two pairs with each oxygen atom: O=C=O.
- Count for carbon: 2 double bonds × 2 shared pairs = 4 pairs = 8 electrons around carbon. Each oxygen also has 8.

Example 3: Nitrogen gas, N2.
- Each nitrogen has 5 outer electrons and needs 3 more.
- They share three pairs: N≡N, a triple bond.
- Each nitrogen now has 5 + 3 = 8 outer electrons. The very strong triple bond makes nitrogen gas unreactive, which is why air is stable.

## Common mistakes

- Using covalent bonding for a metal and a nonmetal such as NaCl. That is ionic.
- Thinking that a double bond means two separate bonds of equal strength to different atoms. It is two shared pairs between the same two atoms.
- Saying that covalent molecules carry a charge. A molecule is neutral.
- Confusing breaking a covalent bond with melting. Melting a molecular substance only separates the molecules, it does not break the bonds inside them.

## Remember

- Covalent bond = sharing of electron pairs between nonmetal atoms.
- Single, double and triple bonds contain 1, 2 and 3 shared pairs.
- Molecular substances usually have low melting points and do not conduct.
