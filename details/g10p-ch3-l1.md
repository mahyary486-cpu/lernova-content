# Force and Free-Body Diagrams

A force is a push or a pull that one object exerts on another. A force can start a motion, stop it, speed it up, or bend its path, and it can also deform an object without moving it. Because a force has both a size and a direction, it is a vector. The SI unit of force is the newton (N), defined by 1 N = 1 kg m/s^2, which is about the weight of a small apple. Almost every problem in mechanics begins with the same step: draw a clear picture of the forces on the object. That picture is the free-body diagram.

## Key ideas

Forces fall into two families. Contact forces need the objects to touch: friction, tension, the normal force and an applied push or pull are examples. Field forces act across a distance without touching: gravity, electric forces and magnetic forces. Four forces appear again and again in problems. Weight is the pull of gravity and always points straight down. The normal force is the push of a surface on an object, and it is perpendicular to the surface. Tension is the pull of a rope or string and points along the rope, away from the object. Friction is parallel to a surface and opposes sliding.

A free-body diagram shows one chosen object, drawn as a dot or a small box, and only the forces acting on that object, drawn as arrows that start at the object. The length of an arrow suggests the size of the force. Forces that the object exerts on other things do not belong in the diagram, since they do not act on it. Drawing the diagram carefully is half of the solution, because it tells you which forces to add.

The net force is the vector sum of all the forces on the object: F_net = F1 + F2 + and so on. Forces along one line add with signs. Perpendicular forces combine with F_net = sqrt(Fx^2 + Fy^2), and the direction comes from tan(theta) = Fy / Fx. A force that points at an angle theta above the horizontal is replaced by its components, Fx = F cos(theta) and Fy = F sin(theta). The net force, not any single force, decides how the motion changes, which the next lessons will make precise.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Perpendicular forces.** A puck on ice feels a force of 12 N east and a force of 5.0 N north. Find the net force.
1. The forces are perpendicular, so F_net = sqrt(12^2 + 5.0^2) = sqrt(169) = 13 N.
2. The direction is theta = tan^-1(5.0 / 12) = 22.6 degrees.
3. The net force is 13 N at 22.6 degrees north of east.

**Example 2: Components of a pull.** A rope pulls a sled with a force of 50 N at 37 degrees above the horizontal. Find the components.
1. The horizontal component is 50 x cos(37) = 50 x 0.799 = 39.9 N.
2. The vertical component is 50 x sin(37) = 50 x 0.602 = 30.1 N.
3. Only the 39.9 N part drives the sled forward; the 30.1 N part lifts slightly on it and reduces the pressure on the ground.

**Example 3: A free-body diagram.** A 2.0 kg book rests on a level table. Draw and analyze the forces.
1. The chosen object is the book. Two forces act on it: its weight, pulling down, and the normal force of the table, pushing up.
2. The weight is mg = 2.0 x 9.8 = 19.6 N.
3. The book is at rest, so the two arrows are equal in length and opposite, and the normal force is also 19.6 N.

## Common mistakes

Students often draw forces that the object exerts on other things; a free-body diagram has only forces on the chosen object. Another mistake is to add a made-up "force of motion" pointing along the velocity; motion does not need its own arrow, and only real pushes and pulls appear. A third error is to add perpendicular forces by plain arithmetic instead of using the Pythagorean theorem. Finally, many mix up sine and cosine: use cosine for the component next to the angle, and sine for the component opposite to it.
