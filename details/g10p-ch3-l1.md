# Force and Free-Body Diagrams

A force is a push or a pull that one object exerts on another. Forces are vectors, so every force has a magnitude and a direction. The SI unit of force is the newton (N), where 1 N = 1 kg m/s^2. Drawing a clear picture of the forces on an object is the first step in solving almost every mechanics problem.

## Key ideas
- Contact forces need touching (friction, tension, normal force, applied push). Field forces act at a distance (gravity, electric, magnetic).
- Common forces: weight (gravity, always straight down), normal force (perpendicular to a surface, pushing on the object), tension (along a rope, pulling), friction (parallel to a surface, opposing sliding).
- A **free-body diagram** shows one chosen object as a dot or box and draws only the forces acting ON that object as arrows starting at the object.
- The **net force** is the vector sum of all forces: F_net = F1 + F2 + ...
- Forces along one line add with signs. Perpendicular forces combine with F_net = sqrt(Fx^2 + Fy^2), and the direction comes from tan(theta) = Fy / Fx.
- A force at angle theta above the horizontal has components Fx = F cos(theta) and Fy = F sin(theta).

## Worked examples
**Example 1: net force of two perpendicular forces.** A puck feels 12 N east and 5 N north.
1. F_net = sqrt(12^2 + 5^2) = sqrt(169) = 13 N.
2. theta = tan^-1(5 / 12) = 22.6 degrees.
3. The net force is 13 N at 22.6 degrees north of east.

**Example 2: components of a pull.** A rope pulls a sled with 50 N at 37 degrees above the horizontal.
1. Horizontal component: 50 x cos(37 degrees) = 50 x 0.799 = 39.9 N.
2. Vertical component: 50 x sin(37 degrees) = 50 x 0.602 = 30.1 N.
3. Only the 39.9 N component drives the sled forward. The 30.1 N component lifts slightly on it.

**Example 3: a free-body diagram.** A book rests on a level table.
1. Identify the object: the book.
2. Forces on the book: weight downward (Earth pulls it) and normal force upward (table pushes it).
3. The book is at rest, so the two arrows are equal in length and opposite.

## Common mistakes
- Drawing forces that the object exerts on others. A free-body diagram shows only forces ON the object.
- Adding a made-up "force of motion". Motion does not need its own force arrow.
- Adding perpendicular forces by simple addition instead of using the Pythagorean theorem.
- Mixing up sine and cosine. Use cosine for the component next to the angle and sine for the component opposite it.
