# Naming Ionic and Covalent Compounds

Thousands of compounds exist, and chemists need a naming system that gives each one a single clear name, so that a name written in one laboratory can be understood in any other. The system works like a set of rules for building a name from the formula, and the first step is always to decide what kind of compound you have. If it contains a metal, or a polyatomic ion such as ammonium, it is ionic. If it is made only of nonmetals, it is covalent, which is also called molecular. This one decision tells you whether prefixes are used, so it is worth making carefully every time.

## Key ideas

Binary ionic compounds contain just two elements, a metal and a nonmetal. The name has two words. The first is the name of the cation, which is the metal and keeps its normal name. The second is the name of the anion, whose ending is changed to -ide. NaCl is sodium chloride, MgO is magnesium oxide and CaBr2 is calcium bromide. No prefixes are used in these names, and the reason is that the charges of the ions already fix the ratio. Calcium is always 2+ and bromide is always 1-, so the formula must be CaBr2, and a prefix would add nothing.

Many transition metals can form ions with more than one charge, so the name must say which one is meant. The charge is written as a Roman numeral in parentheses right after the metal. FeCl2 is iron(II) chloride and FeCl3 is iron(III) chloride. To find the numeral, work backward from the anion. The compound is neutral, so the total positive charge must cancel the total negative charge, and you divide that total by the number of metal ions. Metals of groups 1 and 2, and aluminum, zinc and silver, almost always have one charge, so they need no numeral.

Polyatomic ions are groups of atoms that carry a charge together, and they keep their own names. The important ones are nitrate NO3-, sulfate SO4 2-, hydroxide OH-, ammonium NH4+, carbonate CO3 2- and phosphate PO4 3-. They are named just like single ions, so Ca(OH)2 is calcium hydroxide and (NH4)2S is ammonium sulfide. Never change the ending of a polyatomic ion, because sulfate and sulfide are different ions.

Covalent compounds use Greek prefixes to show how many atoms are present, since nonmetals can combine in several ratios and the charges give no help. The prefixes are mono 1, di 2, tri 3, tetra 4, penta 5, hexa 6, hepta 7, octa 8, nona 9 and deca 10. The prefix mono is not used on the first element. The second element ends in -ide, and when a prefix ends in a or o, that vowel is dropped before oxide. So CO is carbon monoxide, CO2 is carbon dioxide and N2O4 is dinitrogen tetroxide.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Name Fe2O3.**
1. Oxide is O 2-, so three oxide ions give a total charge of 3 x (-2) = -6.
2. The two iron ions must supply +6 in total, so each Fe has a charge of +6 / 2 = +3.
3. The name is iron(III) oxide.

**Example 2: Name CuSO4.**
1. Sulfate is the polyatomic ion SO4 2-, and there is only one of it, so the total negative charge is -2.
2. There is one copper ion, so it must be 2+.
3. The name is copper(II) sulfate.

**Example 3: Name P4O10.**
1. Both elements are nonmetals, so the compound is covalent and prefixes are needed.
2. Four phosphorus atoms use the prefix tetra, and ten oxygen atoms use deca.
3. The name is tetraphosphorus decoxide, with the a of deca dropped before oxide.

## Common mistakes

A very common error is to use prefixes in ionic names, such as calling NaCl sodium monochloride. It happens because students learn the prefixes first and apply them everywhere, but ionic compounds never need them. Another error is to leave out the Roman numeral for a metal such as iron or copper, which makes the name ambiguous. Students also put mono on the first element, but CO2 is carbon dioxide and not monocarbon dioxide. Finally, some change the ending of a polyatomic ion to -ide, but the names of these ions are fixed, and sulfate does not become sulfide.
