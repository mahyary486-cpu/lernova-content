# Naming Ionic and Covalent Compounds

Chemists use a systematic naming system so that every compound has one clear name. The first step is to decide whether a compound is ionic (a metal with a nonmetal, or a polyatomic ion) or covalent (two nonmetals).

## Key ideas
**Binary ionic compounds.** Name the cation (metal) first, then the anion with the ending -ide: NaCl is sodium chloride, MgO is magnesium oxide, CaBr2 is calcium bromide. Never use prefixes for ionic compounds.

**Transition metals with more than one charge.** Write the charge as a Roman numeral in parentheses: FeCl2 is iron(II) chloride and FeCl3 is iron(III) chloride. Find the charge from the total negative charge, because the compound is neutral.

**Polyatomic ions.** Keep the ion name: NO3- nitrate, SO4 2- sulfate, OH- hydroxide, NH4+ ammonium, CO3 2- carbonate, PO4 3- phosphate. Ca(OH)2 is calcium hydroxide and (NH4)2S is ammonium sulfide.

**Covalent (molecular) compounds.** Use prefixes: mono 1, di 2, tri 3, tetra 4, penta 5, hexa 6, hepta 7, octa 8, nona 9, deca 10. Do not use mono on the first element. The second element ends in -ide. The final a or o of a prefix is dropped before oxide: CO is carbon monoxide, CO2 is carbon dioxide, N2O4 is dinitrogen tetroxide.

## Worked examples
**Example 1: Fe2O3**
1. Oxide is O 2-, so three oxides give a charge of 3 x (-2) = -6.
2. Two iron ions must give +6, so each Fe is +3.
3. Name: iron(III) oxide.

**Example 2: CuSO4**
1. Sulfate is SO4 2-, so copper must be 2+.
2. Name: copper(II) sulfate.

**Example 3: P4O10**
1. Both elements are nonmetals, so the compound is covalent.
2. Four P is tetra and ten O is deca.
3. Name: tetraphosphorus decoxide.

## Common mistakes
- Using prefixes in ionic names, such as calling NaCl sodium monochloride.
- Forgetting the Roman numeral for a transition metal such as iron or copper.
- Putting mono on the first element: CO2 is carbon dioxide, not monocarbon dioxide.
- Changing the ending of a polyatomic ion: sulfate does not become sulfide.
