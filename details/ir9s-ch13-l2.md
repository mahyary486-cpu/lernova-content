# Worms

Worms are soft-bodied invertebrates with long bodies, and they are grouped into flatworms, roundworms, and segmented worms.

## Key ideas

- Worms have bilateral symmetry: the left and right sides of the body are mirror images. They have a front end (head) and a back end.
- **Flatworms (phylum Platyhelminthes)** have flat, thin bodies with no body cavity. Their digestive system has only one opening, so food and waste pass through the same hole. Some are free-living, such as planarians, which can regrow lost parts. Others are parasites, such as tapeworms and flukes. A parasite lives on or in another living thing, the host, and harms it.
- Tapeworms live in the intestines of animals. They have no digestive system and absorb food already digested by the host. Their eggs can pass into food or water and infect new hosts.
- **Roundworms (phylum Nematoda)** have long, thin, round bodies with pointed ends. Their digestive tube has two openings: a mouth and an anus. This one-way tube is more efficient. Many live free in soil and water. Others are parasites, such as hookworms, pinworms, and Ascaris. A person can be infected by eating unwashed food or walking barefoot on contaminated soil.
- **Segmented worms (phylum Annelida)** have bodies divided into many ring-like segments. Examples are earthworms, leeches, and marine bristle worms.
- Annelids have a closed circulatory system, in which blood stays inside vessels. They also have a nervous system with a simple brain and a nerve cord, and a complete digestive system.
- Earthworms breathe through their moist skin. They live in damp soil, eat dead plant matter, and leave waste called castings that fertilize the soil. Their tunnels let air and water reach plant roots.
- Earthworms have both male and female organs (hermaphrodites), but two worms still exchange sperm to reproduce.
- Leeches are mostly external parasites that suck blood. They have been used in medicine to keep blood from clotting.

## Examples

**Example 1: Controlling parasites.** A farmer finds that his cattle are weak. A test shows tapeworms in the intestines. Because tapeworm eggs spread through contaminated food and water, the farmer cleans the water troughs, keeps animals from drinking dirty water, and treats the animals with medicine. This breaks the life cycle of the parasite.

**Example 2: Earthworms in a garden.** A gardener adds earthworms to hard, poor soil. The worms dig tunnels, so air and rainwater enter the soil. They eat dead leaves and produce castings rich in nutrients. After a few months the plants grow better. This shows why earthworms are helpful.

**Example 3: Telling worms apart.** A worm has a body made of ring-shaped segments. This means it is a segmented worm (annelid). Another worm has a smooth, thin, unsegmented body and a digestive tube with a mouth and anus. It is a roundworm. A third has a flat ribbon-like body and a digestive system with one opening. It is a flatworm.

## Common mistakes

- Treating all worms as one group. They belong to three different phyla.
- Thinking all worms are harmful. Many, such as earthworms, are very useful.
- Mixing up roundworms and segmented worms. Roundworms have no segments.
- Forgetting that flatworms have only one opening in the digestive system.

## Remember

- Flatworms are flat with one digestive opening; roundworms are round with a mouth and anus; annelids are segmented.
- Parasites live in or on a host and harm it.
- Earthworms improve soil.
