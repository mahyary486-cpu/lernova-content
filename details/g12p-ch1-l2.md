# Electric Fields

A charge can affect another charge without touching it. To describe this, physicists use the idea of a field. The electric field tells what force a charge would feel at a given point, even if no charge is there at the moment. It is like a map of the force available in space, and once the map is known, we can find the force on any charge by a single multiplication. Fields make it possible to think about electricity without always thinking about pairs of charges.

## Key ideas

The electric field at a point is defined as the force per unit positive test charge placed there: E = F / q. Its unit is newtons per coulomb (N/C), which is equal to volts per meter (V/m). The field is a vector. Its direction is the direction of the force on a positive charge. Once the field is known, the force on any charge q is F = q E. A positive charge feels a force along the field, and a negative charge feels a force against it. This is the source of many sign mistakes, so always check the sign of the charge.

For a single point charge Q, the field at a distance r is E = k Q / r^2. It points away from a positive charge and toward a negative charge, and it weakens with the square of the distance. For several charges the fields add as vectors, which is superposition again.

Field lines give a picture of the field. They leave positive charges and enter negative charges, and they never cross, because the field at any point has only one direction. The lines are closer where the field is stronger. Between two large parallel plates with opposite charges, the lines are straight and evenly spaced, so the field is uniform, and its size is E = V / d, where V is the potential difference between the plates and d is their separation.

An important fact concerns conductors. In equilibrium, the electric field inside a conductor is zero, because any field would make the free charges move until the field is canceled. For this reason, charges on a conductor sit on its surface.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Field of a point charge.** Find the electric field 0.50 m from a charge of 5.0 x 10^-9 C.
1. Use E = k Q / r^2 = 9 x 10^9 x 5.0 x 10^-9 / 0.50^2.
2. The numerator is 45 and the denominator is 0.25.
3. The field is E = 180 N/C, pointing away from the charge.

**Example 2: Force on a charge.** A charge of 2.0 x 10^-6 C is placed in a field of 300 N/C. Find the force.
1. Use F = q E = 2.0 x 10^-6 x 300.
2. The force is F = 6.0 x 10^-4 N, in the direction of the field because the charge is positive.

**Example 3: An electron in a field.** An electron (mass 9.1 x 10^-31 kg) is in a field of 1000 N/C. Find its acceleration.
1. The force has size F = e E = 1.6 x 10^-19 x 1000 = 1.6 x 10^-16 N.
2. The acceleration is a = F / m = 1.6 x 10^-16 / 9.1 x 10^-31 = 1.76 x 10^14 m/s^2.
3. Its direction is opposite to the field, because the electron is negative. The huge acceleration shows why gravity can be ignored for electrons.

## Common mistakes

A very common mistake is to believe that a negative charge moves along the field; it moves against it. Another is to forget that the field is a vector and add the sizes of fields from several charges without their directions. Many also confuse the field E with the force F; they are related by F = q E, and the field exists even where there is no charge to feel it. Finally, remember that field lines never cross, and that the field inside a conductor in equilibrium is zero.
