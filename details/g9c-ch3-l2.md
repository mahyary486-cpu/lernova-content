# Kinetic Molecular Theory

The behavior of everything from an inflated balloon to boiling water can be explained by one simple idea: matter is made of particles that are always moving, and the faster they move, the more energy they carry. This is the kinetic molecular theory, and it connects the invisible motion of particles to things we can measure, such as temperature and pressure. In this lesson we see what the theory says and how it explains everyday observations.

## Key ideas

The theory states that all matter is made of particles in constant motion. In a solid they vibrate in place, in a liquid they slide past one another, and in a gas they fly freely. Kinetic energy is the energy of motion, so each moving particle has kinetic energy, and the faster it moves, the more it has.

In a gas, the particles are far apart and move quickly in straight lines until they collide with each other or with the walls of the container. Each collision with a wall gives a tiny push, and the huge number of collisions per second adds up to a steady force on the wall. The force on each unit of area is the gas pressure. This is why a sealed balloon stays inflated: the particles inside hit the inner surface from all sides, and they push it outward.

Temperature has a precise meaning in this theory. It is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles. When a substance is heated, its particles move faster on average, and its temperature rises. When it is cooled, the particles slow down. Temperature is not the same as the total energy, because the total depends on how many particles there are as well. The attractions between particles also matter: they are strongest in solids and weakest in gases, which is why particles of a hot solid still stay in their places while particles of a gas fly apart.

Temperature is often measured in degrees Celsius, but scientists also use the Kelvin scale, where K = C + 273. The lowest possible temperature is absolute zero, 0 K or -273 degrees C, where the motion of the particles would be at a minimum. Kelvin temperatures are always positive, and they are used in gas calculations, because they are directly proportional to the average kinetic energy of the particles.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Heating a solid.** What happens to the particles when a solid is heated?
1. The solid takes in energy, and the particles gain kinetic energy.
2. They vibrate faster around their fixed positions, and the temperature of the solid rises.

**Example 2: Gas pressure.** Why does a sealed balloon stay inflated?
1. The gas particles inside move quickly and hit the inner walls of the balloon many times every second.
2. These collisions push the walls outward and create pressure, which keeps the balloon in shape.

**Example 3: Temperature scales.** Convert 25 degrees C to kelvins.
1. Use K = C + 273.
2. K = 25 + 273 = 298 K.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to think that temperature measures the total energy of a substance. It measures the average kinetic energy of the particles, so a small cup of boiling water has a higher temperature than a swimming pool, although the pool contains much more energy. Another mistake is to think that the particles of a cold solid stop moving. They always vibrate, and they would be at their minimum motion only at absolute zero. Finally, students forget that gas calculations require temperatures in kelvins and not in degrees Celsius.
