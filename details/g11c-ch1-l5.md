# Empirical and Molecular Formulas

Hydrogen peroxide, H2O2, and water, H2O, are made of the same two elements, yet they are very different substances. A formula carries important information, and when a chemist discovers a new compound, the formula is not known in advance. It has to be found from experiments that measure the composition. This lesson shows how a set of percentages is turned into a formula.

## Key ideas

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms in a compound. The molecular formula gives the actual number of atoms in one molecule, and it is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. Hydrogen peroxide has the molecular formula H2O2 and the empirical formula HO. Glucose has the molecular formula C6H12O6 and the empirical formula CH2O. Different compounds can therefore share the same empirical formula, and to tell them apart we need one more piece of data, the molar mass.

Finding the empirical formula follows a fixed path: percent, to grams, to moles, then divide by the smallest, then make whole numbers. First, assume a sample of 100 g, so that each percent becomes the same number of grams. Second, convert each mass to moles by dividing by the molar mass of the element, because a formula counts atoms, and not grams. Third, divide every value by the smallest one, which makes the smallest equal to 1. Fourth, check whether all the numbers are close to whole numbers. If they are not, multiply them all by a small integer. A ratio of 1.5 needs a factor of 2, a ratio of 1.33 needs a factor of 3, and a ratio of 1.25 needs 4. Rounding 1.5 to 1 or 2 is wrong, because it changes the formula.

To go from the empirical to the molecular formula, calculate the mass of the empirical formula and divide the molar mass of the compound by it: n = molar mass / empirical formula mass. Then multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by n.

A hydrate is a salt that contains water molecules built into its crystal, written with a dot, such as CuSO4 . 5H2O. The water is part of the formula and must be counted in the molar mass. Heating a hydrate drives off the water, and the loss of mass gives the percent of water.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Empirical formula.** A compound is 40 percent C, 6.7 percent H and 53.3 percent O by mass.
1. In 100 g there are 40 g of C, 6.7 g of H and 53.3 g of O. The moles are C: 40 / 12.01 = 3.33, H: 6.7 / 1.008 = 6.65 and O: 53.3 / 16.00 = 3.33.
2. Divide by the smallest, 3.33, to get the ratio 1 to 2 to 1.
3. The empirical formula is CH2O.

**Example 2: Molecular formula.** The compound above has a molar mass of 180 g/mol.
1. The mass of the empirical formula CH2O is 12 + 2 + 16 = 30 g/mol.
2. The factor is n = 180 / 30 = 6.
3. The molecular formula is C6H12O6, which is glucose.

**Example 3: Water in a hydrate.** Find the percent of water in CuSO4 . 5H2O, whose molar mass is 250 g/mol.
1. Five water molecules per formula unit have a mass of 5 x 18 = 90 g per mole.
2. The percent of water is 90 / 250 x 100 percent = 36 percent.

## Common mistakes

The most frequent mistake is to use the percentages as if they were moles, without dividing by the atomic masses. Another is to round a ratio such as 1.5 to the nearest whole number, instead of multiplying every value by 2. A third is to confuse the two kinds of formula. If the question gives a molar mass, the answer is probably the molecular formula, and the empirical formula is only a step on the way. Finally, remember that the water in a hydrate belongs to the formula, so it must be included in the total mass.
