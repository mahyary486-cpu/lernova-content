# Periodic Trends in Atomic Radius

Atoms do not have a sharp edge, so their size is defined in a practical way: the atomic radius is half of the distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms of the same element. It is usually measured in picometers (1 pm = 10^-12 m). The radius changes in a regular way across the periodic table, and the reasons for the trend, a battle between the pull of the nucleus and the layers of electrons, will return again in the next lessons.

## Key ideas

Going down a group, the atomic radius increases. Each period adds a new energy level, so the outer electrons sit farther from the nucleus. The inner electrons also shield the outer ones from the full pull of the nucleus, which makes the increase in size even larger.

Going from left to right across a period, the radius decreases. This may seem odd, since the atoms have more and more electrons. But the extra electrons enter the same energy level, so they do not add a new layer, and at the same time each step adds one proton to the nucleus. The inner electrons shield the outer ones almost equally along the period, so the outer electrons feel a stronger and stronger pull. This is described by the effective nuclear charge, Zeff, which is approximately the number of protons minus the number of inner (core) electrons. Zeff grows from left to right, and it pulls the electron cloud inward.

Ions follow a related pattern. A cation is smaller than its parent atom, because it has lost its outer electrons, often the whole outer level, and because there is less repulsion among the electrons that remain, so the nucleus pulls them in more tightly. An anion is larger than its parent atom, because the added electrons repel each other while the number of protons stays the same.

Isoelectronic ions have the same number of electrons, such as O 2-, F-, Na+ and Mg 2+, which all have 10. Among them, the ion with more protons pulls the same electrons more strongly, and it is the smallest. So the order of size is O 2- > F- > Na+ > Mg 2+. When asked to compare the sizes of ions, always start by counting the electrons and the protons.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Ranking atoms.** Rank Na, Mg, Si and Cl in order of increasing radius.
1. All four are in period 3, so the radius decreases from left to right.
2. Sodium is the largest and chlorine is the smallest.
3. The increasing order is Cl, Si, Mg, Na.

**Example 2: Effective nuclear charge.** Compare Zeff for Na (Z = 11) and Cl (Z = 17), each with 10 core electrons.
1. For sodium, Zeff = 11 - 10 = 1.
2. For chlorine, Zeff = 17 - 10 = 7.
3. Chlorine pulls its outer electrons about 7 times harder, which is why it is much smaller: 99 pm against 186 pm.

**Example 3: An ion compared with its atom.** The radius of Cl is 99 pm, and the radius of Cl- is 181 pm. Compare them.
1. The ratio is 181 pm / 99 pm = 1.83.
2. The ion is about 1.8 times larger.
3. The reason is that Cl- has 18 electrons but only 17 protons, so the nucleus holds each electron less tightly.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that the radius increases across a period because more electrons are added, but the pull of the nucleus wins. Another mistake is to forget that anions are larger and cations smaller than their atoms. Some ignore the number of protons when comparing isoelectronic ions, and they wrongly say that Mg 2+ is larger than O 2-. Finally, do not confuse the group trend, which increases downward, with the period trend, which decreases across.
