# Titrations, Indicators and Solubility Equilibria

A titration is a method for finding an unknown concentration by reacting the solution with another solution whose concentration is exactly known. If the pH is recorded as the reagent is added, the result is a titration curve, and its steep part marks the equivalence point, where the two reactants are present in exactly the amounts of the equation. The same ideas of equilibrium also apply to slightly soluble salts, which is the second subject of this lesson.

## Key ideas

At the equivalence point, the moles of acid equal the moles of base for a one-to-one reaction. For a monoprotic acid and a monoprotic base, this gives M_a V_a = M_b V_b. If one reactant has two protons or two hydroxide ions, the mole ratio must be used instead, which is a common point of error. The pH at the equivalence point depends on the strengths. A strong acid with a strong base gives pH 7. A weak acid with a strong base gives pH above 7, because the conjugate base that is formed is basic. A strong acid with a weak base gives pH below 7.

For a weak acid, the half-equivalence point is also useful. When half of the acid has been neutralized, the amounts of HA and A- are equal, so the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation gives pH = pKa. Reading the pH at that point on the curve is a direct way to measure the pKa.

An indicator is a weak acid whose color differs from the color of its conjugate base. It changes color over a pH range of about two units. The right indicator is one whose color change is near the pH of the equivalence point, so that the end point that we see matches the equivalence point. For a weak acid and a strong base, an indicator that changes above pH 7 is required.

Slightly soluble salts have a solubility product, Ksp, which is the equilibrium constant of the dissolving process. For AgCl, Ksp = [Ag+][Cl-] = 1.8 x 10^-10. The ion product Q is calculated in the same way for any given concentrations. If Q is greater than Ksp, a precipitate forms. If Q is smaller, the solution is unsaturated. If Q equals Ksp, the solution is saturated. The common-ion effect lowers the solubility of a salt when a solution already contains one of its ions, since the equilibrium shifts toward the solid.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Titration.** A volume of 25.0 mL of HCl is neutralized by 20.0 mL of 0.100 M NaOH. Find the concentration of the acid.
1. The moles of NaOH are 0.100 mol/L x 0.0200 L = 0.00200 mol, which equals the moles of HCl.
2. M(HCl) = 0.00200 mol / 0.0250 L = 0.0800 M.

**Example 2: Molar solubility of AgCl.** Use Ksp = 1.8 x 10^-10.
1. If s mol/L of AgCl dissolves, then [Ag+] = [Cl-] = s, so Ksp = s^2.
2. s = sqrt(1.8 x 10^-10) = 1.3 x 10^-5 mol/L.

**Example 3: Does a precipitate form?** The concentrations are [Ag+] = 1.0 x 10^-4 M and [Cl-] = 1.0 x 10^-4 M.
1. Q = [Ag+][Cl-] = 1.0 x 10^-4 x 1.0 x 10^-4 = 1.0 x 10^-8.
2. Q is much greater than Ksp, which is 1.8 x 10^-10, so AgCl precipitates.

## Common mistakes

A common error is to choose an indicator that does not match the equivalence point, so that the color change appears too early or too late. Another is to forget the mole ratio when the acid is diprotic, because one mole of H2SO4 needs two moles of NaOH. Students also confuse Q with Ksp. Ksp is a constant for a saturated solution, while Q is calculated from the actual concentrations and is compared with it.
