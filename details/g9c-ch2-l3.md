# Electron Arrangement

Electrons are not spread randomly around the nucleus. They sit in layers called shells, and the outermost layer decides how an atom will react with other atoms. Once you know how to arrange the electrons of an atom, you can predict whether it will give electrons away, take electrons or stay unreactive. This simple model is enough to explain most of the chemistry in the first 20 elements.

## Key ideas

Electrons occupy shells, also called energy levels, at different distances from the nucleus. The shell closest to the nucleus has the lowest energy. Each shell can hold only a certain number of electrons. For the first 20 elements, the rules are that the first shell holds a maximum of 2 electrons, the second holds a maximum of 8, and the third also holds 8 before the fourth starts to fill. Electrons fill the lowest energy shell first, and move to the next shell only when the one before it is full.

The arrangement is written as a list of numbers, one for each shell, starting from the nucleus. Sodium, with 11 electrons, is written 2, 8, 1. Oxygen, with 8 electrons, is 2, 6, and chlorine, with 17 electrons, is 2, 8, 7. To find the arrangement, start with the atomic number, which equals the number of electrons in a neutral atom, and place the electrons shell by shell.

The electrons in the outermost shell are called valence electrons, and they are the ones that take part in chemical reactions. The number of valence electrons is the last number in the arrangement: sodium has 1, oxygen has 6, and chlorine has 7. The inner electrons are shielded by the outer ones and are not involved in bonding. This idea explains why elements with the same number of valence electrons behave in a similar way.

An outer shell that is full makes an atom especially stable. Helium, with 2 electrons in its only shell, and neon, with the arrangement 2, 8, have full outer shells, and they are almost completely unreactive. Other atoms react in order to reach this stable arrangement. Sodium tends to lose its single valence electron, so that the second shell, which is full, becomes the outer one. Chlorine, which needs only one more electron to complete its outer shell, tends to gain one. This drive toward a full outer shell is the key to the formation of ions and compounds.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Sodium.** Sodium has 11 electrons. Write its electron arrangement.
1. Fill the first shell with 2 electrons, leaving 9.
2. Fill the second shell with 8 electrons, leaving 1.
3. The last electron goes in the third shell, so the arrangement is 2, 8, 1.

**Example 2: Valence electrons.** Oxygen has the arrangement 2, 6. How many valence electrons does it have, and what will it tend to do?
1. The last number is the outer shell, so oxygen has 6 valence electrons.
2. It needs 2 more electrons to fill the shell to 8, so it tends to gain 2 electrons when it reacts.

**Example 3: Stability.** Why is neon unreactive?
1. Neon has 10 electrons, arranged 2, 8.
2. Its outer shell holds 8 electrons and is full, so the atom is stable and has no tendency to gain, lose or share electrons.

## Common mistakes

Students sometimes put more than 2 electrons in the first shell, which can only hold 2. Another mistake is to count all the electrons as valence electrons, when only the outer shell counts. For chlorine, the number is 7 and not 17. Finally, students fill the third shell before the second shell is full. The shells must be filled in order, starting from the one closest to the nucleus.
