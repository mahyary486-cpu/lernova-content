# Solubility and the Factors Affecting It

A bottle of soda goes flat when it is warm or when it has been left open, because less gas can stay dissolved at a higher temperature or at a lower pressure. Solubility is the maximum amount of a solute that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature. It explains why some substances mix completely and others do not, why stalactites form in caves, and why divers must come up slowly. This lesson looks at what controls solubility and how to predict when a solid will separate from a solution.

## Key ideas

The first rule is that like dissolves like. Polar and ionic substances dissolve in polar solvents such as water, because the attraction between the solute and the solvent particles can replace the attraction within the solute. Nonpolar substances such as oil dissolve in nonpolar solvents. Oil and water do not mix, since the water molecules attract each other much more strongly than they attract oil molecules.

Temperature has opposite effects on solids and gases. For most solid solutes, a higher temperature gives a higher solubility, because the extra energy helps to break up the solid. For gases, a higher temperature gives a lower solubility, because the gas particles gain energy and escape from the liquid more easily. This is why warm soda loses its fizz, and why lakes that warm up hold less oxygen for fish.

For gases, the pressure matters as well. Henry's law says that the solubility of a gas is proportional to its partial pressure above the liquid: S = k P. When a soda bottle is opened, the pressure above the liquid drops, and the dissolved carbon dioxide leaves the liquid as bubbles. The law applies to gases only, and not to solids and liquids.

A solution can be unsaturated, which means that more solute can still dissolve, or saturated, which means that the maximum amount is already dissolved. A supersaturated solution holds more than the maximum, and it is unstable. A small crystal or a shake makes the extra solute crystallize at once. Saturated is not the same as concentrated, because a saturated solution of a poorly soluble substance can be very dilute.

When two solutions are mixed, a precipitate forms if the ions can combine into an insoluble compound. The useful rules are that all nitrates and all salts of group 1 metals are soluble, while carbonates and hydroxides are usually insoluble, except those of group 1. Most chlorides are soluble, except AgCl and PbCl2.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Henry's law.** A gas has a solubility of 0.02 mol/L at 1.0 atm. Find its solubility at 3.0 atm.
1. The solubility is proportional to the pressure, so the ratio of solubilities equals the ratio of pressures.
2. S = 0.02 mol/L x 3.0 / 1.0 = 0.06 mol/L.

**Example 2: Predicting a precipitate.** Does a precipitate form when AgNO3 and NaCl solutions are mixed?
1. The ions swap partners, giving AgCl and NaNO3.
2. All nitrates are soluble, but AgCl is one of the insoluble chlorides.
3. Silver chloride precipitates as a white solid.

**Example 3: Temperature and gases.** Why does a soda go flat faster when it is warm?
1. The solubility of a gas decreases as the temperature rises.
2. At a higher temperature the carbon dioxide leaves the liquid more easily, and the soda loses its fizz sooner.

## Common mistakes

A common error is to think that all solids dissolve better when hot, but some salts are almost unaffected by temperature, and a few dissolve less. Another is to confuse saturated with concentrated, since these words describe different things: one refers to the maximum amount that can dissolve, and the other to the amount actually present. Finally, Henry's law applies only to gases. It must not be used for a solid in water.
