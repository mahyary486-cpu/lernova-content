# Metallic Bonds and Intermolecular Forces

Two kinds of attraction remain to be described. The first is the metallic bond, which holds a piece of metal together. The second is the group of intermolecular forces, which are the attractions between separate molecules. They are much weaker than the bonds inside molecules, but they decide whether a substance is a gas, a liquid or a solid, and they explain why water boils at 100 degrees C while a similar molecule boils far below zero.

## Key ideas

In a metal, the valence electrons are not tied to any one atom. They are delocalized and move freely among the positive metal ions, in what is often called a sea of electrons. The attraction between the ions and this sea is the metallic bond. It explains the main properties of metals. They conduct electricity and heat, because the electrons are free to move. They are malleable and ductile, because layers of ions can slide past each other and the sea of electrons keeps holding them together. They have a luster, because the free electrons reflect light. An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements, for example brass (copper and zinc), steel (iron and carbon) and bronze (copper and tin).

Intermolecular forces (IMFs) attract separate molecules to each other. London dispersion forces are present in all substances. They come from temporary dipoles, produced when the electrons happen to be unevenly distributed. They grow stronger as molecules get larger and have more electrons, which is why the boiling points of the noble gases rise from helium to neon, argon and krypton. Dipole-dipole forces act between permanent polar molecules, such as HCl. Hydrogen bonds are an especially strong kind of dipole-dipole force. They occur when a hydrogen atom bonded to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine is attracted to a lone pair on another N, O or F atom. This is why water (boiling point 100 degrees C) boils far higher than hydrogen sulfide (about -60 degrees C), even though H2S is a larger molecule.

The typical order of strength is: ionic and metallic bonds are stronger than hydrogen bonds, which are stronger than dipole-dipole forces, which are stronger than dispersion forces. A hydrogen bond is not a true chemical bond, since it is an attraction between molecules. To decide the main force in a substance, check whether it is polar, and whether it has an H bonded to N, O or F.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Copper in brass.** Brass is 70.0 percent copper by mass. Find the mass of copper in 250.0 g of brass.
1. Multiply the fraction by the total mass: 0.700 x 250.0 g.
2. The mass is 175 g of copper.

**Example 2: Which can form hydrogen bonds, CH3OH or CH4?**
1. CH3OH has an H bonded to O, so it can form hydrogen bonds.
2. In CH4 the H is bonded to C, which is not N, O or F.
3. Only CH3OH forms hydrogen bonds, so it boils at a much higher temperature (65 degrees C versus -162 degrees C).

**Example 3: Why does Cl2 liquefy easily?**
1. Cl2 is nonpolar, so only dispersion forces act between its molecules.
2. Each molecule has many electrons, so the dispersion forces are fairly strong, and the gas condenses at -34 degrees C, a much higher temperature than for a small molecule such as H2.

## Common mistakes

Students often call a hydrogen bond a true chemical bond, but it is an intermolecular force. Another mistake is to think that any molecule containing hydrogen can form hydrogen bonds, which needs H bonded to N, O or F. Some believe that nonpolar molecules have no attractions at all, but dispersion forces act between all molecules. Finally, do not mix up the forces inside a molecule, which are bonds, with the forces between molecules.
