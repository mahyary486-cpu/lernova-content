# Metallic Bonds and Intermolecular Forces

## Metallic bonding
In a metal, the valence electrons are **delocalized**. They move freely among positive metal ions, often called a **sea of electrons**. The attraction between the ions and this sea is the **metallic bond**.
- Metals conduct electricity and heat because the electrons move.
- Metals are **malleable** and **ductile**: layers of ions slide past each other and the sea keeps holding them together.
- Metals have a luster because free electrons reflect light.
- An **alloy** is a mixture of a metal with other elements. Brass is copper and zinc; steel is iron and carbon; bronze is copper and tin.

## Intermolecular forces (IMFs)
These attract separate molecules to each other. They are much weaker than bonds inside molecules.
- **London dispersion forces:** temporary dipoles, present in all substances. They get stronger as molecules get larger (more electrons). For noble gases the boiling point rises He, Ne, Ar, Kr.
- **Dipole-dipole forces:** between permanent polar molecules such as HCl.
- **Hydrogen bonds:** an especially strong dipole-dipole force when H is bonded to N, O or F and is attracted to a lone pair on another N, O or F. This is why water (100 C) boils far higher than H2S (about -60 C).

Typical order of strength: ionic and metallic bonds > hydrogen bonds > dipole-dipole > dispersion.

## Worked examples
**Example 1: Copper in brass.**
Brass is 70.0 percent copper by mass. Mass of copper in 250.0 g: 0.700 x 250.0 g = 175 g.

**Example 2: Which can hydrogen bond, CH3OH or CH4?**
1. CH3OH has an H bonded to O, so it can form hydrogen bonds.
2. In CH4 the H is bonded to C, which is not N, O or F.
3. Only CH3OH hydrogen bonds, so it boils higher (65 C versus -162 C).

**Example 3: Why does Cl2 liquefy?**
1. Cl2 is nonpolar, so only dispersion forces act.
2. Cl2 has many electrons, so its dispersion forces are strong enough to make it a gas that liquefies easily (boils at -34 C).

## Common mistakes
- Calling a hydrogen bond a true chemical bond. It is an intermolecular force.
- Thinking any molecule containing H can hydrogen bond.
- Believing nonpolar molecules have no attractions at all.
