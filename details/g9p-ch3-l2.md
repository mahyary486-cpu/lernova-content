# Newton's First Law: Inertia

Why does a standing passenger lurch forward when a bus brakes suddenly? The bus slows down, but the passenger's body keeps moving forward, just as it was before. This everyday experience is described by Newton's first law, one of the three laws that form the foundation of mechanics.

## Key ideas

Newton's first law says that an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion continues to move at constant velocity, which means with the same speed and in the same straight line, unless an unbalanced force acts on it. In other words, objects resist changes to their motion.

This tendency to resist changes in motion is called inertia. Inertia is not a force; it is a property of every object. The measure of an object's inertia is its mass. A bowling ball has more mass than a tennis ball, so it has more inertia and it is harder to start moving or to stop. The same is true in space, where there is no weight; a massive object is still difficult to accelerate.

The first law also corrects a common misunderstanding. People used to think that a force is needed to keep an object moving, because on Earth moving things eventually stop. The reason they stop is friction and air resistance, which are forces. In a place with no friction, such as a puck sliding on very smooth ice or a spacecraft far from planets, an object keeps going at nearly constant velocity without any push.

Many safety devices rely on this law. In a sudden stop, the car is stopped by the brakes, but the passengers tend to keep moving forward. A seat belt provides the force that stops them together with the car. Headrests protect the neck in a collision from behind in a similar way.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: The braking bus.** A bus stops suddenly. Why do standing passengers lean forward?
1. Before braking, the passengers move forward with the bus.
2. The brakes act on the bus, but no force acts on the passengers to stop their bodies at the same moment.
3. By inertia, they continue to move forward while the bus slows. This is not a force pushing them; it is the lack of one.

**Example 2: Constant velocity.** A hockey puck slides across smooth ice at constant velocity. What can you say about the net force on it?
1. Constant velocity means that there is no acceleration.
2. Newton's first law says that without a net force the velocity does not change.
3. So the net force on the puck is zero, or very close to zero if there is a little friction.

**Example 3: Comparing inertia.** Which is harder to start moving, a 2 kg book or a 20 kg box?
1. Inertia depends on mass.
2. The box has ten times the mass of the book.
3. So it has more inertia, and a bigger force is needed to change its motion.

## Common mistakes

A very common mistake is to believe that a force is always needed to keep an object moving. Another is to treat inertia as if it were a force that acts on an object; it is only the tendency to keep the present motion. Some students also think that objects in space have no inertia because they have no weight, but inertia depends on mass, not on weight.
