# Chemical Equations and Balancing

A chemical equation is a short way to describe a chemical reaction. The reactants are written on the left, the products on the right, and an arrow means yields. State symbols show the physical state of each substance: (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas and (aq) for dissolved in water. A correct equation is also a precise statement about amounts, so learning to balance it is the first step toward every calculation that follows.

## Key ideas

The central principle is the conservation of mass. In a chemical reaction atoms are not created or destroyed. They are only rearranged into new combinations. For this reason the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides of the equation, and the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products. An equation that satisfies this condition is called balanced.

An equation is balanced only by changing coefficients, which are the numbers in front of the formulas. A coefficient multiplies the whole formula, so 2H2O means two molecules of water, with 4 H atoms and 2 O atoms. Subscripts must never be changed, because a subscript is part of the identity of a substance. H2O is water, but H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, which is a different compound with different properties. Changing a subscript to make the numbers match is therefore wrong, however convenient it seems.

A reliable method has four steps. First, write the correct formula for every substance. Second, count the atoms of each element on both sides. Third, add coefficients one element at a time. It helps to start with the element that appears in the fewest formulas, and to leave hydrogen and oxygen until last, since they often appear in several places. If a polyatomic ion appears unchanged on both sides, treat it as one unit and count it once. Fourth, recount every element and reduce the coefficients to the smallest whole numbers.

The coefficients also have a meaning for amounts. They give the ratio in moles of the substances, so in 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O, two moles of hydrogen react with one mole of oxygen to give two moles of water. This mole ratio is the bridge to the calculations in later lessons.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Balance C3H8 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O.**
1. Three carbon atoms are on the left, so put the coefficient 3 in front of CO2.
2. Eight hydrogen atoms are on the left, and each water has 2, so put 4 in front of H2O.
3. Count the oxygen on the right: 3 x 2 + 4 x 1 = 10, so the coefficient of O2 is 5.
4. The result is C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O, and the sum of the coefficients is 1 + 5 + 3 + 4 = 13.

**Example 2: Balance Al + O2 -> Al2O3.**
1. Oxygen is 2 on the left and 3 on the right, and the smallest common multiple is 6, so use 3 O2 and 2 Al2O3, which gives 6 O on each side.
2. Two Al2O3 contain 4 Al atoms, so put 4 in front of Al.
3. The result is 4Al + 3O2 -> 2Al2O3, with 4 Al and 6 O on each side.

**Example 3: Check the conservation of mass.** In 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O, 4.0 g of H2 react completely with 32.0 g of O2.
1. The total mass of the reactants is 4.0 + 32.0 = 36.0 g.
2. Mass is conserved, so the mass of the water formed is also 36.0 g.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is changing subscripts to make atoms match, which changes the substance. Another is counting atoms on one side only, so that an error on the other side goes unnoticed, and it is why the final recount matters. Students also forget that a coefficient multiplies the whole formula. For example 2Al2(SO4)3 contains 4 Al, 6 S and 24 O, since the 2 multiplies the 3 and the 4 inside the parentheses as well. Finally, leaving coefficients that can still be reduced, such as 2, 4 and 6, means the equation is not in its simplest form.
