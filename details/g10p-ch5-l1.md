# Linear Momentum

Momentum measures how hard it is to stop a moving object. A loaded truck is harder to stop than a bicycle moving at the same speed, and a fast bullet is hard to stop even though it is very light. Neither mass alone nor speed alone gives the full picture. Momentum combines both, and it turns out to be one of the most useful quantities in physics, because, as the following lessons show, it is conserved in collisions and explosions.

## Key ideas

The momentum of an object is its mass times its velocity: p = mv. The unit follows directly from the definition and is kg m/s. Doubling the mass doubles the momentum, and doubling the speed also doubles it. Because velocity appears in the formula, momentum is a vector. It points in the same direction as the velocity. In one dimension we choose a positive direction and give a negative sign to motion in the opposite direction, so a cart moving left has negative momentum if right is positive.

Momentum is closely tied to force. Newton originally wrote his second law in terms of momentum: the net force equals the rate of change of momentum, F_net = delta p / delta t. For a constant mass this is the same as F = ma, because delta p / delta t = m delta v / delta t = ma. This form shows the real meaning of the law: a net force is what changes momentum, and a larger force changes it faster. It also prepares the ground for the idea of impulse in the next lesson.

Momentum and kinetic energy are different quantities, even though both depend on mass and speed. Kinetic energy is a scalar that grows with the square of the speed, while momentum is a vector that grows in direct proportion to the speed. They are linked by KE = p^2 / (2m), which can be turned around into p = sqrt(2 m KE). Doubling the speed doubles p but makes KE four times larger.

A comparison shows why neither mass nor speed alone decides. A 2000 kg truck at 10 m/s has a momentum of 20 000 kg m/s. A 1000 kg car at 25 m/s has 25 000 kg m/s. The lighter car has more momentum because its speed is so much larger.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Computing momentum.** A 1500 kg car moves at 20 m/s. Find its momentum.
1. Write the definition: p = mv.
2. Substitute the values: p = 1500 x 20.
3. The momentum is 30 000 kg m/s in the direction of travel.

**Example 2: Direction matters.** A 0.50 kg cart moves to the left at 4.0 m/s. Take right as positive. Find its momentum.
1. The velocity is v = -4.0 m/s because the motion is to the left.
2. p = 0.50 x (-4.0) = -2.0 kg m/s.
3. The negative sign tells us that the momentum points to the left.

**Example 3: Momentum from kinetic energy.** A 0.40 kg object has 50 J of kinetic energy. Find its momentum.
1. Use p = sqrt(2 m KE).
2. Substitute: p = sqrt(2 x 0.40 x 50) = sqrt(40).
3. The momentum is 6.3 kg m/s.

## Common mistakes

Students often treat momentum as a scalar and ignore its direction, which causes sign errors in collision problems. Another common error is to confuse momentum (mv) with kinetic energy (1/2 mv^2) or with force (ma); each has different units and meaning. A third mistake is to forget unit conversions, such as km/h to m/s or grams to kilograms, before multiplying. Finally, do not assume that two objects with the same speed have the same momentum, because the mass counts as much as the speed.
