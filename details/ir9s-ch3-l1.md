# Natural cycles

Natural cycles are the repeating paths by which water, nitrogen, oxygen and other materials move between air, water, soil and living things.

## Key ideas

Matter on Earth is not made or destroyed in large amounts. Instead, it moves around in cycles. Because of these cycles, the same atoms can be used again and again by different living things.

The water cycle has several steps:
- Evaporation: the Sun heats liquid water in seas, lakes and rivers, and it becomes water vapor.
- Transpiration: plants release water vapor from their leaves.
- Condensation: water vapor cools high in the air and forms tiny drops, which make clouds.
- Precipitation: water falls as rain, snow or hail.
- Runoff and infiltration: some water flows over the ground into rivers, and some soaks into the soil and becomes groundwater.

The energy that drives the water cycle comes from the Sun. About 97 percent of Earth's water is salty water in the oceans, so only a small part is fresh water.

The nitrogen cycle is important because all living things need nitrogen to make proteins and DNA. Air is about 78 percent nitrogen gas (N2), but most plants and animals cannot use this gas directly. The cycle works like this:
- Nitrogen fixation: lightning and special bacteria change N2 into nitrogen compounds. Some bacteria live in the roots of legumes such as beans, peas and lentils.
- Absorption: plants take up nitrogen compounds from the soil and use them to build proteins.
- Feeding: animals get nitrogen by eating plants or other animals.
- Decay: when plants and animals die, decomposers (bacteria and fungi) return nitrogen compounds to the soil.
- Denitrification: some bacteria change nitrogen compounds back into N2 gas, which returns to the air.

The oxygen cycle is linked to the carbon cycle. Plants and algae release oxygen (O2) during photosynthesis. Animals, plants and microbes use oxygen in respiration. Air is about 21 percent oxygen.

## Examples

Example 1: A rain puddle on a warm day. The Sun heats the puddle, and the liquid water evaporates into vapor. The vapor rises, cools and condenses into cloud drops. Later the drops fall as rain, and part of the water fills the puddle again. The same water has made a full loop.

Example 2: Farmers and beans. A farmer grows wheat in a field for several years, and the soil loses nitrogen compounds because the wheat takes them up. Then the farmer plants beans in the same field. Bacteria in the bean roots fix nitrogen from the air, so the soil gains nitrogen compounds. The next wheat crop grows better with less chemical fertilizer.

Example 3: A fallen tree in a forest. Fungi and bacteria break down the wood. They release nitrogen compounds into the soil, and nearby young plants absorb them. Without decomposers, these nutrients would stay locked inside dead material.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking that water is used up when it rains. The water is only moving to another place in the cycle.
- Saying that plants take nitrogen gas straight from the air. Most plants need nitrogen compounds from the soil.
- Forgetting that decomposers are part of the cycle. Without them, nutrients would not return to the soil.
- Believing that the oxygen in the air comes only from trees. Algae and other water plants also make a large share of it.

## Remember

- Natural cycles let the same materials be reused many times.
- The Sun's energy drives the water cycle.
- Bacteria are essential in the nitrogen cycle: they fix, recycle and release nitrogen.
