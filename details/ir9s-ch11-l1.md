# Classification of living things

Classification is the method scientists use to sort living things into groups based on the features they share.

## Key ideas

- There are millions of kinds of living things on Earth. Without a system of groups, it would be almost impossible to study them, talk about them, or compare them.
- Classification places organisms into levels. From the largest group to the smallest, the levels are: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. Each level contains the levels below it. A kingdom is very large and its members share only a few basic features. A species is very small and its members are very similar.
- A species is a group of organisms of the same kind that can mate and produce fertile young, which means young that can also reproduce. A horse and a donkey can produce a mule, but a mule is usually not fertile, so horses and donkeys are different species.
- Every species has a scientific name made of two Latin words. This is called binomial naming. The first word is the genus and starts with a capital letter. The second word is the species and starts with a small letter. The name is written in italics or underlined. Example: Homo sapiens is the scientific name of humans.
- Scientific names are the same in every country. This avoids confusion, because one animal can have many common names in different languages.
- The main kingdoms are animals, plants, fungi, protists and bacteria. Animals are living things that cannot make their own food, so they must eat other organisms. Most animals can move at some stage of their life, and their bodies are made of many cells.
- Scientists classify organisms using features such as cell type, number of cells, how they get food, and body structure. Today they also compare genes, because similar genes suggest close relationship.

## Examples

**Example 1: Classifying the lion.** Kingdom: Animalia. Phylum: Chordata (animals with a backbone or similar rod). Class: Mammalia (animals with fur that feed milk to their young). Order: Carnivora (meat eaters). Family: Felidae (cats). Genus: Panthera. Species: Panthera leo. Notice that each level is more specific than the one before it.

**Example 2: Comparing two animals.** The lion (Panthera leo) and the tiger (Panthera tigris) have the same genus, Panthera. So they are close relatives. The domestic cat (Felis catus) is in the same family, Felidae, but in a different genus. So the cat is related to the lion, but less closely than the tiger is.

**Example 3: Why a scientific name is useful.** The animal called "puma" in one country is called "cougar" or "mountain lion" in others. All these names mean the same animal. Its scientific name, Puma concolor, is the same everywhere, so scientists in Iran, Brazil and Japan all know which animal is meant.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking that a bigger group has more similar members. The opposite is true: a kingdom has very different members, while a species has very similar members.
- Writing the scientific name wrongly. The genus must have a capital letter and the species must start with a small letter, for example Homo sapiens, not homo Sapiens.
- Believing that animals which look alike must be in the same group. A bat flies like a bird, but it has fur and feeds milk to its young, so it is a mammal, not a bird.
- Saying that two organisms are the same species only because they can mate. They must also produce fertile young.

## Remember

- The seven levels, from largest to smallest, are kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
- A scientific name has two parts: genus first, species second.
- Classification makes it easier to study living things and shows how closely they are related.
