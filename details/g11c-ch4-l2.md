# Calorimetry and Specific Heat

Nobody can see the energy inside a reaction directly. To measure it, chemists look at something they can measure, which is the temperature change of the water, or of the solution, around the reaction. This is called calorimetry. If the temperature of the surroundings rises, the reaction has released heat, and if it falls, the reaction has absorbed heat. With a thermometer and a balance, it is possible to find the enthalpy change of a reaction.

## Key ideas

The heat gained or lost by a substance is given by q = m c delta T. Here m is the mass, delta T is the change in temperature, and c is the specific heat, the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of the substance by 1 degree C. Water has a large specific heat, c = 4.18 J/(g degrees C), which is why it is an excellent coolant and why coastal climates are mild. Metals have small values, so they heat up and cool down quickly.

In a calorimeter, the heat released by the reaction is absorbed by the solution, and so the two have equal sizes and opposite signs: q_rxn = -q_solution. The minus sign is easy to forget, and it is the heart of the method. If the solution warms up, q_solution is positive and q_rxn is negative, which means that the reaction is exothermic. The enthalpy change per mole is then delta H = q_rxn / n, where n is the number of moles of the substance that reacted or formed. The result is divided by 1000 to convert joules to kilojoules.

A simple coffee-cup calorimeter works at constant pressure, so the heat it measures is exactly delta H. A bomb calorimeter is a sealed steel container, used for combustion reactions. It works at constant volume, and it is surrounded by a known mass of water. It is used, for example, to measure the energy content of foods and fuels.

The same equation also solves problems in which a hot object is placed in cold water. The heat lost by the hot object is equal to the heat gained by the cold one, and from this balance an unknown specific heat or a final temperature can be found. Calorimetry rests on the conservation of energy: no energy is lost, and it only moves from one place to another.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Heat absorbed by water.** A sample of 100 g of water warms from 20 degrees C to 30 degrees C.
1. The change in temperature is delta T = 30 - 20 = 10 degrees C.
2. q = m c delta T = 100 x 4.18 x 10 = 4180 J.

**Example 2: Enthalpy of neutralization.** A reaction between an acid and a base forms 0.05 mol of water and warms 100 g of solution by 6.5 degrees C.
1. The heat absorbed by the solution is q = 100 x 4.18 x 6.5 = 2717 J, so the reaction released 2717 J.
2. delta H = -2717 J / 0.05 mol = -54,340 J/mol.
3. This is delta H = -54.3 kJ/mol, and the negative sign shows that the reaction is exothermic.

**Example 3: Specific heat of a metal.** A piece of metal of mass 50 g absorbs 1150 J and its temperature rises by 10 degrees C.
1. Solve for c: c = q / (m delta T).
2. c = 1150 / (50 x 10) = 2.3 J/(g degrees C).

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to forget the minus sign in q_rxn = -q_solution, which gives the wrong sign for delta H. Another is to mix joules and kilojoules. Specific heat is given in joules, but delta H is normally written in kilojoules per mole. Students also divide by the wrong number of moles, so the equation must be checked to see which substance the delta H refers to. Finally, use the mass of the whole solution, and not only of the water or of one reactant, in the formula q = m c delta T.
