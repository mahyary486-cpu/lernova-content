# Sponges and cnidarians

Sponges and cnidarians are two groups of simple invertebrate animals, most of which live in water.

## Key ideas

- An invertebrate is an animal without a backbone. More than 95 percent of all animal species are invertebrates.
- **Sponges (phylum Porifera)** are adults that stay fixed to rocks or the sea floor. The name Porifera means "pore bearer".
- A sponge body is like a hollow vase. Water enters through many small pores, passes into a central cavity, and leaves through a large opening at the top called the osculum.
- Special cells called collar cells have tiny whip-like parts that push water along. They trap food particles such as bacteria and tiny algae.
- Sponges have no true tissues, no organs, no muscles, and no nerves. Their cells work mostly on their own. A hard support made of spicules (tiny needles) or a soft protein called spongin holds the body up.
- Sponges can reproduce sexually, and also asexually by budding. A broken piece can grow into a new sponge.
- **Cnidarians (phylum Cnidaria)** include jellyfish, sea anemones, hydras, and corals.
- They have radial symmetry: the body can be divided into equal parts around a center, like a pizza.
- The body has two cell layers and one opening, the mouth, which is also used to remove waste. A cavity inside digests food.
- Tentacles around the mouth carry cnidocytes, stinging cells. Each one fires a tiny thread that can inject poison into prey.
- Cnidarians have a nerve net, a simple web of nerve cells, but no brain.
- There are two body forms. A polyp is shaped like a tube and is usually attached (sea anemone, hydra). A medusa is shaped like a bell and swims freely (jellyfish). Some cnidarians have both forms in their life.
- Corals are small polyps that build hard skeletons of calcium carbonate. Over many years, these skeletons form coral reefs, which are home to many species.

## Examples

**Example 1: How a sponge feeds.** Water carrying tiny food enters the pores on the side of the sponge. The collar cells in the central cavity beat their whips, so the water keeps moving. The cells catch the food and digest it. The cleaned water leaves through the osculum. A sponge about 10 cm tall can filter many liters of water every day, so sponges help keep seawater clean.

**Example 2: A jellyfish catching prey.** A small fish touches a tentacle of a jellyfish. The cnidocytes fire their stinging threads and poison the fish. The tentacle bends toward the mouth and pushes the fish inside. The nerve net passes the signal quickly, so the whole body reacts, even without a brain.

**Example 3: A coral reef.** Millions of coral polyps live side by side. Each one takes calcium from seawater and builds a hard cup around itself. When polyps die, the cups remain, and new polyps grow on top. Over thousands of years this builds a reef, which shelters fish, crabs, and many other animals.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking sponges are plants because they do not move. Sponges are animals: they cannot make their own food and must eat.
- Thinking a sponge has a mouth like other animals. A sponge takes in water through pores and releases it through the osculum.
- Saying that jellyfish are fish. They have no backbone and are cnidarians.
- Forgetting that cnidarians have only one opening, which works as both mouth and waste exit.

## Remember

- Sponges: pores, osculum, collar cells, no tissues or nerves.
- Cnidarians: radial symmetry, stinging cells, polyp and medusa forms.
- Both groups are invertebrates and live mostly in water.
