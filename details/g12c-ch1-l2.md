# Strength of Acids and Bases: Ka, Kb and Kw

Vinegar and hydrochloric acid can both be 1 M in concentration, yet one is safe on a salad and the other burns skin. The difference is strength. Strength is a property of the substance, and it measures how far the acid ionizes in water. Concentration, by contrast, tells how much of the substance was dissolved. Keeping these two ideas apart is the key to this chapter, and equilibrium constants give us the numbers to compare acids and bases precisely.

## Key ideas

A strong acid ionizes completely in water, so a solution contains almost no unionized acid molecules. The common strong acids are HCl, HBr, HI, HNO3, H2SO4 and HClO4. A weak acid ionizes only partly, and most of its molecules remain intact, with a balance between the acid and its ions. For a weak acid HA, the balance is HA + H2O <-> H3O+ + A-, and its equilibrium constant is the acid ionization constant: Ka = [H3O+][A-] / [HA]. Water is a liquid in large excess, so it is left out. The larger the Ka, the further the ionization goes, and the stronger the acid is. The same idea applies to a base, with Kb.

Water itself ionizes slightly, and the constant for this is Kw = [H3O+][OH-] = 1.0 x 10^-14 at 25 degrees C. In pure water, the two concentrations are equal, 1.0 x 10^-7 M. Taking logarithms gives the useful relation pH + pOH = 14. The value of Kw depends on temperature, so the number 14 holds only at 25 degrees C.

For a conjugate pair, the acid and the base are linked by Ka x Kb = Kw. This follows from multiplying the two equilibrium expressions, and it means that a strong acid has a very weak conjugate base, and a weak acid has a conjugate base of moderate strength. It also lets us find Kb of an anion from the Ka of its acid, without a separate table.

Because Ka values are small numbers with negative exponents, chemists often use pKa = -log Ka. A smaller pKa means a stronger acid, since the exponent is less negative. A pKa of 4.74 corresponds to Ka = 1.8 x 10^-5.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Find Kb for the acetate ion.** Acetic acid has Ka = 1.8 x 10^-5.
1. Acetate is the conjugate base of acetic acid, so Kb = Kw / Ka.
2. Kb = 1.0 x 10^-14 / 1.8 x 10^-5 = 0.556 x 10^-9.
3. In scientific notation, Kb = 5.6 x 10^-10, which is small, so acetate is a weak base.

**Example 2: Find pKa for Ka = 1.8 x 10^-5.**
1. pKa = -log(1.8 x 10^-5).
2. The logarithm of 1.8 is 0.255, so log(1.8 x 10^-5) = 0.255 - 5 = -4.745.
3. pKa = 4.74 (rounded to two decimal places).

**Example 3: Which is the stronger acid, HF with Ka = 7.2 x 10^-4 or HCN with Ka = 6.2 x 10^-10?**
1. A larger Ka means that more of the acid ionizes.
2. Since 7.2 x 10^-4 is much larger than 6.2 x 10^-10, HF is the stronger acid.
3. The ratio is about one million, so HF ionizes far more than HCN at the same concentration.

## Common mistakes

The commonest error is to confuse strong with concentrated. A dilute solution of a strong acid is still a strong acid, and a concentrated solution of a weak acid is still a weak acid. Another error is to use Ka for a base, where the constant is Kb. Students also forget that Kw changes with temperature, so pH 7 is neutral only at 25 degrees C, and pH + pOH = 14 is not valid at other temperatures.
