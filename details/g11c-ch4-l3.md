# Hess's Law and Standard Enthalpies of Formation

Some reactions cannot be measured directly. Carbon does not burn cleanly to carbon monoxide in a calorimeter, because some of it always goes on to carbon dioxide. Hess's law solves this problem: if the enthalpy change cannot be measured for the reaction we want, we can build it from other reactions whose enthalpy changes are known. The answer is the same, because enthalpy depends only on the starting and ending states and not on the route between them.

## Key ideas

Hess's law states that the total enthalpy change of a reaction is the same, whether it takes place in one step or in several. This is true because enthalpy is a state function: it depends only on the present state of the substances and not on how they got there. Hiking up a mountain is a good comparison. The change in height is the same for a steep path and a long gentle path.

To use the law, the known equations are manipulated and then added, in the following way. If an equation is reversed, the sign of its delta H changes. If it is multiplied by a number, its delta H is multiplied by the same number. When the equations are added, the substances that appear on both sides, the intermediates, must cancel completely, and the sum of the delta H values is the answer. Checking that the intermediates cancel is the best test of a correct setup.

The second tool uses standard enthalpies of formation. The enthalpy of formation, delta H_f, is the change in enthalpy when 1 mol of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states. The standard state of an element is its most stable form at 1 atm, for example O2(g), H2(g) and C(graphite), and by definition delta H_f = 0 for each element in this state. The enthalpy change of any reaction is then delta H_rxn = sum of n delta H_f (products) - sum of n delta H_f (reactants), where n is the coefficient of each substance. The products are written first, and the subtraction is always products minus reactants.

This second method is the fastest, since tables of delta H_f values exist for thousands of compounds, and it is a direct application of Hess's law. It works because each compound is imagined to form from its elements, so that the elements act as a common starting point for both the reactants and the products.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Using Hess's law.** Given C + O2 -> CO2 with delta H = -393 kJ, and CO + 1/2 O2 -> CO2 with delta H = -283 kJ, find delta H for C + 1/2 O2 -> CO.
1. Keep the first equation as it is, and reverse the second: CO2 -> CO + 1/2 O2 with delta H = +283 kJ.
2. Add them. The CO2 cancels, and half of the oxygen cancels, leaving C + 1/2 O2 -> CO.
3. delta H = -393 + 283 = -110 kJ.

**Example 2: Using enthalpies of formation.** Find delta H for CH4 + 2 O2 -> CO2 + 2 H2O(l), given delta H_f of CH4 = -75, CO2 = -393 and H2O(l) = -286 kJ/mol.
1. The products give -393 + 2 x (-286) = -965 kJ, and the reactants give -75 kJ (oxygen is an element, so it adds 0).
2. delta H = -965 - (-75) = -890 kJ.

**Example 3: An element in its standard state.** What is delta H_f of O2(g)?
1. O2 is an element in its standard state.
2. By definition delta H_f = 0.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to forget to multiply delta H_f by the coefficients, so that 2 H2O is counted as one mole. Another is to subtract in the wrong order, reactants minus products, which reverses the sign of the answer. Students also give elements a nonzero delta H_f. Finally, in Hess's law problems, do not forget to change the sign when an equation is reversed, and check that the intermediates cancel before adding the delta H values.
