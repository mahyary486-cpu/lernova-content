# Work

In everyday speech, "work" means effort: studying, carrying a bag or holding a heavy box can all feel like work. In physics the word has a precise and narrower meaning. Work is the energy that is transferred when a force makes an object move. This definition lets us measure how much energy a force delivers and compare very different tasks.

## Key ideas

Work is done when a force moves an object in the direction of the force. It is calculated as W = F x d, where F is the force in newtons and d is the distance in meters that the object moves in the direction of the force. The SI unit of work is the joule (J): 1 J = 1 N x 1 m. One joule is about the work needed to lift a small apple by one meter.

The definition has two requirements, a force and a movement. If an object does not move, no work is done, no matter how hard you push or how tired you become. Pushing against a solid wall for ten minutes transfers no energy to the wall, so the work is zero. In the same way, if the force is at a right angle to the motion, it does no work. A waiter who carries a tray horizontally at constant speed holds it up with an upward force, but the tray moves sideways, so that upward force does no work on the tray.

Lifting is an important special case. To lift an object at constant speed, you must pull upward with a force equal to its weight, m x g. The distance is the height h, so the work is W = m x g x h. This work is stored as gravitational potential energy, which the next lessons discuss.

Work is a way of transferring energy: when work is done on an object, the object gains energy. That is why the unit of work is the same as the unit of energy.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Pushing a box.** A force of 20 N moves a box 5 m in the direction of the force. How much work is done?
1. Use W = F x d.
2. Substitute: W = 20 N x 5 m.
3. The work is 100 J.

**Example 2: Pushing a wall.** A student pushes against a wall with 200 N, but the wall does not move. How much work is done?
1. The distance moved by the wall is 0 m.
2. W = 200 x 0 = 0.
3. No work is done on the wall, even though the student pushes hard and gets tired.

**Example 3: Lifting a book.** A 2 kg book is lifted 1.5 m at constant speed. How much work is done? Use g = 9.8 m/s^2.
1. The force needed equals the weight: 2 x 9.8 = 19.6 N.
2. The distance is the height, 1.5 m.
3. W = 19.6 x 1.5 = 29.4 J.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that effort or tiredness always means that work was done, but without movement there is no work. Another mistake is to use the total distance when the force and the motion are not in the same direction. A third error is to confuse mass in kilograms with weight in newtons when lifting; the force in the work formula must be in newtons.
