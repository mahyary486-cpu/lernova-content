# Types of Chemical Reactions and Balancing Equations

Every chemical reaction can be described by an equation, and every correct equation must be balanced. A balanced equation is more than a piece of bookkeeping, because it tells us exactly how many particles of each substance take part, and every calculation in this course, from gas volumes to energy changes, starts from it. This lesson reviews the five basic types of reaction and the method for balancing, which you will use in every chapter that follows.

## Key ideas

The law of conservation of mass says that atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. They are only rearranged into new substances. For this reason, the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides of the equation. We make this true by changing coefficients, the numbers in front of the formulas, and never by changing subscripts. A subscript is part of the identity of a substance, so changing H2O into H2O2 would replace water with hydrogen peroxide, which is a different compound with different properties. A coefficient only changes how many units of the substance take part.

Most reactions fit one of a few patterns. In synthesis, two or more substances combine into one product: A + B -> AB. In decomposition, one compound breaks into simpler substances: AB -> A + B, and this usually needs heat, light or electricity. In single replacement, an element takes the place of another element in a compound: A + BC -> AC + B. In double replacement, two compounds exchange partners: AB + CD -> AD + CB. In combustion, a fuel reacts rapidly with oxygen, and a hydrocarbon burning completely gives carbon dioxide and water. Recognizing the pattern helps you predict the products before you balance.

State symbols tell us the physical condition of each substance: (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas and (aq) for a substance dissolved in water. They matter because later lessons, on gases, solutions and equilibrium, depend on knowing which substances are gases, which are dissolved and which are solids.

A reliable balancing method is to count the atoms of each element on both sides, to start with an element that appears in only one substance on each side, and to leave hydrogen and oxygen for last when they appear in several places. For combustion, balance carbon first, then hydrogen, and oxygen last, because oxygen appears in two products and in the reactant. If you reach a fraction, multiply the whole equation by the smallest number that clears it. At the end, recount every element on both sides.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Balance Al + HCl -> AlCl3 + H2.**
1. Chlorine is 1 on the left and 3 on the right, so put 3 in front of HCl, which gives 3 H on the left. Then 3 H on the left needs 3 / 2 H2 on the right, which is a fraction.
2. To clear the fraction, double everything: 2Al + 6HCl -> 2AlCl3 + 3H2.
3. Recount: 2 Al, 6 H and 6 Cl on each side, so the equation is balanced. It is a single replacement.

**Example 2: Balance the combustion CH4 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O.**
1. Carbon is 1 on each side already. Hydrogen is 4 on the left, and each water has 2, so use 2 H2O.
2. Count oxygen on the right: 2 in CO2 plus 2 in the two waters, which is 4 in total.
3. Four oxygen atoms need 2 O2, so the balanced equation is CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O.

**Example 3: Classify 2KClO3 -> 2KCl + 3O2.**
1. There is only one reactant, and it breaks into two simpler substances, a salt and oxygen gas.
2. A single reactant forming several products is the pattern AB -> A + B.
3. This is a decomposition reaction, and it needs heat. Check: 2 K, 2 Cl and 6 O on each side.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is to change a subscript in order to make the atoms match, which changes the substance and makes the equation meaningless. Another is to balance oxygen too early in a combustion, because oxygen appears in more than one product and the count has to be done after carbon and hydrogen are fixed. Students also leave a fraction in the equation. A fraction can be acceptable in some energy calculations, but in an ordinary equation it should be cleared by multiplying through, so that the coefficients are the smallest whole numbers.
