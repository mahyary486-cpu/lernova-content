# Types of fossils

Fossils come in several types, depending on whether they are the original body, a copy of the body in stone, or only a trace of what the animal did.

## Key ideas

- **Original remains.** Sometimes the actual body or its parts are kept with little change. Examples are insects trapped in amber (hardened tree resin), animals frozen in ice, and animals kept in tar or very dry places. Woolly mammoths found frozen in Siberia still have skin and hair.
- **Petrified (mineralized) fossils.** Minerals replace the original material, often particle by particle. Petrified wood is a well-known case: a tree trunk turns into stone but keeps the pattern of its rings.
- **Molds.** A mold is a hollow shape left in rock after the original body dissolves or decays. It shows the outside shape of the shell or bone.
- **Casts.** A cast forms when a mold fills with minerals or sediment that harden. The cast has the same shape as the original organism. A mold and its cast are like a hole and the filling that fits it.
- **Carbon films (impressions).** A thin film of carbon is left when the other chemicals in a leaf or small animal escape. The result is a dark outline in the rock. Fern leaves are often preserved this way.
- **Trace fossils.** These show the activity of a living thing, not its body. Examples are footprints, tracks, burrows, nests, eggs and coprolites (fossil droppings). They show how the animal moved, lived or fed.
- Each type needs different conditions. For example, amber needs resin from trees, and carbon films need fine mud.

## Examples

**Example 1: Insect in amber.** A fly lands on sticky resin on a pine trunk and gets trapped. More resin covers it, and over millions of years the resin hardens into amber. The fly's wings and legs are seen in great detail. This is an original-remains fossil because the animal's own body is kept, sealed from air and bacteria.

**Example 2: Mold and cast of a shell.** A shell is buried in mud. The mud hardens into rock, and groundwater later dissolves the shell, leaving a hollow in the rock. This hollow is the mold. If minerals then fill the hollow and harden, the new stone object has the same outside shape as the shell. This is the cast. A scientist can use the mold to learn the pattern on the shell's outside and the cast to learn its shape.

**Example 3: Dinosaur footprints.** A dinosaur walks over soft, wet mud at the edge of a lake. The footprints dry and are then covered by sand, which hardens into rock. The footprints are trace fossils. If a line of prints is found, scientists can see whether the animal walked on two legs or four, and a wider spacing between prints shows it was moving faster. The prints give information that bones alone may not give. No body part of the animal is needed.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up a mold and a cast. The mold is the empty space, and the cast is the filling.
- Thinking a footprint or a burrow is a body fossil. It is a trace fossil, which shows behavior.
- Thinking petrified wood is still wood. Its material has been replaced by minerals, so it is stone.
- Thinking original remains are common. They are rare and need special conditions such as freezing or resin.

## Remember

- Fossil types: original remains, petrified, molds and casts, carbon films, and trace fossils.
- Trace fossils record what an animal did, while the other types record what it looked like.
