# Chemical Equilibrium and the Equilibrium Constant

Many reactions do not go to completion. If you place N2O4 gas in a closed flask, some of it turns into NO2, but some N2O4 always remains, and the mixture stops changing. It seems that nothing is happening, but the reaction has not stopped. Forward and reverse reactions go on at the same rate, so each substance is formed as fast as it is used up. This is dynamic equilibrium, and the equilibrium constant is the number that describes where it lies.

## Key ideas

A system at equilibrium has a forward rate equal to the reverse rate, so the concentrations stay constant, although both reactions continue. This happens only in a closed system, where nothing can escape. The reaction can be approached from either side, and the same equilibrium mixture is reached from the reactants or from the products.

For a general reaction a A + b B <-> c C + d D, the equilibrium constant is K = [C]^c [D]^d / ([A]^a [B]^b). The concentrations of the products are on top, the concentrations of the reactants are at the bottom, and every concentration is raised to the power of its coefficient. Pure solids and pure liquids are left out, because their concentrations do not change. The value of K depends only on the temperature, and not on the starting amounts, on the volume, or on a catalyst. A catalyst makes equilibrium come sooner, but it does not change where it lies.

The size of K tells which side is favored. If K is much larger than 1, the products are favored at equilibrium, and the reaction goes almost to completion. If K is much smaller than 1, the reactants are favored, and very little product forms. If K is near 1, there are significant amounts of both. The value of K says nothing about speed.

The reaction quotient Q is calculated in the same way as K, but with the concentrations at any moment. Comparing Q with K tells the direction of the change. If Q is smaller than K, there are too many reactants, and the reaction proceeds in the forward direction. If Q is larger than K, there are too many products, and it proceeds in reverse. If Q equals K, the system is at equilibrium. This idea is the basis of Le Chatelier's principle in the next lesson.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Writing K.** Write the expression of K for N2 + 3 H2 <-> 2 NH3.
1. The product is ammonia, so [NH3] goes on top, raised to the power 2.
2. The reactants go below: [N2] with the power 1, and [H2] with the power 3.
3. K = [NH3]^2 / ([N2][H2]^3).

**Example 2: Calculating K.** For N2O4 <-> 2 NO2, the equilibrium concentrations are [N2O4] = 0.40 M and [NO2] = 0.20 M.
1. K = [NO2]^2 / [N2O4].
2. K = (0.20)^2 / 0.40 = 0.04 / 0.40 = 0.10.
3. Since K is less than 1, the reactant is favored.

**Example 3: Using the reaction quotient.** For the same reaction, K = 0.10 and a mixture has Q = 0.50.
1. Q is greater than K, so there are too many products.
2. The reaction shifts to the left, which is the reverse direction, until Q falls to 0.10.

## Common mistakes

A common error is to include solids and pure liquids in the expression of K. Another is to forget the powers that come from the coefficients, or to put the products at the bottom. Students also think that a catalyst changes K, but K changes only with temperature. Finally, do not mistake a large K for a fast reaction, because the equilibrium constant describes the position of the equilibrium and not the time needed to reach it.
