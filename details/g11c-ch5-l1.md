# Rates of Reaction and Collision Theory

Food spoils slowly in a refrigerator and quickly on a hot day, a log burns faster when it is split into small pieces, and a medicine works faster as a powder than as a large tablet. All these observations concern the rate of a reaction, which is how fast the reactants are used up or the products formed. Understanding what controls the rate lets us speed up useful reactions, such as industrial synthesis, and slow down harmful ones, such as corrosion and decay.

## Key ideas

The rate of a reaction is the change in the concentration of a reactant or a product per unit of time. It is usually written in mol/(L s), or M/s. Because a reactant is used up, its concentration falls, so the rate is calculated as rate = -delta[A] / delta t for a reactant, and the minus sign makes the rate positive. The square brackets mean the concentration in mol/L.

Collision theory explains what controls the rate. For two particles to react, they must collide, and the collision must meet two conditions: it must have enough energy, which is at least the activation energy, and the particles must meet in the right orientation. Most collisions fail one of these tests, which is why most collisions in a gas do not lead to a reaction.

Four factors increase the rate, and each can be explained by the theory. A higher concentration, or a higher pressure for gases, means more particles per unit of volume, so there are more collisions each second. A higher temperature makes the particles move faster, so there are more collisions, and a much larger share of them has the required energy, and this second effect is the larger one. As a rough rule, a rise of 10 degrees C doubles the rate. A larger surface area, such as a powder instead of a lump, exposes more particles to collisions. A catalyst provides a different path with a lower activation energy, so a larger part of the collisions succeeds.

A catalyst is not used up in the reaction, and it can be recovered unchanged at the end. It lowers the activation energy of the forward and the reverse reaction equally, so it makes a reaction faster without changing delta H, and it does not change the final equilibrium. Enzymes are the catalysts of living cells, and the catalytic converter of a car is a familiar example of an industrial catalyst.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Average rate.** The concentration of A falls from 0.80 M to 0.50 M in 30 s.
1. The change in concentration is 0.50 - 0.80 = -0.30 M.
2. rate = -delta[A] / delta t = 0.30 M / 30 s = 0.010 M/s.

**Example 2: Effect of temperature.** The rate of a reaction doubles for every 10 degrees C. By how much does it increase when the temperature rises by 30 degrees C?
1. A rise of 30 degrees C is three steps of 10 degrees C.
2. The rate doubles three times: 2 x 2 x 2 = 8.
3. The reaction is 8 times faster.

**Example 3: Effect of a catalyst.** What does a catalyst do to the activation energy and to delta H?
1. It provides a path with a lower activation energy, so more collisions are successful.
2. The starting and final energies are unchanged, so delta H stays the same.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that a catalyst is consumed, but it is regenerated and can be used again. Another mistake is to think that a catalyst shifts the equilibrium or increases the yield, whereas it only helps the system to reach equilibrium sooner. Some forget that a rate is a change over a period of time, and so they report a concentration instead of a rate. Finally, remember that a higher temperature works mainly by increasing the share of high-energy collisions, and not only by increasing their number.
