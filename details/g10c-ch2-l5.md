# Metals, Nonmetals and Metalloids

A stair-step line runs across the right side of the periodic table, from boron to astatine. Elements to the left of it are metals, elements to the right are nonmetals, and the elements along the line are metalloids. This division is one of the most useful on the table, because it predicts how an element looks, how it conducts electricity and which kind of ions or bonds it forms.

## Key ideas

Metals make up about 80 percent of the elements. They are shiny, which is called luster, and they are malleable (they can be hammered into sheets) and ductile (they can be drawn into wires). They conduct heat and electricity well, and most of them are solid at room temperature, with mercury as the exception. Chemically, metals lose electrons to form cations, and their oxides are basic.

Nonmetals have the opposite properties. They are dull, they are brittle when solid, and they are poor conductors, so they are insulators. They have lower melting points, and they gain or share electrons when they react. Their oxides are acidic. Bromine is the only liquid nonmetal at room temperature, and several others are gases.

Metalloids, namely boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium, have mixed properties. They look somewhat metallic, but they are brittle, and they are semiconductors, meaning that they conduct electricity better than nonmetals but worse than metals. Unlike metals, their conductivity increases with temperature, and it can be controlled by adding small amounts of other elements. This is why silicon is the basis of electronics.

The properties of metals are explained by metallic bonding. The valence electrons of the metal atoms are free to move among the positive metal ions, so the metal is a lattice of cations in a sea of electrons. The moving electrons carry charge and heat, which explains the conductivity, and they reflect light, which explains the luster. When a metal is hammered, layers of ions slide over each other without breaking the bonding, which explains malleability.

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements, designed to have better properties. Brass is copper and zinc, steel is iron and carbon, and bronze is copper and tin. Alloys are mixtures, so their composition can vary, and it is usually given as a percent by mass.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Mass in an alloy.** Brass is 70.0 percent copper by mass. How much copper is in 250 g of brass?
1. The copper mass is the fraction times the total: 0.700 x 250 g.
2. The mass of copper is 175 g, and the rest, 75 g, is zinc.

**Example 2: Moles of a metal.** How many moles are in 5.40 g of aluminum, which has a molar mass of 26.98 g/mol?
1. Use moles = mass / molar mass.
2. Moles = 5.40 g / 26.98 g/mol = 0.200 mol.

**Example 3: Mass from density.** Iron has a density of 7.87 g/cm^3. What is the mass of 10.0 cm^3 of iron?
1. Use mass = density x volume.
2. Mass = 7.87 g/cm^3 x 10.0 cm^3 = 78.7 g.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that every metal is hard or solid, but mercury is a liquid and sodium is so soft that it can be cut with a knife. Another mistake is to classify aluminum as a metalloid, since it is next to the staircase, but it is a metal. Hydrogen is not a metal, even though it sits in group 1. Finally, metalloids are semiconductors, and they do not conduct as well as metals.
