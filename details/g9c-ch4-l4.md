# Concentration

A drink can be slightly sweet or very sweet depending on how much sugar is dissolved in it. Concentration puts a number on this idea. It tells how much solute is present in a given amount of solution, and it is essential in chemistry, medicine and cooking, where the right strength of a solution matters. This lesson introduces three common ways of expressing concentration, and a rule for diluting solutions.

## Key ideas

The mass of a solution is the mass of the solute plus the mass of the solvent. This is important for the first measure, the mass percent: mass percent = (mass of solute / mass of solution) x 100%. A solution of 20 g of salt in 80 g of water has a mass of 100 g, and its mass percent is 20 percent. Notice that the denominator is the whole solution and not only the solvent. A second simple measure is grams per liter, the mass of solute divided by the volume of solution in liters, for example 20 g in 0.50 L is 40 g/L.

Chemists prefer to count particles, and they do this with the mole, a unit that stands for a very large fixed number of particles, 6.02 x 10^23. The number of moles is found from the mass and the molar mass, the mass of one mole of the substance in g/mol: n = mass / molar mass. For example, the molar mass of sodium chloride is 58.5 g/mol, so 58.5 g of it is exactly 1 mole.

The most common measure in chemistry is molarity, M, the number of moles of solute per liter of solution: M = n / V, with the volume in liters. A solution with a concentration of 2 mol/L is called 2 molar, written 2 M. If a volume is given in milliliters, divide by 1000 to convert it to liters before calculating.

Often a concentrated solution is made weaker by adding solvent. This is called dilution. The key point is that the number of moles of solute does not change when water is added; only the volume changes. Since n = M V, the moles before and after are equal, which gives the dilution equation: M1 V1 = M2 V2. The volumes can be in any unit, as long as they are the same on both sides. Adding solvent makes the solution more dilute, so the concentration always falls.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Mass percent.** 20 g of salt is dissolved in 80 g of water. Find the mass percent of salt.
1. The mass of the solution is 20 + 80 = 100 g.
2. The mass percent is 20 / 100 x 100% = 20%.

**Example 2: Molarity.** 58.5 g of NaCl (molar mass 58.5 g/mol) is dissolved to make 2.0 L of solution. Find the molarity.
1. The number of moles is n = 58.5 / 58.5 = 1.0 mol.
2. The molarity is M = n / V = 1.0 / 2.0 = 0.50 M.

**Example 3: Dilution.** 100 mL of a 4.0 M solution is diluted with water to a volume of 400 mL. Find the new concentration.
1. Use M1 V1 = M2 V2, so M2 = M1 V1 / V2 = 4.0 x 100 / 400.
2. The new concentration is M2 = 1.0 M, which is four times weaker, as expected, because the volume became four times larger.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to use the mass of the solvent instead of the mass of the solution when finding the mass percent. Another is to use milliliters in the molarity formula, which needs liters. Finally, students think that dilution changes the amount of solute. Only the volume, and so the concentration, changes.
