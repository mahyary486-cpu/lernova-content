# Changes of State

Water can freeze in a freezer, melt on a counter, boil in a pot and condense on a cold glass. Each of these is a change of state, a physical change in which a substance moves from one state of matter to another. The substance itself does not change; only the arrangement and motion of its particles do. This lesson names all of the changes and explains the role of energy in them.

## Key ideas

Six changes of state are important. Melting is the change from solid to liquid, and freezing is the reverse. Vaporization is the change from liquid to gas, and condensation is the reverse, from gas to liquid. Sublimation is the change of a solid directly into a gas without becoming a liquid, as dry ice does, and deposition is the opposite, a gas becoming a solid directly, as frost forms on a cold window.

Vaporization takes two forms. Evaporation happens only at the surface of a liquid and can occur at any temperature, because some particles at the surface have enough energy to escape. Boiling happens throughout the whole liquid, with bubbles of vapor forming inside, and it occurs at a specific temperature, the boiling point. At sea level, water melts at 0 degrees C and boils at 100 degrees C. Pure substances have a fixed melting point and boiling point, which are useful for identification.

Energy is involved in every change of state. To melt a solid or to vaporize a liquid, energy must be absorbed, because the particles must overcome the attractions that hold them together. Melting and vaporization are therefore endothermic, and they take energy from the surroundings. Freezing and condensation are the reverse. The particles come together, the attractions form again, and energy is released into the surroundings. This is why steam burns can be worse than burns from boiling water: when steam condenses on the skin, it releases a large amount of energy.

During all these changes, the identity of the substance does not change. Ice, water and steam are all H2O, with the same molecules. This is why changes of state are physical changes and can be reversed, simply by adding or removing energy.

Evaporation also explains a familiar effect. The particles that escape from a liquid are the ones with the highest energy. The particles that remain have a lower average kinetic energy, so the liquid cools down. This is how sweat cools the body.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Naming the change.** Dry ice turns directly into carbon dioxide gas. What is the change called?
1. The solid becomes a gas without passing through the liquid state.
2. This change is called sublimation.

**Example 2: A cold glass.** Droplets of water appear on the outside of a glass of cold water. Explain what happens.
1. Water vapor in the air touches the cold surface of the glass and loses energy.
2. The gas changes into a liquid. This change is condensation, and the water did not come from inside the glass.

**Example 3: Evaporation cooling.** Why does sweat cool you?
1. The fastest, most energetic particles leave the liquid by evaporation.
2. The average kinetic energy of the particles that remain is lower, so the temperature of the sweat and the skin falls.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to think that evaporation only happens at 100 degrees C, when it happens at any temperature, while boiling happens at the boiling point. Another is to think that melting is a chemical change, but no new substance forms. Finally, students forget that freezing releases energy, even though we think of it as cooling.
