# Activation Energy and the Arrhenius Equation

A small change in temperature can change the rate of a reaction by a surprisingly large amount, and the reason is that only the particles with an energy above a certain minimum can react. This minimum is the activation energy, written Ea. The Arrhenius equation gives the exact link between the rate constant, the temperature and the activation energy, and it allows the activation energy to be found from simple measurements.

## Key ideas

The Arrhenius equation is k = A e^(-Ea / RT). Here k is the rate constant, A is a constant related to the frequency and the orientation of collisions, e is the base of natural logarithms, R = 8.31 J/(mol K) and T is the temperature in kelvins. The expression e^(-Ea / RT) is the share of collisions that have at least the activation energy. A higher Ea makes that share smaller, so the reaction is slower. A higher T makes the share larger, so the reaction is faster. A reaction with a very large barrier is practically not happening at room temperature, even when it is strongly exothermic.

Taking the natural logarithm of the equation gives a straight line: ln k = ln A - Ea / (R T). If ln k is plotted against 1 / T, the points lie on a line with a slope of -Ea / R. In this way Ea can be found from the slope of the graph. A steep line means a high activation energy and a rate that is very sensitive to temperature. Using two measurements of k at two temperatures gives the same result without a graph: ln(k2 / k1) = (Ea / R)(1 / T1 - 1 / T2).

An energy profile is a graph that shows the energy of the system during a reaction. It starts at the level of the reactants and climbs to a peak, which is the transition state or activated complex, and then falls to the level of the products. The height from the reactants to the peak is Ea. The difference between the levels of the products and the reactants is delta H. In an exothermic reaction the products lie lower than the reactants, and in an endothermic reaction they lie higher.

A catalyst lowers the peak of the profile, so the activation energy is smaller for both the forward and the reverse reaction. The levels of the reactants and of the products do not move, so delta H does not change.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Finding Ea from a graph.** A plot of ln k against 1 / T has a slope of -6000 K. Take R = 8.31 J/(mol K).
1. The slope is -Ea / R, so Ea = 6000 x 8.31.
2. Ea = 49,860 J/mol.
3. In kilojoules, Ea = 49.9 kJ/mol.

**Example 2: Comparing two reactions.** Which reaction is faster at the same temperature, one with Ea = 40 kJ/mol or one with Ea = 80 kJ/mol?
1. A lower activation energy makes the factor e^(-Ea / RT) larger.
2. The reaction with Ea = 40 kJ/mol has the larger k, so it is faster.

**Example 3: Reading a profile.** In an exothermic reaction, where are the products compared with the reactants?
1. In an exothermic reaction delta H is negative.
2. The products are at a lower energy than the reactants, and the peak between them is the barrier.

## Common mistakes

A common error is to use Celsius in the Arrhenius equation, since T must be in kelvins. Another is to forget that Ea must be in joules per mole when R = 8.31 J/(mol K), so a value in kilojoules must be multiplied by 1000. Students also believe that a catalyst changes delta H or the position of the products on the profile. It only lowers the barrier. Finally, do not forget the minus sign in the slope, or the result for Ea will be negative, which is not possible here.
