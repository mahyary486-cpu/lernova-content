# Limiting Reactants and Percent Yield

Reactions rarely begin with the exact ratio of reactants shown in the equation. A cook who has four slices of bread and one slice of cheese can make only one sandwich with cheese, however much bread there is. The same thing happens in chemistry: one reactant runs out first and stops the reaction. In addition, real experiments always produce a little less product than the calculation promises. This lesson deals with both ideas, and they complete the study of stoichiometry.

## Key ideas

The limiting reactant is the reactant that is completely used up first, and it controls how much product can form. The other reactants, which are not used up completely, are called excess reactants, and some of each is left over when the reaction ends. The limiting reactant is not necessarily the one with the smaller mass, because the equation counts moles, and each substance has a different molar mass. The only reliable comparison is made after applying the mole ratio.

The method is to calculate the mass of product that each reactant could give, using the usual mass-to-mass path. The smaller answer is the correct one, and the reactant that gives it is the limiting reactant. The excess reactant must never be used to calculate the product, because it would predict more product than can possibly form.

The theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product, calculated from the limiting reactant. The actual yield is the amount that is really obtained in the experiment. The percent yield compares them: percent yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) x 100. A percent yield below 100 percent is normal, since reactants may be impure, side reactions may use some of the material, and some product is lost during transfer, filtering and drying. A value above 100 percent is a warning. It usually means that the product is still wet or contains impurities.

The same equation can be rearranged when the percent yield is known. If you know the actual yield and the percent yield, the theoretical yield is the actual yield divided by the fraction, which is the percent yield divided by 100.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Limiting reactant.** For 2Na + Cl2 -> 2NaCl, 4.60 g of Na (22.99 g/mol) and 5.00 g of Cl2 (70.90 g/mol) are mixed. Find the mass of NaCl (58.44 g/mol) that forms.
1. Moles of Na = 4.60 / 22.99 = 0.200 mol, and moles of Cl2 = 5.00 / 70.90 = 0.0705 mol.
2. NaCl from Na: 0.200 mol x (2 / 2) = 0.200 mol. NaCl from Cl2: 0.0705 mol x (2 / 1) = 0.141 mol.
3. Chlorine gives the smaller amount, so Cl2 is the limiting reactant.
4. Mass of NaCl = 0.141 mol x 58.44 g/mol = 8.24 g.

**Example 2: Percent yield.** The theoretical yield is 20.15 g of MgO, and the student collects 17.2 g.
1. Percent yield = actual / theoretical x 100 = 17.2 / 20.15 x 100.
2. The percent yield is 85.4 percent.

**Example 3: Finding the theoretical yield.** The actual yield is 12.6 g and the percent yield is 70.0 percent.
1. Theoretical yield = actual yield / (percent yield / 100).
2. Theoretical yield = 12.6 / 0.700 = 18.0 g.

## Common mistakes

A common error is to choose the reactant with the smaller mass as the limiting one, but the comparison must be made in moles after applying the ratio. Another is to invert the percent yield formula, writing theoretical divided by actual, which would give values above 100 percent for a normal experiment. Students also use the excess reactant to find the amount of product, which overestimates it. Finally, a percent yield above 100 percent should not be accepted as correct. It points to a wet or impure product, or to a mistake in the work.
