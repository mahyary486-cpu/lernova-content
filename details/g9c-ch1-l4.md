# Physical and Chemical Changes

Ice melting and wood burning are both changes, but they are very different kinds of change. After the ice melts you still have water, and you can freeze it again. After the wood burns, you have ash and gases, and you cannot turn them back into wood. Telling these two kinds of change apart is one of the most basic skills in chemistry, and it depends on a single question: did a new substance form?

## Key ideas

In a physical change the form or appearance of a substance changes, but the substance itself stays the same. Melting, freezing, boiling, dissolving, cutting, bending and breaking are physical changes. Every change of state is a physical change, because the particles are the same before and after, and only their arrangement and motion are different. Physical changes can usually be reversed by simple means: water that has been boiled can be condensed, and salt that has been dissolved can be recovered by evaporation.

In a chemical change, also called a chemical reaction, one or more new substances form, with properties that differ from those of the starting substances. Burning, rusting, cooking an egg, digesting food, and the souring of milk are chemical changes. Many of them cannot be reversed easily. The atoms are rearranged into new combinations, and the old substances are used up.

How can we tell that a chemical change took place? There are several signs. A gas may be produced, seen as bubbles. A solid, called a precipitate, may form when two clear liquids are mixed. There may be a change of color, the release of heat or light, or a new odor. These signs are useful clues, but they are not proof on their own. Boiling water also makes bubbles, and mixing paints changes the color, yet both are physical changes. The key is whether a new substance appears.

An important rule applies to all changes, physical and chemical: the law of conservation of mass. In a closed system, the total mass before a change equals the total mass after it. Atoms are rearranged, but they are not created or destroyed. When it seems that mass has disappeared, such as when wood burns and leaves a lighter pile of ash, the missing mass has gone into gases that escaped into the air. Gases have mass too.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Baking soda and vinegar.** Bubbles form when baking soda is mixed with vinegar. What kind of change is it?
1. The bubbles are a gas, carbon dioxide, that was not there before.
2. A new substance has formed, so this is evidence of a chemical change.

**Example 2: Melting ice.** Ice melts into liquid water on a warm table. What kind of change is it?
1. The liquid is still water, made of the same particles as the ice.
2. Only the state has changed, so melting is a physical change, and the water can be frozen to ice again.

**Example 3: Rusting nails.** Rusted nails weigh more than the original nails. Does this break the law of conservation of mass?
1. Iron combines with oxygen from the air, and with water, to form rust.
2. The extra mass came from the oxygen. If we include the oxygen that was used, the total mass before and after is the same, so mass is conserved.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to think that every change of color is a chemical change; mixing blue and yellow paint to make green is physical, because no new substance forms. Another is to think that dissolving sugar in water is a chemical change. The sugar particles spread out among the water particles, and the sugar can be recovered by evaporation. Finally, students often forget that gases have mass, which leads them to think that mass is lost when a candle burns or when bubbles escape.
