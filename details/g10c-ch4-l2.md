# Writing Chemical Formulas

A chemical formula tells us which elements are in a compound and how many atoms or ions of each are present. Writing a formula is the reverse of naming a compound. You begin with the name, work out the ions or the prefixes it describes, and then find the subscripts that make the formula correct. The skill matters because every equation and every calculation in chemistry depends on a correct formula. A single wrong subscript describes a different substance.

## Key ideas

For an ionic compound, the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge, because the compound is neutral. The quickest way to find the subscripts is the criss-cross method. Write the charge of each ion, then let the size of each charge become the subscript of the other ion. Then reduce the subscripts to the lowest whole-number ratio, and do not write a subscript of 1. The reason this works is that a charge of 3+ on one ion needs three of the ions with 1- on the other, so the numbers swap places. Always check the result by adding the charges, because the sum should be zero.

Polyatomic ions are kept together as a single unit. When more than one is needed, put the whole ion in parentheses and write the subscript outside, as in Ca(NO3)2. This shows that the subscript 2 multiplies every atom inside the parentheses. When only one polyatomic ion is needed, parentheses are not used, so sodium hydroxide is NaOH. You must never change a subscript inside the ion, because that would turn it into a different ion.

A Roman numeral in a name gives the charge of the metal, so iron(III) is Fe 3+ and copper(I) is Cu+. Use that charge in the criss-cross method.

Covalent formulas are simpler, because the Greek prefixes give the subscripts directly. Dinitrogen pentoxide is N2O5, with no need for charges.

Reading a formula is the other half of the skill. A subscript multiplies the atom just before it, and a subscript outside parentheses multiplies everything inside them. Adding up the atoms in this way allows you to calculate the formula mass of a compound, which is the sum of the atomic masses of all its atoms.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Write the formula for aluminum oxide.**
1. The ions are Al 3+ and O 2-.
2. In the criss-cross method, aluminum receives the 2 from oxygen, and oxygen receives the 3 from aluminum.
3. The formula is Al2O3. Check: 2 x (+3) + 3 x (-2) = +6 - 6 = 0.

**Example 2: Write the formula for iron(III) hydroxide.**
1. The ions are Fe 3+ and OH-.
2. Three hydroxide ions are needed to balance one Fe 3+, and since hydroxide is a polyatomic ion, it goes in parentheses.
3. The formula is Fe(OH)3.

**Example 3: Find the formula mass of magnesium hydroxide.**
1. The ions are Mg 2+ and OH-, so the formula is Mg(OH)2, with 1 Mg, 2 O and 2 H.
2. Use the atomic masses Mg 24.3, O 16.0 and H 1.0.
3. The mass is 24.3 + 2 x 16.0 + 2 x 1.0 = 24.3 + 32.0 + 2.0 = 58.3 amu.

## Common mistakes

The most frequent error is to change the subscript of a polyatomic ion, writing CaOH2 instead of Ca(OH)2. This happens because students forget that the ion must stay whole. A second error is to forget to reduce the ratio, so that Mg2O2 appears instead of MgO. Another is to use the criss-cross method for covalent compounds, where the prefixes in the name already give the subscripts. Finally, some students ignore the Roman numeral and use the most common charge of the metal, which gives the wrong formula.
