# Writing Chemical Formulas

A chemical formula shows which elements are in a compound and how many atoms or ions of each. Writing a formula is the reverse of naming: start from the name and find the subscripts.

## Key ideas
**Ionic formulas.** The positive and negative charges must add to zero. Write the ion charges, then use the criss-cross method: the number of each charge becomes the subscript of the other ion. Reduce to the lowest whole-number ratio. A subscript of 1 is not written.

**Polyatomic ions.** Keep the ion together. If you need more than one, put the ion in parentheses and write the subscript outside: Ca(NO3)2. Do not use parentheses for a single ion.

**Roman numerals.** The numeral gives the charge of the metal: iron(III) is Fe 3+.

**Covalent formulas.** Prefixes give the subscripts directly: dinitrogen pentoxide is N2O5.

**Formula unit and counting atoms.** A subscript multiplies the atom just before it. A subscript outside parentheses multiplies everything inside.

## Worked examples
**Example 1: aluminum oxide**
1. Ions: Al 3+ and O 2-.
2. Criss-cross: Al gets 2 and O gets 3.
3. Formula: Al2O3. Check: 2 x (+3) + 3 x (-2) = 0.

**Example 2: iron(III) hydroxide**
1. Ions: Fe 3+ and OH-.
2. Three hydroxides are needed, so use parentheses.
3. Formula: Fe(OH)3.

**Example 3: formula mass of magnesium hydroxide**
1. Ions: Mg 2+ and OH-, so the formula is Mg(OH)2.
2. Atoms: 1 Mg, 2 O, 2 H.
3. Mass = 24.3 + 2(16.0) + 2(1.0) = 58.3 amu (atomic masses Mg 24.3, O 16.0, H 1.0).

**Example 4: atoms in Ca3(PO4)2**
1. Ca: 3. P: 2. O: 2 x 4 = 8.
2. Total = 3 + 2 + 8 = 13 atoms.

## Common mistakes
- Changing a subscript of a polyatomic ion, such as writing CaOH2 instead of Ca(OH)2.
- Forgetting to reduce: Mg2O2 must be MgO.
- Using the criss-cross method for covalent compounds, where prefixes are used instead.
- Ignoring the Roman numeral and using the wrong charge.
