# Conservation of Momentum

When two objects interact, they push on each other with equal and opposite forces, and these forces last for the same time. By Newton's third law their impulses are equal and opposite, so one object gains exactly the momentum that the other loses. The momentum is passed from one to the other, and the total does not change. This is the law of conservation of momentum, and it is one of the most reliable rules in all of physics.

## Key ideas

The law says that if the net external force on a system is zero, the total momentum of the system stays constant: p_before = p_after. A system is a group of objects that we choose to study. The forces that the objects exert on each other are internal forces, and they cannot change the total momentum. Only a net external force, such as friction from the ground, can do that. A system with no net external force is called an isolated system. Skaters on very smooth ice and objects in space are good approximations.

For two objects, the law is written m1 v1 + m2 v2 = m1 v1' + m2 v2', where the primes mean after the interaction. A good strategy has four steps. Choose a positive direction. Write the momenta of all objects before the event with their signs. Write the momenta after the event. Set the two totals equal and solve for the unknown.

A system that starts at rest has a total momentum of zero. After an explosion or a push, the pieces must therefore move in opposite directions, so that their momenta add up to zero again. A light piece gets a large velocity and a heavy piece gets a small one. This is why a rifle recoils backward when it fires a bullet, and why an astronaut who throws a tool drifts the other way.

Conservation of momentum also settles an apparent puzzle. A falling ball clearly gains momentum, so is the law broken? No: gravity is an external force on the ball alone. If the system is ball plus Earth, the Earth gains an equal and opposite momentum, and the total is conserved. The Earth is so massive that its change in speed is far too small to notice.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Rifle recoil.** A 4.0 kg rifle fires a 0.010 kg bullet at 400 m/s. Find the recoil speed of the rifle.
1. The total momentum before firing is zero.
2. After firing: 0 = 4.0 x v + 0.010 x 400, so 4.0 v = -4.0.
3. The velocity is v = -1.0 m/s, so the rifle recoils at 1.0 m/s opposite to the bullet.

**Example 2: Colliding carts.** A 1.0 kg cart moves right at 6.0 m/s and meets a 2.0 kg cart moving left at 1.0 m/s. Afterward, the 1.0 kg cart moves left at 1.0 m/s. Find the velocity of the 2.0 kg cart.
1. The momentum before is 1.0 x 6.0 + 2.0 x (-1.0) = 4.0 kg m/s.
2. The momentum after is 1.0 x (-1.0) + 2.0 x v.
3. Setting them equal: 4.0 = -1.0 + 2.0 v, so v = 2.5 m/s to the right.

**Example 3: An astronaut in space.** An 80 kg astronaut at rest throws a 2.0 kg tool at 5.0 m/s. Find the astronaut's velocity.
1. The total momentum before the throw is zero.
2. After the throw: 0 = 80 x v + 2.0 x 5.0, so 80 v = -10.
3. The velocity is v = -0.125 m/s, so the astronaut drifts backward at 0.125 m/s.

## Common mistakes

Students often forget the negative signs for motion in the negative direction, which is the most common source of wrong answers. Another error is to apply the law when a large external force, such as friction, acts during the interaction. Some assume that equal and opposite momenta mean equal speeds, but this is true only when the masses are equal. Finally, be careful to put the velocities from before the event on one side and the velocities from after the event on the other, and never mix them.
