# Free Fall and Motion Graphs

An object that moves under the pull of gravity alone is said to be in free fall. Air resistance is ignored, and the object can be going up, going down, or momentarily at the top. Free fall is the most important example of constant acceleration, and it makes a perfect partner for graphs. A graph gives a picture of the whole motion at once, and with a few simple rules you can read velocity, acceleration and displacement from it without a single equation.

## Key ideas

Near the surface of the Earth, every freely falling object has the same acceleration, g = 9.8 m/s^2, directed downward. This does not depend on the mass: a heavy stone and a light stone dropped together reach the ground together when air resistance is negligible. The acceleration is the same on the way up, at the very top, and on the way down. This is why a thrown ball slows going up, stops for an instant, and then speeds up going down; all three stages come from a single downward acceleration.

To solve free-fall problems, use the constant-acceleration equations with a = -g if you choose up as positive. For an object dropped from rest, the formulas simplify to v = gt and d = (1/2) g t^2, where d is the distance fallen. At the highest point of a throw the velocity is zero but the acceleration is still g, a fact that students often find hard to accept. The time to rise to the top equals the time to fall back to the launch height, so the total time in the air is twice the time to the top.

Graphs show the same information in a different form. On a position-time graph, the slope equals the velocity: a straight line means constant velocity, a steeper line means a larger velocity, and a curve means that the velocity is changing. On a velocity-time graph, the slope equals the acceleration, so a straight sloped line means constant acceleration. The area between the velocity line and the time axis equals the displacement, because velocity multiplied by time gives distance traveled. Area below the axis counts as negative displacement.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Dropped stone.** A stone is dropped from rest. What are its velocity and its fall distance after 3.0 s?
1. For a drop from rest, v = gt = 9.8 x 3.0 = 29.4 m/s downward, which is about 29 m/s.
2. The distance fallen is d = (1/2) g t^2 = 0.5 x 9.8 x 9.0 = 44.1 m.
3. The stone has gained about 10 m/s of speed for every second of fall.

**Example 2: Thrown upward.** A ball is thrown straight up at 19.6 m/s. Find the time to the top, the maximum height and the total time in the air.
1. At the top the velocity is zero, so 0 = 19.6 - 9.8 t, which gives t = 2.0 s.
2. The maximum height comes from v^2 = v0^2 - 2 g h, so h = v0^2 / (2 g) = 384.16 / 19.6 = 19.6 m.
3. The fall takes the same 2.0 s as the rise, so the total time in the air is 4.0 s.

**Example 3: Reading a graph.** On a velocity-time graph, the velocity of a cart rises along a straight line from 0 to 10 m/s in 4.0 s. Find the acceleration and the displacement.
1. The slope of the line is the acceleration: a = 10 / 4.0 = 2.5 m/s^2.
2. The area under the line is a triangle with base 4.0 s and height 10 m/s.
3. The displacement is (1/2) x 4.0 x 10 = 20 m.

## Common mistakes

Many students believe that heavier objects fall faster, but without air resistance the mass does not matter. Another mistake is to set a = 0 at the top of the flight because the velocity is zero there; the acceleration is still 9.8 m/s^2 downward. Students also mix up the two graph rules, using the area for acceleration or the slope for displacement; remember that slope means "rate of change" and area means "accumulated amount". Finally, when solving d = (1/2) g t^2 for the time, do not forget the square root at the end.
