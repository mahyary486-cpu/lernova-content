# Functional Groups and Their Compounds

The functional group tells you how a compound will behave, whatever the length of its carbon chain. A small group of atoms, attached to a hydrocarbon chain, gives the molecule its typical reactions and many of its physical properties. This is why chemists can organize millions of organic compounds into a handful of families, and predict the behavior of a new compound from the group that it contains.

## Key ideas

The main groups are these. An alcohol contains the -OH group attached to a carbon chain. An aldehyde has the -CHO group at the end of a chain, and a ketone has a C=O group in the middle of a chain. A carboxylic acid has the -COOH group. An ester has the -COO- link between two carbon chains, and an amine has the -NH2 group. In the name, the ending shows the family: -ol for alcohols, -al for aldehydes, -one for ketones, -oic acid for acids and -oate for esters.

Alcohols can be oxidized by an oxidizing agent such as acidified dichromate. A primary alcohol is oxidized first to an aldehyde, and then, if the oxidation continues, to a carboxylic acid. A secondary alcohol is oxidized to a ketone, which cannot be oxidized further under the same conditions. A tertiary alcohol, which has no hydrogen on the carbon carrying the -OH group, resists oxidation. The reason is that the oxidation needs a hydrogen atom to be removed from that carbon.

Alcohols have much higher boiling points than hydrocarbons of similar size, because the -OH groups form hydrogen bonds between molecules. Ethanol boils at 78 degrees C, while propane is a gas at room temperature. The small alcohols also dissolve in water, for the same reason. Carboxylic acids are weak acids, since they ionize only partly in water, and they also form hydrogen bonds.

An ester forms when a carboxylic acid reacts with an alcohol in the presence of a strong acid catalyst: acid + alcohol <-> ester + water. The reaction is reversible, and it is called esterification. To name the ester, the alcohol gives the first word, and the acid gives the second word, so that methanol and ethanoic acid give methyl ethanoate. Many esters have fruity smells, and they are used in perfumes and in flavorings.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Identify the functional group in CH3CH2CHO.**
1. The formula ends in -CHO, which is a carbonyl group at the end of the chain.
2. The compound is an aldehyde, and with three carbons its name is propanal.

**Example 2: What is formed when ethanol is fully oxidized?**
1. Ethanol is a primary alcohol, so it is first oxidized to ethanal, which is an aldehyde.
2. With a strong oxidizing agent and enough time, ethanal is oxidized further.
3. The final product is ethanoic acid, CH3COOH.

**Example 3: What ester forms from methanol and ethanoic acid?**
1. The alcohol part, methyl, gives the first word, and the acid part, ethanoate, gives the second word.
2. The product is methyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH3.
3. Water is also formed: CH3COOH + CH3OH <-> CH3COOCH3 + H2O.

## Common mistakes

Students often confuse aldehydes and ketones, but the aldehyde group is at the end of the chain, and the ketone group is inside it. Another error is to forget the water produced in esterification, which leaves the equation unbalanced. The third mistake is to think that tertiary alcohols are oxidized easily, while they have no hydrogen on the carbon of the -OH group and so resist oxidation. A useful habit is to check the carbon that holds the -OH group before deciding what the product of oxidation will be.
