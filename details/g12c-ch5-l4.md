# Atmospheric Chemistry: Pollution, the Greenhouse Effect and Ozone

Cars, power stations and factories release gases that cause smog, acid rain and global warming. The atmosphere is a huge chemical system, and human activities have changed its composition in a few generations. Understanding the reactions involved explains why these problems occur and what can be done about them. This lesson covers three separate problems, and keeping them apart is one of its main tasks.

## Key ideas

Carbon monoxide forms from incomplete combustion. It is dangerous because it binds to hemoglobin much more strongly than oxygen does, and so it reduces the oxygen that the blood can carry. Nitrogen oxides, NOx, form when nitrogen and oxygen in the air react at the high temperatures of an engine, and they take part in the formation of photochemical smog. Sulfur dioxide comes from burning fuels that contain sulfur. It is oxidized in the air to sulfur trioxide, and dissolves in rain to give sulfuric acid, so SO2 + H2O gives H2SO3, and SO3 + H2O gives H2SO4. This is acid rain. Nitrogen oxides contribute to it in the same way. Acid rain damages forests, lakes, buildings and statues made of limestone.

The greenhouse effect is a natural process. Sunlight warms the Earth, and the Earth radiates infrared energy back toward space. Greenhouse gases, such as carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and water vapor, absorb part of this infrared and send it back, which keeps the planet warm enough for life. The problem is the enhanced greenhouse effect, because burning fossil fuels and clearing forests raise the concentration of CO2, and the extra absorption warms the climate.

Ozone, O3, in the stratosphere absorbs most of the harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun. Chlorofluorocarbons, CFCs, which were used in refrigerators and sprays, are stable and rise to the stratosphere. There, UV light splits off chlorine atoms. A chlorine atom acts as a catalyst, since it destroys ozone and is regenerated, so one atom can destroy many thousands of ozone molecules. The international ban on CFCs has slowed the damage. The ozone hole and the greenhouse effect are different problems with different causes.

A catalytic converter in a car exhaust uses metals such as platinum and rhodium to convert harmful gases into less harmful ones. Carbon monoxide and hydrocarbons are oxidized to CO2 and water, and nitrogen oxides are reduced to nitrogen.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Acid rain.** Write the reaction of sulfur trioxide with water.
1. Sulfur trioxide is the oxide of a nonmetal, and it combines with water to form an acid.
2. The equation is SO3 + H2O -> H2SO4.
3. The product is sulfuric acid, which is a strong acid and lowers the pH of rain.

**Example 2: Carbon dioxide from combustion.** How many moles of CO2 are produced by burning 1 mol of octane, C8H18?
1. The balanced equation is C8H18 + 12.5 O2 -> 8 CO2 + 9 H2O, or 2 C8H18 + 25 O2 -> 16 CO2 + 18 H2O.
2. The ratio of CO2 to octane is 8 to 1.
3. Burning 1 mol of octane gives 8 mol of CO2, which is 352 g.

**Example 3: Why do CFCs damage the ozone layer?**
1. UV light in the stratosphere splits a CFC molecule and releases a chlorine atom.
2. The chlorine atom reacts with ozone and is then released again, so it acts as a catalyst.
3. Each chlorine atom can therefore destroy many ozone molecules.

## Common mistakes

Students often confuse the greenhouse effect with the ozone hole, but the first is about infrared absorption, and the second is about ultraviolet protection. Another error is to say that CO2 is the main cause of acid rain, when sulfur and nitrogen oxides are the main cause. The third mistake is to forget that chlorine acts as a catalyst, and is not used up.
