# Power and Efficiency

Two machines can do the same work, but one may do it faster. Power tells how fast work is done, and efficiency tells how much of the input energy becomes useful output.

## Key ideas
- **Power: P = W / t**, measured in watts. 1 W = 1 J/s. A kilowatt is 1000 W.
- For a constant force acting along the motion at constant speed: **P = F v**.
- 1 horsepower is about 746 W.
- A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is a unit of energy: 1 kWh = 3.6 x 10^6 J.
- **Efficiency = useful output / total input x 100 percent.** It can be found with energy or with power.
- Efficiency is always less than 100 percent for real machines because some energy is wasted, usually as heat.

## Worked examples
**Example 1: Lifting.** A motor lifts a 20 kg crate 3 m in 4 s at constant speed. Use g = 9.8 m/s^2.
1. Work = m g h = 20 x 9.8 x 3 = 588 J.
2. P = W / t = 588 / 4 = 147 W.

**Example 2: P = F v.** A car engine provides 1800 N of forward force at a steady 30 m/s.
1. P = F v = 1800 x 30.
2. P = 54000 W = 54 kW.

**Example 3: Efficiency and energy cost.** A machine uses 2000 J of input energy and delivers 1500 J of useful work.
1. Efficiency = 1500 / 2000 = 0.75 = 75 percent.
2. Wasted energy = 2000 - 1500 = 500 J.
3. A 100 W bulb left on for 10 hours uses 100 W x 10 h = 1000 Wh = 1 kWh, which is 3.6 million joules.

## Common mistakes
- Confusing work (joules) with power (watts).
- Calling the kilowatt-hour a unit of power. It is a unit of energy.
- Dividing output by input in the wrong order, giving efficiency above 100 percent.
- Forgetting to convert minutes or hours to seconds when using P = W / t in watts.
