# Power and Efficiency

Two machines can do exactly the same work, but one may finish much sooner. A small motor and a large motor can both lift a load through 10 m, but the large one does it in less time. Power tells how fast work is done. A second question is just as important: how much of the energy supplied to a machine ends up doing the job we want? That is the question answered by efficiency. Together these two ideas describe how real machines perform.

## Key ideas

Power is the rate of doing work, P = W / t. The SI unit is the watt (W), where 1 W = 1 J/s. Larger units are common: 1 kilowatt (kW) equals 1000 W. An older unit still used for engines is the horsepower, about 746 W. A power of 100 W means that 100 joules of energy are transferred every second. Power measures how quickly energy changes form, so it can also be written as energy divided by time.

When a constant force acts along the direction of motion at a steady speed, the power can be found from P = F v. This is the form used for vehicles: the power of the engine equals the driving force times the speed. At a given power, a higher speed means a smaller force, which explains why a car or a cyclist feels the pull of a hill most strongly when moving slowly in a high gear.

Electric bills use a unit of energy, not power: the kilowatt-hour (kWh). One kilowatt-hour is the energy used by a 1 kW device running for one hour, and 1 kWh = 3.6 x 10^6 J. A 100 W bulb that is left on for 10 hours uses 100 W x 10 h = 1000 Wh = 1 kWh, which is 3.6 million joules.

Efficiency is the fraction of the input energy that becomes useful output: efficiency = (useful output / total input) x 100 percent. It can be calculated with energy or with power, as long as both are of the same kind. No real machine reaches 100 percent efficiency, because some energy always escapes, usually as heat caused by friction. A value above 100 percent means that the division was done in the wrong order, or that the numbers are wrong.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Lifting a crate.** A motor lifts a 20 kg crate through 3.0 m in 4.0 s at constant speed. Use g = 9.8 m/s^2. Find the power.
1. The work done is W = m g h = 20 x 9.8 x 3.0 = 588 J.
2. The power is P = W / t = 588 / 4.0.
3. The motor delivers 147 W to the crate.

**Example 2: Power from force and speed.** A car engine provides a forward force of 1800 N at a steady 30 m/s. Find the power.
1. Use P = F v = 1800 x 30.
2. The result is P = 54 000 W.
3. In kilowatts this is 54 kW, or about 72 horsepower.

**Example 3: Efficiency.** A machine takes in 2000 J of energy and delivers 1500 J of useful work. Find the efficiency and the wasted energy.
1. Efficiency = 1500 / 2000 = 0.75, which is 75 percent.
2. The wasted energy is 2000 - 1500 = 500 J.
3. This 500 J leaves the machine as heat and sound.

## Common mistakes

The commonest mistake is to confuse work, measured in joules, with power, measured in watts; power includes the time. Another is to call the kilowatt-hour a unit of power, when it is a unit of energy. Students also divide the input by the output when finding efficiency, which gives a value above 100 percent. Finally, remember to convert minutes and hours to seconds when using P = W / t in watts, or the answer will be too small by a factor of 60 or 3600.
