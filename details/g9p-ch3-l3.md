# Newton's Second Law: F = ma

Newton's first law says that a net force changes motion. The second law tells exactly how much it changes. It explains why a shopping cart speeds up quickly with a small push while a loaded truck needs a very large force to do the same.

## Key ideas

Newton's second law states that the acceleration of an object is equal to the net force divided by the mass: a = F / m. It is often written in the form F = ma, where F is the net force in newtons, m is the mass in kilograms and a is the acceleration in meters per second squared. The acceleration is always in the same direction as the net force.

The law shows how force and mass affect acceleration. If the mass stays the same, a bigger force gives a bigger acceleration: doubling the force doubles the acceleration. If the force stays the same, a bigger mass gives a smaller acceleration: doubling the mass halves the acceleration. This is why a heavy object is harder to speed up than a light one.

The newton is defined by this law. One newton is the net force that gives a 1 kg object an acceleration of 1 m/s^2, so 1 N = 1 kg x m/s^2.

It is very important to use the net force in the formula. If several forces act on an object, first add them with their directions, and then divide the total by the mass. The second law can be combined with the equations of motion from the previous chapter. Once you find the acceleration, you can use v = v_initial + a x t to find the speed after some time.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Finding acceleration.** A 10 kg object has a net force of 50 N acting on it. What is its acceleration?
1. Use a = F / m.
2. Substitute: a = 50 / 10.
3. The acceleration is 5 m/s^2 in the direction of the force.

**Example 2: Finding force.** What net force gives a 1200 kg car an acceleration of 2.5 m/s^2?
1. Use F = m x a.
2. Substitute: F = 1200 x 2.5.
3. The net force is 3000 N.

**Example 3: Changing the mass.** A force of 20 N acts on a 4 kg cart and then on an 8 kg cart. Compare the accelerations.
1. For the first cart, a = 20 / 4 = 5 m/s^2.
2. For the second cart, a = 20 / 8 = 2.5 m/s^2.
3. Doubling the mass reduced the acceleration to half of its value.

## Common mistakes

A common error is to use the weight of an object instead of its mass in F = ma. The mass must be in kilograms. Another is to use only one of the forces instead of the net force, especially when friction is also present. Finally, students sometimes think that a bigger force always produces a bigger speed. A force produces an acceleration, which is a change in speed, not the speed itself.
