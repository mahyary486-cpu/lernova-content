# Density

Why does a huge ship float while a small stone sinks to the bottom? The answer is not mass alone, but density. Density compares how much mass is packed into a given volume, and it is one of the most useful physical properties in chemistry, because each pure substance has its own density that does not depend on how much of it you have. It can be used to identify materials, to predict what will float and to design everything from boats to balloons.

## Key ideas

Density is defined as mass divided by volume: D = m / V. A material with a large mass in a small volume has a high density, and a material with a small mass in a large volume has a low density. Lead is much denser than wood, which is why a small piece of lead feels heavy. The units depend on the measurements. For solids and liquids, the most common unit is grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm^3), which is the same as grams per milliliter (g/mL), because 1 mL equals 1 cm^3. In SI units, density is in kilograms per cubic meter, and 1 g/cm^3 equals 1000 kg/m^3.

The formula can be rearranged to find other quantities. If you know the density and the volume, the mass is m = D x V. If you know the density and the mass, the volume is V = m / D. Writing the formula in a triangle or checking the units, which show whether you should multiply or divide, helps avoid confusion.

To find the density, you need both the mass and the volume. Mass is measured with a balance. The volume of a regular solid, like a box, is length x width x height. For an irregular solid, such as a stone, use water displacement: place the solid in a graduated cylinder with water, and the rise in the water level equals the volume of the solid. The solid must sink completely and must not dissolve.

Density is a physical property of the substance, so it does not change with the size of the sample. A small gold ring and a large gold bar have the same density, about 19.3 g/cm^3. This is why density helps us identify materials. It also decides floating and sinking. An object floats in a liquid if it is less dense than the liquid, and sinks if it is denser. Water has a density of 1.0 g/cm^3, so ice (0.92 g/cm^3) floats on it and iron (7.9 g/cm^3) sinks. A steel ship floats because its hollow shape makes its average density lower than that of water.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Finding density.** A piece of metal has a mass of 54 g and a volume of 20 cm^3. Find its density.
1. Use D = m / V = 54 / 20.
2. The density is D = 2.7 g/cm^3, which matches aluminum.

**Example 2: Finding mass.** A liquid has a density of 0.80 g/cm^3. Find the mass of 50 cm^3 of it.
1. Rearrange the formula: m = D x V = 0.80 x 50.
2. The mass is m = 40 g. This liquid is less dense than water, so it would float on water.

**Example 3: Water displacement.** A stone with a mass of 45 g is placed in a cylinder, and the water level rises from 40 mL to 55 mL. Find the density of the stone.
1. The volume of the stone is 55 - 40 = 15 mL, which equals 15 cm^3.
2. The density is D = 45 / 15 = 3.0 g/cm^3. Since this is more than 1.0 g/cm^3, the stone sinks.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to divide volume by mass instead of mass by volume, which gives the wrong units. Another is to think that a bigger piece of the same material has a larger density; both mass and volume grow in the same proportion, so the density is unchanged. Finally, students forget that 1 mL equals 1 cm^3, and they spend time converting when no conversion is needed.
