# Interference and Young's Double Slit

The shimmering colors of a soap bubble or an oil film on a road come from interference of light waves reflected from the front and back surfaces of the thin film. Interference is the clearest proof that light is a wave, because only waves can add up to give more light in some places and darkness in others. In 1801 Thomas Young demonstrated it with a simple experiment using two narrow slits, and the experiment still serves as the standard way to measure the wavelength of light.

## Key ideas

When two waves meet, they add. If a crest meets a crest, they reinforce each other, which is constructive interference. If a crest meets a trough, they cancel, which is destructive interference. For two light sources to give a stable pattern, they must be coherent, that is, they must keep a constant phase relationship. Two separate bulbs are not coherent, so in practice a single source illuminates both slits, and the slits act as two coherent sources.

In Young's experiment, light passes through two narrow slits a distance d apart, and bright and dark fringes appear on a screen far away. At a given angle theta, the waves from the two slits travel different distances, and the difference in path is d sin(theta). Bright fringes appear where this path difference is a whole number of wavelengths: d sin(theta) = m lambda, with m = 0, 1, 2 and so on. Dark fringes appear where it is a half-integer number of wavelengths: d sin(theta) = (m + 1/2) lambda.

For small angles and a screen at a distance L, these relations simplify to an even spacing of the fringes: delta y = lambda L / d. The spacing is larger for a longer wavelength, a more distant screen and a smaller slit separation. Red light gives wider fringes than blue light, and this is how the wavelength of light is measured.

Interference also happens in thin films. When light reflects from a surface where the second medium has a higher refractive index, the reflected wave is shifted by 180 degrees in phase, which is like a half wavelength. The two reflections from a thin film combine according to the thickness and this phase change, so that some colors cancel and others reinforce. Anti-reflection coatings on lenses use a film of the right thickness so that the reflected light cancels and more light enters the lens.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Fringe spacing.** Two slits 0.20 mm apart are lit by light of wavelength 600 nm, and the screen is 2.0 m away. Find the fringe spacing.
1. Convert the units: d = 2.0 x 10^-4 m and lambda = 6.0 x 10^-7 m.
2. Use delta y = lambda L / d = 6.0 x 10^-7 x 2.0 / 2.0 x 10^-4.
3. The spacing is delta y = 6.0 x 10^-3 m, or 6.0 mm.

**Example 2: Finding the wavelength.** The fringe spacing is 1.2 mm when d = 0.50 mm and L = 1.0 m. Find the wavelength.
1. Use lambda = delta y d / L = 1.2 x 10^-3 x 5.0 x 10^-4 / 1.0.
2. The wavelength is 6.0 x 10^-7 m.
3. This is 600 nm, orange-red light.

**Example 3: A dark fringe.** Light of 600 nm is used. Find the path difference at the first dark fringe.
1. Dark fringes need a half-integer number of wavelengths, and the first one has m = 0.
2. The path difference is lambda / 2 = 300 nm.
3. The waves from the two slits arrive exactly out of phase and cancel.

## Common mistakes

Students often forget to convert millimeters and nanometers into meters, which is the main source of errors in this topic. Another mistake is using a whole wavelength for dark fringes, when the path difference must be a half-integer number of wavelengths. A third error is to use two separate lamps as sources, which are not coherent. Finally, remember that red light produces wider fringes than violet light, because its wavelength is longer.
