# Subatomic Particles and the Nuclear Atom

Atoms are made of three subatomic particles: protons, neutrons and electrons. How scientists discovered them is a good example of how evidence changes a model, because each new experiment showed that the previous picture of the atom was incomplete. In this lesson we describe the particles, then follow the two experiments that led to the nuclear atom.

## Key ideas

The proton has a charge of +1 (+1.602 x 10^-19 C) and a mass of 1.673 x 10^-27 kg, which is about 1 atomic mass unit (amu). The neutron has no charge and almost the same mass, 1.675 x 10^-27 kg, or about 1 amu. Both are found in the nucleus. The electron has a charge of -1 and a mass of 9.11 x 10^-31 kg, about 1/1836 of an amu, and it is found outside the nucleus. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the charges cancel.

In 1897 J. J. Thomson studied cathode rays, beams that travel inside a glass tube from which most of the air has been removed. He found that the beams were bent away from a negative plate and toward a positive plate by an electric field, so they must be made of negatively charged particles. These particles, now called electrons, were part of every atom he tested. Since atoms are neutral, he proposed the plum pudding model, in which electrons are embedded in a sphere of positive charge, like raisins in a pudding.

In 1911 Ernest Rutherford's team fired positively charged alpha particles at a very thin gold foil. According to the plum pudding model, the particles should pass through with only slight bending. Most did go straight through, but a small number were deflected at large angles, and a few even bounced almost straight back. Rutherford concluded that the atom is mostly empty space, with a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus that holds nearly all the mass. Only a direct approach to such a concentrated charge could turn an alpha particle back. This is the nuclear atom.

The scales involved are striking. An atom is about 1.0 x 10^-10 m across, and its nucleus is about 1.0 x 10^-15 m, so the atom is about 100 000 times wider than the nucleus. The electrons make up a negligible part of the mass, so the mass of an atom is practically the mass of its protons and neutrons.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Nuclear charge.** A nucleus has 8 protons and 9 neutrons. Find its charge.
1. Only protons carry charge, and the neutrons contribute nothing.
2. The charge is 8 x 1.602 x 10^-19 C.
3. The charge is 1.28 x 10^-18 C, equal to +8 in units of the elementary charge.

**Example 2: Size ratio.** How many times wider than its nucleus is an atom?
1. Divide the atom's diameter by the nucleus's diameter.
2. 1.0 x 10^-10 m / 1.0 x 10^-15 m = 1.0 x 10^5.
3. The atom is about 100 000 times wider than its nucleus.

**Example 3: Mass comparison.** Compare the mass of a proton with the mass of an electron.
1. Divide: 1.673 x 10^-27 kg / 9.11 x 10^-31 kg.
2. The ratio is about 1836.
3. The mass of the electrons adds almost nothing to the mass of an atom.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that electrons contribute much to the mass of an atom, when they have only about 1/1836 of the mass of a proton. Another mistake is to call the nucleus negative, when it contains protons and is positive. Many give Thomson credit for the nucleus: he discovered the electron, and Rutherford discovered the nucleus. Finally, remember that neutrons are neutral, so they add mass but no charge.
