# Gravity, Weight and Friction

Two forces influence almost every motion on Earth. Gravity pulls everything toward the ground, and friction opposes sliding. Together they decide whether an object starts moving, how it speeds up and why it eventually comes to rest.

## Key ideas

Gravity is the attractive force between objects that have mass. Near the Earth, the force of gravity on an object is called its weight. Weight is calculated as W = m x g, where m is the mass in kilograms and g is the acceleration due to gravity. On Earth g is 9.8 m/s^2, and on the Moon it is about 1.6 m/s^2. Weight is a force, so it is measured in newtons and it points downward, toward the center of the planet. Mass, on the other hand, is the amount of matter in an object, and it is the same everywhere. A person with a mass of 60 kg weighs 588 N on Earth but only 96 N on the Moon.

Friction is a force that opposes the motion, or the attempt at motion, between two surfaces that touch each other. It always points opposite to the direction in which the object moves or would move. Static friction acts on objects that are not moving; it is what keeps a parked car from rolling away. Sliding friction acts on objects that slide over a surface. Rolling friction acts on wheels and is usually much smaller. Fluid friction, or air resistance, acts on objects that move through a gas or liquid.

The amount of friction depends on the two surfaces and on how hard they are pressed together. Rough surfaces and larger pressing forces give more friction. Friction can be useful, as it is for the grip of shoe soles and tires, and it can be reduced by wheels, smooth surfaces, polish or oil.

When you analyze motion, friction must be included in the net force. If a box moves at constant velocity, the net force is zero, so the pushing force and the friction force are equal. If the push is greater than the friction, the net force is the difference, and the box accelerates according to F = ma. Air resistance works the same way; when a falling object reaches terminal velocity, the air resistance equals the weight and the net force is zero.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Weight on Earth and on the Moon.** Find the weight of a 60 kg person on Earth and on the Moon, where g = 1.6 m/s^2.
1. On Earth, W = m x g = 60 x 9.8 = 588 N.
2. On the Moon, W = 60 x 1.6 = 96 N.
3. The mass is 60 kg in both places. Only the weight changes, because g is different.

**Example 2: Constant velocity.** A box slides at constant velocity when it is pushed with 40 N. What is the friction force?
1. Constant velocity means zero acceleration, so the net force is zero.
2. The push of 40 N must be balanced by the friction force.
3. The friction force is 40 N, in the direction opposite to the push.

**Example 3: Net force with friction.** A 50 kg box is pushed with 100 N, and the friction force is 60 N. What is its acceleration?
1. The net force is the push minus the friction: 100 - 60 = 40 N.
2. Use a = F / m = 40 / 50.
3. The acceleration is 0.8 m/s^2.

## Common mistakes

The most frequent error is to mix up mass and weight; mass is in kilograms and does not change with location, but weight is in newtons and does. Another mistake is to think that friction acts only on objects that are moving, while static friction acts on objects at rest. Students also forget to subtract friction from the pushing force before using F = ma.
