# Relative Velocity

Velocity is always measured relative to something. A passenger sitting still on a moving train has zero velocity relative to the train, yet moves fast relative to the ground. Neither answer is wrong; they simply use different reference frames. When a boat crosses a river or a plane flies through wind, the motion has two parts, and relative velocity is the tool that combines them. The idea is just vector addition applied to velocities.

## Key ideas

The central rule says that the velocity of object A relative to object C equals the velocity of A relative to B plus the velocity of B relative to C. In symbols, v(AC) = v(AB) + v(BC). The middle letter B appears once on each side and cancels, which is an easy way to remember the order. In practice, this rule says that the velocity you measure from the ground equals the velocity of the object in its moving medium plus the velocity of that medium.

Two common examples follow. For a boat in a river, the velocity relative to the shore equals the velocity relative to the water plus the velocity of the water, that is, the current. For an aircraft in wind, the ground velocity equals the air velocity plus the wind velocity. If the velocities point in the same direction, the speeds add. If they point in opposite directions, the speeds subtract. If they are perpendicular, use the Pythagorean theorem: v = sqrt(v1^2 + v2^2).

A powerful fact makes river-crossing problems easy: motion in perpendicular directions is independent. A boat aimed straight across a river moves across at its own speed relative to the water, and the current moves it downstream at the same time. The crossing time depends only on the width of the river and the speed across, while the downstream drift depends only on the current and that same time. The current does not slow down the crossing when the boat is aimed straight across.

Always ask the question "relative to what?" before reading a velocity. A velocity quoted without its reference frame has no meaning, and many wrong answers come from mixing velocities that belong to different frames.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Upstream and downstream.** A boat moves at 5.0 m/s in still water, and the river flows at 2.0 m/s. Find its speed relative to the shore in each direction.
1. Downstream the velocities point the same way, so 5.0 + 2.0 = 7.0 m/s.
2. Upstream they point opposite ways, so 5.0 - 2.0 = 3.0 m/s.
3. The same engine gives different shore speeds, because the water itself is moving.

**Example 2: Crossing a river.** A boat heads straight across a river 100 m wide at 5.0 m/s relative to the water. The current is 2.0 m/s. Find the crossing time, the drift and the speed relative to the shore.
1. The crossing time is the width divided by the speed across: 100 / 5.0 = 20 s.
2. In that time the current carries the boat downstream by 2.0 x 20 = 40 m.
3. The two velocities are perpendicular, so the speed relative to the shore is sqrt(5.0^2 + 2.0^2) = sqrt(29) = 5.4 m/s.

**Example 3: Two cars.** Car A moves at 30 m/s east. Find its velocity relative to car B in two cases.
1. If B moves at 20 m/s east, then v(AB) = 30 - 20 = 10 m/s east. B sees A pulling slowly ahead.
2. If B moves at 20 m/s west, then v(AB) = 30 - (-20) = 50 m/s east. B sees A rushing past.
3. The difference shows that opposite motion makes the relative speed larger.

## Common mistakes

Students often add the boat speed and the current speed even when the boat goes against the current; moving against the current calls for subtraction, as in the upstream case, and always for a reason: the water pushes the boat backward. Another mistake is to use the full boat speed in the crossing-time formula when the boat is not aimed straight across. Forgetting the reference frame is also common, so write the "relative to" part beside every velocity. Finally, never add perpendicular velocities by plain arithmetic, since 5.0 + 2.0 = 7.0 m/s is larger than the true speed of 5.4 m/s.
