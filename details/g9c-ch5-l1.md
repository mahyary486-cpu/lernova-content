# Properties of Acids and Bases

Lemon juice is sour and soap is slippery. These everyday differences come from two important groups of chemicals, acids and bases. They are found in food, in cleaning products, in our bodies and in industry, and they react with each other in a very useful way. This lesson describes the properties of each group, explains where they come from and gives a safety rule for handling them.

## Key ideas

Acids are substances with a sour taste, such as the citric acid in lemons and the acetic acid in vinegar. They turn blue litmus paper red, and they react with many metals, such as zinc and magnesium, to produce hydrogen gas. They also react with carbonates, such as chalk or baking soda, producing carbon dioxide, water and a salt, which is why vinegar fizzes on baking soda. The common feature of acids is that when they dissolve in water they release hydrogen ions, H+. These ions are responsible for the typical acid properties.

Bases taste bitter and feel slippery, like soap. They turn red litmus paper blue. When they dissolve in water, they release hydroxide ions, OH-. Examples are sodium hydroxide, used in drain cleaners, and ammonia, used in window cleaners. Bases that dissolve in water are also called alkalis.

Both acids and bases conduct electricity when dissolved in water, because the ions they produce are free to move and carry charge. This is a useful way to tell them apart from sugar solutions, which do not conduct.

Acids and bases are also described as strong or weak. A strong acid or base ionizes almost completely in water, so nearly every molecule releases its ions. Hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide are strong. A weak acid or base ionizes only partly, and most of its molecules stay intact. Acetic acid in vinegar and ammonia are weak. This is a different idea from concentration. Concentrated means a large amount of the substance in a given volume, while strong refers to how completely it ionizes. A dilute solution of a strong acid can still be more dangerous than a concentrated solution of a weak acid, and the reverse can also be true, so both words are needed.

One safety rule applies to all unknown chemicals: never taste or touch them. The tastes described above are known from food only, and many strong acids and bases cause serious burns. Always wear goggles and follow the instructions when working with them.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Identify.** Is vinegar an acid or a base?
1. Vinegar tastes sour and contains acetic acid, and it fizzes when poured on baking soda.
2. These are properties of acids, and it is a weak acid, so it is safe to use in food.

**Example 2: Metal and acid.** Zinc is placed in hydrochloric acid and bubbles form. What are the bubbles?
1. Acids react with many metals to produce hydrogen gas.
2. The bubbles are hydrogen. The zinc is slowly used up, and a salt, zinc chloride, forms in the solution.

**Example 3: Ions.** Which ion is released by a base when it dissolves in water?
1. Bases release hydroxide ions into the solution.
2. The ion is OH-, and it is responsible for the slippery feeling and the bitter taste of bases.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to think that all acids are dangerous, when many are weak and found in food, such as citric acid and acetic acid. Another is to think that strong means concentrated. Strength is about how completely a substance ionizes, and concentration is about how much is dissolved. Finally, never test an unknown substance by tasting it.
