# Hydrocarbons: Alkanes and Their Names

Natural gas, petrol and candle wax look very different, yet all of them are made mainly of alkanes, which are hydrocarbons with chains of carbon atoms of different lengths. Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds, and carbon is special because its atoms bond to each other in long chains and rings. A huge number of compounds are possible, so chemists need a clear system for writing their formulas and their names. The alkanes are the simplest family, and the rules learned here apply to all the other families.

## Key ideas

Alkanes are hydrocarbons with only single bonds, so they are called saturated, since each carbon holds as many hydrogen atoms as it can. The general formula of a chain alkane is CnH2n+2. Methane is CH4, ethane is C2H6, and propane is C3H8. Each carbon atom forms four bonds, and the formula follows from this. A ring of carbon atoms, called a cycloalkane, has two hydrogens fewer, so its formula is CnH2n, because the ends of the chain are joined.

The names are built from a stem that counts the carbon atoms in the longest chain: meth- for 1, eth- for 2, prop- for 3, but- for 4, pent- for 5, hex- for 6, hept- for 7 and oct- for 8, with the ending -ane. Branches are groups attached to the chain, named with the ending -yl, such as methyl and ethyl. The method for a branched alkane has three steps. First find the longest continuous chain of carbon atoms, which gives the parent name. Second, number the chain from the end that gives the lowest numbers to the branches. Third, write the name as the position and name of each branch, followed by the parent name, as in 2-methylbutane. The longest chain is not always drawn in a straight line, so the structure must be examined carefully.

The physical properties follow the size of the molecule. Alkanes are nonpolar, so only dispersion forces act between them. Those forces become stronger as the chain gets longer, so the boiling point rises with chain length. The small alkanes are gases, the middle ones are liquids such as petrol, and the large ones are waxy solids. Branching lowers the boiling point of an isomer, because a compact molecule has less surface in contact with its neighbors.

Alkanes are not very reactive. Their two main reactions are combustion, which gives carbon dioxide and water when the oxygen is sufficient, and substitution with halogens in the presence of light, in which a hydrogen atom is replaced by a halogen atom.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Formula of an alkane with 10 carbons.**
1. Use CnH2n+2 with n = 10.
2. The number of hydrogens is 2 x 10 + 2 = 22.
3. The formula is C10H22, and the name is decane.

**Example 2: Name CH3-CH(CH3)-CH2-CH3.**
1. The longest chain has 4 carbon atoms, so the parent is butane.
2. A methyl group is attached to the second carbon of the chain, counting from the end that gives the branch the lowest number.
3. The name is 2-methylbutane.

**Example 3: Combustion of propane.**
1. The unbalanced equation is C3H8 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O, and the carbon gives 3 CO2 and the hydrogen gives 4 H2O.
2. The oxygen on the right is 3 x 2 + 4 = 10 atoms, which needs 5 O2.
3. The balanced equation is C3H8 + 5 O2 -> 3 CO2 + 4 H2O.

## Common mistakes

Students often count the wrong longest chain, because they follow the way the structure is drawn, and not the longest path of carbon atoms. Another error is to number from the wrong end, so that the branch receives a higher number than necessary. The third mistake is to forget the 2n + 2 rule, and to write a formula such as C5H10 for pentane, which has 12 hydrogens.
