# Relationships between living things

Living things in an ecosystem affect each other through feeding, competing and living together in close partnerships.

## Key ideas

- **Predation.** A predator hunts and kills another animal, the prey, for food. Predators have adaptations such as sharp claws, speed and good eyesight. Prey have adaptations such as camouflage, speed and warning colors. Predators also help prey populations stay healthy, because they often catch weak or sick animals.
- **Competition.** Organisms compete when they need the same limited resource: food, water, light, space or mates. Competition can be within one species (two male deer fighting for territory) or between different species (trees growing in a forest compete for sunlight). Competition limits the size of a population.
- **Symbiosis.** Symbiosis means two different species living in close contact for a long time. There are three main types.
- **Mutualism (+/+).** Both species benefit. Bees collect nectar from flowers, and in return they carry pollen from flower to flower. Another example is the bacteria in the human intestine that help digestion while they receive food.
- **Commensalism (+/0).** One species benefits and the other is neither helped nor harmed. Small birds that build nests in a large tree are an example.
- **Parasitism (+/-).** The parasite benefits and the host is harmed, but usually not killed quickly. Ticks, fleas and tapeworms are parasites.
- **Balance.** These relationships keep populations in balance. If one population grows too large, its food runs short or its predators increase, and the numbers fall again.

## Examples

**Example 1: Predator and prey.** In a forest, a population of rabbits grows when there is plenty of grass. More rabbits mean more food for foxes, so the fox population rises a little later. More foxes eat more rabbits, so the rabbit population drops. Then the foxes have less food and their numbers drop too. The two populations rise and fall in a repeating pattern.

**Example 2: Identifying symbiosis.** A clownfish lives among the stinging tentacles of a sea anemone. The anemone protects the fish from predators, and the fish cleans the anemone and drives away fish that eat it. Both benefit, so this is mutualism. A remora fish attached to a shark eats scraps from the shark's meals; the shark is not affected, so this is commensalism. A tapeworm in the intestine of a cow takes the cow's food and harms it, so this is parasitism.

**Example 3: Competition.** Young pine trees grow close together. Each tree needs sunlight, water and minerals. Taller trees shade the smaller ones. The smaller trees get less light and grow slowly or die. This is competition for light between members of the same species.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that all symbiosis is helpful. Symbiosis only means close living together; it can be helpful, neutral or harmful.
- Mixing up commensalism and mutualism. In commensalism the second species gets no benefit at all.
- Thinking a parasite quickly kills its host. A successful parasite usually keeps the host alive.
- Thinking predators are bad for an ecosystem. Without predators, prey populations may grow too large and destroy their food supply.

## Remember

- Predation, competition and symbiosis control the numbers of populations.
- Mutualism is +/+, commensalism is +/0 and parasitism is +/-.
- Competition happens when a resource is limited.
