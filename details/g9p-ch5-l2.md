# Sound Waves

When a guitar string is plucked, it vibrates and pushes on the nearby air. The air passes this vibration along, particle by particle, until it reaches your ear. Sound is therefore a wave, and understanding its properties explains why we hear thunder after we see lightning and how an echo works.

## Key ideas

Sound is a longitudinal wave that is produced by vibrations. As the source moves, it pushes the air particles together to make regions of high pressure called compressions, and then leaves regions of low pressure called rarefactions. These alternating regions travel through the air. Because sound needs particles to carry it, it needs a medium; it cannot travel through the vacuum of space.

The speed of sound depends on the medium. In air at room temperature it is about 343 m/s, which is often rounded to 340 m/s. Sound travels faster in liquids, about 1500 m/s in water, and even faster in solids, about 5000 m/s in steel, because the particles are closer together. Light travels far faster than sound, which is why you see lightning before you hear thunder.

Two features of a sound are easy to notice. The pitch, how high or low a sound is, depends on the frequency of the wave: a high frequency gives a high pitch. The loudness depends on the amplitude: a larger amplitude gives a louder sound, which is measured in decibels (dB). A louder sound does not travel faster. Humans can hear frequencies from about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz; sounds above this range are called ultrasound.

An echo is sound that has been reflected from a surface and returns to the listener. To find the distance to the reflecting surface, remember that the sound travels there and back, so the distance to the wall is half of the total distance: d = v x t / 2.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Thunder.** You hear thunder 3 s after you see lightning. The speed of sound is 340 m/s. How far away is the lightning?
1. The light arrives almost instantly, so the 3 s is the travel time of the sound.
2. Use d = v x t = 340 x 3.
3. The lightning is about 1020 m away.

**Example 2: Echo.** An echo of a shout returns after 2 s. The speed of sound is 340 m/s. How far away is the cliff?
1. The sound travels to the cliff and back, so the total distance is 340 x 2 = 680 m.
2. The cliff is only half of that distance away.
3. The cliff is 340 m away.

**Example 3: Pitch.** A flute plays a higher note than a tuba. What does that tell you about the sound waves?
1. A higher note means a higher pitch.
2. Pitch depends on frequency.
3. So the flute produces sound waves with a higher frequency than the tuba.

## Common mistakes

A common error is to believe that sound can travel in space, where there is no medium. Another is to think that a louder sound travels faster; loudness depends on amplitude, but the speed depends only on the medium. Finally, when solving echo problems, students often use the full path as the distance to the wall instead of half of it.
