# Electrolytes, Ionic Equations and Precipitation

Tap water conducts electricity, because it contains dissolved ions, but pure sugar water does not. This difference is the starting point for understanding what really happens when ionic compounds react in water. A normal equation hides the ions, while an ionic equation shows which particles take part and which only watch. This lesson teaches how to write ionic equations and how to use them to predict and measure a precipitate.

## Key ideas

An electrolyte is a substance that gives ions when dissolved in water, so that the solution conducts electricity. A strong electrolyte dissociates completely, as NaCl, strong acids and soluble ionic compounds do. A weak electrolyte ionizes only partly, as acetic acid does, and it conducts poorly. A nonelectrolyte, such as sugar or ethanol, dissolves as whole molecules and does not conduct.

There are three ways to write a reaction in solution. The molecular equation shows the formulas of the compounds. The complete ionic equation shows every strong electrolyte that is dissolved as separate ions, while solids, liquids, gases and weak electrolytes are left as formulas. The net ionic equation is obtained by removing the spectator ions, the ions that appear unchanged on both sides, and it shows only the particles that actually react. For example, mixing silver nitrate and sodium chloride gives the net ionic equation Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s), where sodium and nitrate are the spectators. Any soluble silver salt and any soluble chloride give the same net equation.

Two checks are needed for a correct net ionic equation. The atoms must balance, and the total charge must be the same on both sides. A precipitate is predicted with the solubility rules: nitrates and group 1 salts are soluble, most chlorides and sulfates are soluble with a few exceptions, and carbonates, hydroxides and sulfides are mostly insoluble.

A net ionic equation also works in calculations, because its coefficients are mole ratios. When the amount of one ion is known from a molarity and a volume, the amount of precipitate follows directly, and it can be converted to a mass with the molar mass.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Writing a net ionic equation.** Write the net ionic equation for Pb(NO3)2 + 2 KI -> PbI2 + 2 KNO3.
1. PbI2 is an insoluble solid, so it stays as a formula. Pb2+, I-, K+ and NO3- are dissolved ions.
2. The ions K+ and NO3- appear on both sides unchanged, so they are spectators and are removed.
3. The net ionic equation is Pb2+(aq) + 2 I-(aq) -> PbI2(s). The charge is +2 - 2 = 0 on the left and 0 on the right.

**Example 2: Mass of a precipitate.** A volume of 50 mL of 0.1 M AgNO3 reacts with an excess of NaCl. The molar mass of AgCl is 143.5 g/mol.
1. The moles of silver ions are 0.1 mol/L x 0.050 L = 0.005 mol.
2. The net equation has a ratio of 1 to 1, so 0.005 mol of AgCl forms.
3. The mass is 0.005 mol x 143.5 g/mol = 0.72 g.

**Example 3: Conductivity.** Which conducts better, 0.1 M NaCl or 0.1 M sugar solution?
1. NaCl is a strong electrolyte and gives mobile ions, while sugar stays as molecules.
2. The NaCl solution conducts much better.

## Common mistakes

A common error is to write the spectator ions in the net ionic equation, when they must be removed. Another is to split solids or weak electrolytes into ions, although only dissolved strong electrolytes are written as ions. Students also forget to check the charge, which must balance as well as the atoms. A last error is to use the volume in milliliters directly with the molarity, so convert it to liters first.
