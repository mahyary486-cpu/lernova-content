# Special Relativity

At speeds close to the speed of light, time and length are no longer absolute, and mass and energy turn out to be two forms of the same thing, linked by the famous equation E = m c^2. Einstein's special theory of relativity, published in 1905, replaced the everyday ideas of space and time with a more accurate picture. The effects are tiny at ordinary speeds, which is why we never notice them, but they are real and they are measured every day.

## Key ideas

The theory rests on two postulates. First, the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames, meaning frames moving at constant velocity relative to each other. Second, the speed of light in a vacuum, c = 3.00 x 10^8 m/s, is the same for all observers, whatever their own motion or the motion of the source. The second postulate seems to contradict common sense, but experiments confirm it, and everything else follows from it.

The size of the relativistic effects is set by the Lorentz factor, gamma = 1 / sqrt(1 - v^2 / c^2). At everyday speeds, v is tiny compared to c, and gamma is essentially 1. As v approaches c, gamma grows without limit. Two effects follow. Time dilation says that a moving clock runs slow: the time measured between two events by an observer who sees the clock move is t = gamma t0, where t0 is the proper time, measured in the frame where the events happen at the same place. Length contraction says that a moving object is shorter in the direction of motion: L = L0 / gamma, where L0 is the proper length measured at rest.

Mass and energy are linked. An object at rest has the rest energy E = m c^2. The total energy of a moving object is E_total = gamma m c^2, and its kinetic energy is KE = (gamma - 1) m c^2, which becomes the familiar 1/2 m v^2 at low speeds. As the speed approaches c, the energy needed grows without limit, so no object with mass can reach the speed of light.

Relativity has strong experimental support. Muons created in the upper atmosphere live only 2.2 microseconds at rest, and they should decay before reaching the ground, yet many arrive because their lifetime is extended by time dilation. The GPS satellites need corrections for relativity to keep accurate positions.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: The Lorentz factor.** A spaceship moves at 0.60 c. Find gamma.
1. v^2 / c^2 = 0.36, so 1 - 0.36 = 0.64.
2. gamma = 1 / sqrt(0.64) = 1 / 0.80.
3. The factor is gamma = 1.25.

**Example 2: Time dilation.** A trip takes 10 years by Earth's clocks for a ship moving at 0.60 c. How long does it take by the ship's clock?
1. The Earth time is the dilated time, t = 10 years, and the ship measures the proper time t0.
2. t0 = t / gamma = 10 / 1.25.
3. The trip takes t0 = 8.0 years for the travelers.

**Example 3: Rest energy.** Find the rest energy of a mass of 1.0 g.
1. Convert the mass: m = 0.0010 kg.
2. E = m c^2 = 0.0010 x (3.0 x 10^8)^2 = 0.0010 x 9.0 x 10^16.
3. The energy is E = 9.0 x 10^13 J, about the energy released by an explosion of 20 000 tons of TNT.

## Common mistakes

Students often mix up proper time and dilated time; the proper time is the shortest one, measured in the frame where the events happen at the same place. Another mistake is to put v / c as a percent into the formula instead of a fraction. Many think that mass is converted into energy only in nuclear reactions, but every change of energy changes the mass slightly, even in chemical reactions. Finally, remember that gamma is always 1 or larger.
