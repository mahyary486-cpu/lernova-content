# Fission, Fusion and the Standard Model

The Sun releases its energy by fusing hydrogen into helium, while nuclear power stations release energy by splitting uranium. Both processes turn a tiny part of the mass of nuclei into energy, following E = m c^2, and both release millions of times more energy per atom than any chemical reaction. This lesson explains why splitting and joining nuclei both release energy, and then takes a step further to the smallest known building blocks of matter.

## Key ideas

The key to understanding the two processes is the binding energy per nucleon. It rises quickly for the lightest nuclei, reaches a maximum near iron-56, and then falls slowly for the heaviest nuclei. A reaction releases energy when it moves nuclei toward the maximum. Heavy nuclei such as uranium can gain stability by splitting into medium-sized pieces, and light nuclei such as hydrogen gain stability by joining.

In nuclear fission, a heavy nucleus, usually uranium-235, absorbs a neutron and splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing about 200 MeV of energy and two or three more neutrons. These neutrons can cause further fissions, creating a chain reaction. In a power reactor the chain reaction is controlled, so that on average one neutron from each fission causes another, while in a bomb it is uncontrolled. In fusion, light nuclei, such as hydrogen isotopes, join to form a heavier one, and energy is released. Fusion powers the Sun and other stars. It needs temperatures of millions of degrees, because the nuclei must come close enough to overcome their electric repulsion, so it does not happen at room temperature. This is why fusion energy is difficult to harness on Earth.

Beyond the nucleus, the Standard Model describes the elementary particles. Matter is made of quarks and leptons. Protons and neutrons contain quarks, held together by the strong force; the electron is a lepton. There are six types of quarks, including up and down, and six leptons, including the electron and the neutrino. Forces are carried by particles called bosons: the photon for electromagnetism, the gluon for the strong force, and the W and Z bosons for the weak force responsible for beta decay. Every particle has an antiparticle with the same mass and opposite charge, and when matter meets antimatter, both disappear and turn into energy, which is called annihilation.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Energy from fission.** How much energy is released by the fission of 1 mole of uranium-235, at 200 MeV per nucleus? Use 1 MeV = 1.6 x 10^-13 J and N_A = 6.02 x 10^23.
1. The energy per nucleus is 200 x 1.6 x 10^-13 = 3.2 x 10^-11 J.
2. For a mole, E = 3.2 x 10^-11 x 6.02 x 10^23.
3. The energy is E = 1.9 x 10^13 J, for only 235 g of uranium.

**Example 2: Mass converted.** A fusion reaction releases 17.6 MeV. How much mass is converted into energy?
1. Use 1 u = 931.5 MeV, so delta m = 17.6 / 931.5 u.
2. The mass converted is delta m = 0.0189 u.
3. This is only a fraction of one percent of the mass of the particles involved.

**Example 3: Quarks in a proton.** A proton is made of two up quarks, each with charge +2/3, and one down quark with charge -1/3. Find its charge.
1. The total is 2 x (+2/3) + (-1/3) = +4/3 - 1/3.
2. The charge is +1, in units of the elementary charge.
3. A neutron has one up and two down quarks, giving 2/3 - 2/3 = 0.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that fusion can happen at room temperature, but it needs extreme temperatures. Another mistake is to mix up fission and fusion: fission splits heavy nuclei, and fusion joins light ones. Many forget that the mass of the products is slightly smaller than the mass of the reactants, and that this missing mass is the released energy. Finally, remember that the force carrier of electromagnetism is the photon, not the gluon.
