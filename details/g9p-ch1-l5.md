# Graphs and Data Analysis

A table of numbers contains information, but it is hard to see a pattern in a long list of values. A graph turns the same data into a picture in which patterns are easy to notice: whether one quantity rises when another rises, how fast it changes, and whether a few points do not fit. Reading and drawing graphs correctly is a basic skill for all of physics.

## Key ideas

A graph compares two variables. By convention the independent variable, the one that you change or that is given, goes on the horizontal x-axis. The dependent variable, the one that responds, goes on the vertical y-axis. A good graph has a title, labeled axes with units, and an even scale so that equal distances on the paper stand for equal amounts. The points are plotted from the data. Line graphs are used when something changes continuously, such as the position of a cart over time, while bar graphs compare separate categories, such as the heights of different plants. If the points follow a trend, draw a best-fit line or curve that passes as close as possible to all of them, rather than connecting each dot with a zigzag, because measurements always contain small errors.

The slope of a straight line tells how fast y changes when x changes. It is calculated as rise over run, which means the change in y divided by the change in x. The unit of the slope is the unit of y divided by the unit of x. On a distance-time graph, for example, the slope has units of meters per second, so it is the speed. When a straight line passes through the origin, the two variables are directly proportional: doubling x doubles y, and the relationship can be written y = kx, where k is the slope.

Graphs can also be used to find values that were not measured. Reading a value between two measured points is called interpolation and is usually reliable. Extending the line beyond the measured data is called extrapolation; it is a useful estimate but less reliable, because the pattern may change outside the tested range.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Finding a slope.** A distance-time graph is a straight line through the points (0 s, 0 m) and (4 s, 12 m). Find its slope and say what it means.
1. The rise is the change in distance: 12 m - 0 m = 12 m.
2. The run is the change in time: 4 s - 0 s = 4 s.
3. The slope is 12 m / 4 s = 3 m/s. Since distance is divided by time, the slope is the speed of the object.

**Example 2: Using the equation of the line.** A straight line through the origin has a slope of 3 m/s. What distance does the object reach at 10 s?
1. A line through the origin has the form y = kx, so here distance = 3 m/s x time.
2. Substitute the time: d = 3 m/s x 10 s.
3. The distance is 30 m.

**Example 3: A line that does not start at the origin.** A cart is at 5 m when t = 0 and at 25 m when t = 4 s. Find its speed.
1. The change in position is 25 m - 5 m = 20 m.
2. The time interval is 4 s.
3. The speed is 20 m / 4 s = 5 m/s. The line starts at 5 m on the vertical axis, so the cart did not start at the origin, and this line is not directly proportional.

## Common mistakes

A frequent error is to place the independent variable on the y-axis, which turns the graph around and confuses its meaning. Another is to choose an uneven scale or to leave out units, which makes the numbers impossible to interpret. Some students join every dot with straight segments instead of drawing a smooth best-fit line. When calculating slope, always use two points that lie on the line itself, not two data points that happen to be off the line.
