# Current, Resistance and Ohm's Law

Electric current is a flow of charge, and a good way to picture it is water flowing in a pipe. The voltage of a battery acts like the pressure that pushes the water, the current is the amount of water passing a point each second, and the resistance is how narrow or long the pipe is, which determines how hard it is for the water to flow. This analogy is not perfect, but it helps to keep the three quantities apart as we meet Ohm's law, the basic rule of circuits.

## Key ideas

Electric current is the rate at which charge passes a point in a conductor: I = delta Q / delta t. The unit is the ampere (A), where 1 A = 1 C/s. By convention, the direction of the current is the direction in which positive charge would move, from the positive terminal of the battery toward the negative terminal, even though in metals the moving charges are actually electrons going the other way. Current is not used up as it passes through a device; the same current that enters a resistor leaves it. What changes is the energy of each charge.

Resistance measures how much a component opposes the flow of current. Ohm's law states that for many materials the potential difference across a conductor is proportional to the current: V = I R. The unit of resistance is the ohm. A conductor that obeys this law is called ohmic, and a graph of V against I for it is a straight line through the origin. Not every device is ohmic; a light bulb filament, for instance, is not, because its resistance changes as it heats.

The resistance of a wire depends on its shape and material through R = rho L / A, where L is the length, A is the cross-sectional area, and rho is the resistivity, a property of the material measured in ohm meters. Doubling the length doubles the resistance, and doubling the area halves it, just as a longer and narrower pipe is harder for water to flow through. Good conductors such as copper have very small resistivity.

Temperature matters too. For metals, the resistance increases as the temperature rises, because the atoms vibrate more and scatter the moving electrons more often. For this reason a bulb has less resistance when it is cold, and it draws a large current at the moment it is switched on.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Current.** A charge of 30 C passes a point in 10 s. Find the current.
1. Use I = Q / t = 30 / 10.
2. The current is I = 3.0 A.

**Example 2: Ohm's law.** A 12 V battery drives current through a 4.0 ohm resistor. Find the current.
1. Rearrange Ohm's law: I = V / R = 12 / 4.0.
2. The current is I = 3.0 A.
3. If the resistor were doubled to 8.0 ohm, the current would be halved to 1.5 A.

**Example 3: Resistance of a wire.** A copper wire has resistivity 1.7 x 10^-8 ohm m, length 10 m and cross-sectional area 1.0 x 10^-6 m^2. Find its resistance.
1. Use R = rho L / A = 1.7 x 10^-8 x 10 / 1.0 x 10^-6.
2. The numerator is 1.7 x 10^-7.
3. The resistance is R = 0.17 ohm, small, as expected for copper.

## Common mistakes

Many students think that current is used up as it passes through a resistor; the current is the same before and after, and it is the energy that is delivered. Another error is to use the diameter of the wire in place of its area in R = rho L / A. Students also confuse resistivity, a property of the material, with resistance, a property of the particular wire. Finally, remember that Ohm's law applies to a single component or to the whole circuit, but the voltage must be the one across that same part.
