# Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity

Carbon-14 dating works because the amount of radioactive carbon in a dead plant or animal halves every 5730 years. By measuring what remains, scientists can tell how long ago the organism died. This is one of many uses of radioactivity, the spontaneous change of unstable nuclei. This lesson describes the structure of the nucleus, how it releases energy and particles, and how to describe the rate of decay.

## Key ideas

The nucleus contains protons and neutrons, together called nucleons. The atomic number Z is the number of protons, and the mass number A is the number of nucleons. Isotopes of an element have the same Z but different A, so they have the same chemistry but different nuclear properties. The nucleus is held together by the strong nuclear force, which overcomes the electric repulsion of the protons at very short distances.

The mass of a nucleus is slightly smaller than the total mass of its separate nucleons. This difference, the mass defect, corresponds to the binding energy that holds the nucleus together, given by E = delta m c^2. In atomic mass units, 1 u corresponds to 931.5 MeV. The larger the binding energy per nucleon, the more stable the nucleus.

Unstable nuclei decay in three main ways. In alpha decay a nucleus emits a helium nucleus, so A decreases by 4 and Z by 2. In beta-minus decay a neutron changes into a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino, so Z increases by 1 and A is unchanged. In gamma decay the nucleus emits a high-energy photon and changes neither A nor Z. In every case, mass number and charge are conserved on both sides of the equation.

Radioactive decay is random for any single nucleus, but it is very predictable for a large number of them. The number of nuclei left falls exponentially, N = N0 e^(-lambda t), where lambda is the decay constant. The half-life is the time for half of the nuclei to decay, T(1/2) = 0.693 / lambda, and it does not depend on how much material there is. After n half-lives, the fraction (1/2)^n remains. Half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years, which is what makes different isotopes useful for medicine, dating and power.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Alpha decay.** Uranium-238 (Z = 92) emits an alpha particle. Find the daughter nucleus.
1. The mass number becomes 238 - 4 = 234.
2. The atomic number becomes 92 - 2 = 90.
3. The element with Z = 90 is thorium, so the daughter is thorium-234.

**Example 2: Half-life.** A sample of 80 g has a half-life of 6.0 hours. How much remains after 18 hours?
1. The time of 18 hours equals 18 / 6.0 = 3 half-lives.
2. The remaining fraction is (1/2)^3 = 1/8.
3. The mass left is 80 x 1/8 = 10 g.

**Example 3: Binding energy.** A nucleus has a mass defect of 0.030 u. Find the binding energy.
1. Use 1 u = 931.5 MeV.
2. E = 0.030 x 931.5.
3. The binding energy is E = 27.9 MeV, about 28 MeV.

## Common mistakes

Students often confuse the mass number with the atomic number. Another mistake is to think that the half-life depends on the amount of the substance; it is a constant for each isotope. Many forget that beta-minus decay increases Z by 1 while A stays the same. Finally, remember that after two half-lives one quarter remains, not zero.
