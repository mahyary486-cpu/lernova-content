# Electric Potential and Potential Energy

Electric potential is to electricity what height is to gravity. A ball rolls downhill from a high place to a low place, and a positive charge moves from high potential to low potential. Thinking in terms of potential lets us deal with energy instead of forces, and the energy approach is often simpler, because energy is a scalar and does not need directions or components.

## Key ideas

A charge in an electric field has electric potential energy, just as a mass in a gravitational field has gravitational potential energy. Electric potential, V, is the potential energy per unit charge: V = U / q, or U = q V. The unit is the volt (V), with 1 V = 1 J/C. What matters in practice is the potential difference between two points. When a charge q moves through a potential difference delta V, the work done by the field is related to the change by delta V = W / q, so a charge q gains or loses an energy of q delta V.

For a point charge Q, the potential at a distance r is V = k Q / r, with the reference value of zero at infinity. Notice that this falls off as 1 / r, more slowly than the field, which falls as 1 / r^2. Potential is a scalar, so for several charges you simply add the potentials as ordinary numbers, with the signs of the charges included. This is an important difference from the electric field, which must be added as vectors.

At the atomic scale the joule is too large, so physicists use the electron-volt (eV): 1 eV is the energy gained by an electron moving through a potential difference of 1 V, and 1 eV = 1.6 x 10^-19 J. A particle with the charge e that is accelerated through 500 V gains 500 eV.

Potential and field are connected. In a uniform field, E = V / d, where d is the distance along the field between two points with potential difference V. The field points from high potential to low potential, and it is always perpendicular to the equipotential surfaces, which are surfaces of constant potential. No work is needed to move a charge along an equipotential surface.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Potential of a point charge.** Find the potential 0.30 m from a charge of 2.0 x 10^-9 C.
1. Use V = k Q / r = 9 x 10^9 x 2.0 x 10^-9 / 0.30.
2. The numerator is 18, so V = 18 / 0.30.
3. The potential is V = 60 V.

**Example 2: Energy gained.** A proton moves through a potential difference of 500 V. Find the energy it gains.
1. Use energy = q delta V = 1.6 x 10^-19 x 500.
2. The energy is 8.0 x 10^-17 J.
3. In electron-volts this is simply 500 eV.

**Example 3: Field between plates.** Two parallel plates are 2.0 cm apart and have a potential difference of 400 V. Find the field between them.
1. Convert the distance: d = 2.0 cm = 0.020 m.
2. Use E = V / d = 400 / 0.020.
3. The field is E = 20 000 V/m, the same as 20 000 N/C.

## Common mistakes

Students often treat potential as a vector and try to add it with directions, but it is a scalar. Another mistake is using centimeters instead of meters in E = V / d. Many also confuse potential (V, in volts) with potential energy (U, in joules); the first is per unit charge, and the second depends on the size of the charge. Finally, take care with signs: the potential of a negative charge is negative, and a positive charge that is released moves toward lower potential, gaining kinetic energy as it goes.
