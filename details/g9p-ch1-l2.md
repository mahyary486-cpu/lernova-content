# SI Units and Measurement

A measurement is not just a number. Saying that a table is "2" long is meaningless, because the reader does not know whether you mean 2 meters, 2 feet or 2 hand spans. A measurement always consists of a number and a unit. To make sure that scientists in every country can compare and repeat each other's work, they agreed on one common system of units called the International System of Units, abbreviated SI.

## Key ideas

The SI system is built on a small number of base units. The ones used most often in physical science are the meter (m) for length, the kilogram (kg) for mass, the second (s) for time and the kelvin (K) for temperature. All other units are built from these. A unit that is made by combining base units is called a derived unit. For example, area is measured in square meters (m^2), volume in cubic meters (m^3), speed in meters per second (m/s) and density in kilograms per cubic meter (kg/m^3). The liter (L) is a handy unit for the volume of liquids, and 1 L equals 1000 mL, which equals 1000 cubic centimeters.

Quantities in nature range from the width of a hair to the distance between cities, so the SI uses prefixes that make a unit larger or smaller by a power of ten. Kilo (k) means 1000, so 1 km is 1000 m. Centi (c) means one hundredth, so 1 cm is 0.01 m. Milli (m) means one thousandth, so 1 mm is 0.001 m. Micro means one millionth and mega (M) means one million. Learning these prefixes lets you move between a large and a small unit without confusion.

Temperature is measured in kelvins in the SI system, but in everyday life we use degrees Celsius. The two scales have the same size of degree, so a change of 1 K equals a change of 1 degree Celsius. They differ only in where zero is placed: K = C + 273 (more exactly 273.15). Water freezes at 0 C, which is 273 K.

When you take a measurement, two words describe how good it is. Accuracy tells how close a measurement is to the true value. Precision tells how close repeated measurements are to each other. A scale that always reads 2 kg too high gives precise but inaccurate results. To read an instrument well, look at the scale at eye level so that the position of the pointer does not seem to shift, write down all the digits you can read for certain and estimate one more digit between the smallest marks. For a liquid in a graduated cylinder, read the bottom of the curved surface, the meniscus.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Choosing a unit.** Which SI unit is suitable for the length of a classroom, and about how long is it?
1. A classroom is several steps long, so we need a unit that is neither tiny nor huge.
2. The meter is the SI base unit of length and fits this size well.
3. A typical classroom is about 8 m long.

**Example 2: Reading a ruler.** A ruler is marked every 0.1 cm. The end of a pencil lies between the marks 4.3 cm and 4.4 cm, about halfway between them. How should the length be recorded?
1. The marks tell us for certain that the length is 4.3 cm plus a little more.
2. We estimate one more digit: halfway between the marks is 0.05 cm.
3. The length is recorded as 4.35 cm. The last digit, 5, is the estimated one, and everyone reading the result understands that it is less certain.

**Example 3: Celsius to kelvin.** Water is at 25 degrees Celsius. What is its temperature in kelvins?
1. The relation is K = C + 273.
2. Substituting gives K = 25 + 273 = 298 K.
3. A rise of 10 degrees Celsius would also be a rise of 10 K, because the degrees are the same size.

## Common mistakes

Students often mix up mass and weight. Mass, measured in kilograms, is the amount of matter in an object, while weight is a force measured in newtons. Another frequent error is to read a scale at an angle, which gives a wrong value because of parallax. Forgetting the prefix is also common: 5 mm is five thousandths of a meter, not 5 m. Finally, many people think that accurate and precise mean the same thing. Repeated readings can agree closely with each other and still all be wrong if the instrument is not set up correctly.
