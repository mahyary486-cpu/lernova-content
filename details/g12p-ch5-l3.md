# Matter Waves and the Bohr Atom

Atoms give off only certain colors of light, and each element has its own set of colors, like a fingerprint. This is possible because electrons in atoms can have only certain energies. The explanation needed a new idea: that particles such as electrons also behave like waves. This lesson connects the wave nature of matter to the energy levels of the hydrogen atom, and it ends with the limit on what can be known about a particle.

## Key ideas

In 1924, Louis de Broglie proposed that every moving particle has a wavelength, lambda = h / (m v) = h / p, where h is Planck's constant and p the momentum. For everyday objects the wavelength is far too small to notice. A ball has a wavelength of about 10^-34 m, much smaller than anything measurable. For an electron, however, the wavelength is comparable to the size of atoms, and the wave nature shows up clearly. Electrons passing through a crystal produce diffraction patterns, and electron microscopes use this wave property to see tiny structures.

The wave nature of matter comes with a basic limit. The Heisenberg uncertainty principle says that the position and the momentum of a particle cannot both be known exactly at the same time: delta x delta p is at least h / (4 pi). This is not a result of poor instruments; it is a property of nature. Squeezing a particle into a smaller region makes its momentum less certain.

The Bohr model describes hydrogen with electrons that exist only in certain energy levels. The energies are E_n = -13.6 eV / n^2, where n = 1, 2, 3 and so on. The negative sign means that the electron is bound to the nucleus. The lowest level, n = 1, is the ground state, with the energy -13.6 eV, and the ionization energy, needed to remove the electron completely, is +13.6 eV. The energy levels get closer together at larger n.

When an electron jumps from a higher level to a lower one, the atom emits a photon with the energy equal to the difference: h f = E_high - E_low. Only specific energies are possible, so only specific colors appear in the spectrum of an element. The sharp lines of atomic spectra are direct evidence for discrete energy levels.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: A de Broglie wavelength.** An electron (mass 9.11 x 10^-31 kg) moves at 1.0 x 10^6 m/s. Find its wavelength.
1. The momentum is p = m v = 9.11 x 10^-31 x 1.0 x 10^6 = 9.11 x 10^-25 kg m/s.
2. Use lambda = h / p = 6.63 x 10^-34 / 9.11 x 10^-25.
3. The wavelength is lambda = 7.3 x 10^-10 m, similar to the spacing between atoms in a crystal.

**Example 2: An energy level.** Find the energy of the n = 2 level of hydrogen.
1. Use E_n = -13.6 / n^2 with n = 2.
2. E_2 = -13.6 / 4.
3. The energy is E_2 = -3.4 eV.

**Example 3: A photon from a jump.** An electron falls from n = 3 to n = 2 in hydrogen. Find the energy and wavelength of the photon.
1. E_3 = -13.6 / 9 = -1.51 eV and E_2 = -3.40 eV.
2. The photon energy is E_3 - E_2 = -1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89 eV.
3. The wavelength is lambda = 1240 eV nm / 1.89 eV = 656 nm, which is red light.

## Common mistakes

Students often use the wrong sign for the energy levels, which are all negative. Another mistake is to think that only light has a wavelength, when every moving particle does. Many believe that the uncertainty principle comes from clumsy instruments, but it is a basic property of nature. Finally, in the photon energy, subtract the lower level from the higher one, so that the energy comes out positive.
