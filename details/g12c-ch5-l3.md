# Fission, Fusion and Nuclear Energy

A single gram of uranium-235 releases about as much energy as three tonnes of coal. This enormous difference between nuclear and chemical energy has a simple origin. In a chemical reaction, the changes involve the outer electrons, and the energy per atom is a few electronvolts. In a nuclear reaction, the changes involve the nucleus, where the forces are much stronger, and the energy per atom is millions of electronvolts. Fission and fusion are the two ways of releasing it.

## Key ideas

The source of the energy is the mass defect. The mass of a nucleus is slightly less than the sum of the masses of its separate protons and neutrons, and the difference is the energy that holds the nucleus together, the binding energy. Einstein's equation E = delta m c^2 links the two. The practical unit is the atomic mass unit, where 1 u corresponds to 931.5 MeV. The binding energy per nucleon is largest near iron-56. Because of this, energy is released when a very heavy nucleus splits into medium nuclei, and also when very light nuclei combine into a heavier one.

In fission, a heavy nucleus such as U-235 absorbs a neutron and splits into two lighter nuclei, plus two or three neutrons and a lot of energy. The new neutrons can split other nuclei, and this is a chain reaction. In a nuclear reactor, the chain reaction is controlled. Control rods, made of a material such as boron or cadmium, absorb neutrons, and when they are pushed in, the reaction slows down. A moderator, such as water or graphite, slows the neutrons, because slow neutrons are more likely to cause fission in U-235. The heat produces steam, which turns a turbine. The drawbacks are the radioactive waste, which stays dangerous for thousands of years, and the risk of an accident.

In fusion, light nuclei, such as isotopes of hydrogen, join to form a heavier nucleus, as in the Sun. The nuclei repel each other because they are positively charged, so a temperature of about 10^8 K is needed to give them enough energy to come close. At that temperature matter is a plasma, and it must be confined by magnetic fields, since no container can hold it. Fusion releases more energy per gram than fission, and it produces less long-lived waste, but a stable and productive fusion reactor has not yet been achieved.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Mass and energy.** A reaction has a mass defect of 0.20 u. How much energy is released?
1. Use the conversion 1 u = 931.5 MeV.
2. E = 0.20 x 931.5 MeV = 186.3 MeV.
3. The energy is about 186 MeV for a single nucleus, compared with a few eV in a chemical reaction.

**Example 2: Conservation in fission.** In U-235 + n -> Ba-141 + Kr-92 + x n, find x.
1. The mass number on the left is 235 + 1 = 236.
2. On the right it is 141 + 92 + x = 233 + x, so x = 3. The atomic numbers agree: 92 on the left and 56 + 36 = 92 on the right.
3. Three neutrons are released, and they can each start another fission.

**Example 3: Control rods.** What happens to the reaction rate when the control rods are inserted?
1. The rods contain a material that absorbs neutrons.
2. Fewer neutrons are left to cause fission, so fewer nuclei split in each generation.
3. The rate falls, and with the rods fully in, the chain reaction stops.

## Common mistakes

Students often confuse fission with fusion. Fission splits a heavy nucleus, and fusion joins light nuclei. Another error is to say that control rods slow the neutrons, when they absorb them. The moderator is the part that slows the neutrons. The third mistake is to forget that fusion needs a very high temperature, so that the nuclei can overcome their mutual repulsion.
