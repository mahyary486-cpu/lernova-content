# Potential Energy

Not all energy is visible as motion. A book on a high shelf, a stretched rubber band and a compressed spring all store energy because of their position or shape. This stored energy is called potential energy, because it has the potential to be changed into kinetic energy. When the book falls or the spring is released, the stored energy turns into motion. This lesson covers the two kinds that appear most often: gravitational and elastic potential energy.

## Key ideas

Gravitational potential energy is the energy stored by an object because of its height in a gravitational field. Near Earth's surface it is PE = m g h, with g = 9.8 m/s^2 and h the height above a reference level. The reference level is a free choice. You may take the floor, the table or the ground as the place where PE equals zero, as long as you use the same level for the whole problem. Only changes in potential energy have physical meaning, and the change does not depend on where you put the zero.

When you lift an object at constant speed, your work against gravity is stored as potential energy. The amount depends only on the vertical height gained, not on the path. Carrying a box up a long gentle ramp and lifting it straight up through the same height store the same energy. The ramp requires a smaller force over a longer distance, but the product, the energy, is the same. Therefore the height in the formula must always be the vertical height, never the length along a slope.

Elastic potential energy is stored in a stretched or compressed spring. It is PE = 1/2 k x^2, where k is the spring constant in newtons per meter, a measure of how stiff the spring is, and x is the stretch or compression from the natural length, in meters. Because x is squared, doubling the stretch makes the stored energy four times larger. Both kinds of potential energy are measured in joules, and both can be changed into kinetic energy and back, which sets the stage for conservation of energy in the next lesson.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Height.** A 4.0 kg object is held 6.0 m above the floor. Find its gravitational potential energy relative to the floor.
1. Use PE = m g h.
2. Substitute: PE = 4.0 x 9.8 x 6.0.
3. The potential energy is 235.2 J, about 235 J.

**Example 2: A spring.** A spring with k = 250 N/m is stretched by 0.12 m. Find the stored energy.
1. Use PE = 1/2 k x^2.
2. First square the stretch: 0.12^2 = 0.0144.
3. Then PE = 0.5 x 250 x 0.0144 = 1.8 J.

**Example 3: A ramp.** A 2.0 kg block is carried up a 3.0 m long ramp that makes 30 degrees with the ground. Find the gain in potential energy.
1. The vertical height gained is h = 3.0 x sin(30) = 3.0 x 0.5 = 1.5 m.
2. The gain in potential energy is m g h = 2.0 x 9.8 x 1.5.
3. The change is 29.4 J. The length of the ramp was used only to find the height.

## Common mistakes

Students often use the length of a ramp instead of the vertical height in m g h. Another common mistake is to forget to square x in the spring formula, or to forget the factor 1/2. Some think that potential energy has one absolute value, but it depends on the reference level chosen; only differences are meaningful. Finally, keep the units consistent: a stretch of 12 cm must be changed to 0.12 m before it is used, otherwise the answer is wrong by a large factor.
