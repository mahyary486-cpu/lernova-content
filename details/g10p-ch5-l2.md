# Impulse and the Impulse-Momentum Theorem

A force that acts for some time changes the momentum of an object. A tennis racket hits a ball for only a few thousandths of a second, yet it changes the ball's momentum greatly. The product of the force and the time for which it acts is called impulse. The idea explains many everyday facts: why you bend your knees when you land from a jump, why a cricket fielder pulls the hands back while catching, and why cars have airbags and crumple zones.

## Key ideas

Impulse is defined as J = F delta t, the force multiplied by the time during which it acts. The unit is the newton second (N s), and since 1 N = 1 kg m/s^2, we have 1 N s = 1 kg m/s, the same unit as momentum. This is not an accident: the two quantities are linked by the impulse-momentum theorem, which says that the impulse of the net force equals the change in momentum, J = delta p. Written in full, F delta t = m vf - m vi.

Solving for the force gives the average force during the contact: F = delta p / delta t. This formula explains the role of time. If the change in momentum is fixed, then a longer contact time gives a smaller force, and a shorter time gives a larger force. A ball that is caught with stiff hands stops in a very short time and stings; a ball that is caught with hands moving back stops in a longer time and the force is much smaller, although the change in momentum is the same.

Impulse is a vector, so its sign matters. When an object rebounds, its velocity reverses, and the change in momentum is larger than for simply stopping. If a ball arrives with velocity +20 m/s and leaves with -30 m/s, the change in velocity is -50 m/s, which is the sum of the two speeds, not their difference. This is why a bouncing ball receives a larger impulse from a wall than a ball that sticks to it.

Safety devices use the theorem directly. In a crash, a passenger must lose all of the forward momentum, whatever happens. An airbag, a crumple zone or a bent knee spreads the loss over a longer time, so by F = delta p / delta t the force, and the damage, are smaller.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: A kick.** A 0.50 kg ball at rest is kicked, and it leaves at 12 m/s. Find the impulse given to the ball.
1. Use J = m(vf - vi) = 0.50 x (12 - 0).
2. The impulse is J = 6.0 N s in the direction of the kick.

**Example 2: Stopping a ball.** A 0.20 kg ball moving at 25 m/s is caught and stopped in 0.050 s. Find the average force on the ball.
1. The change in momentum has the size delta p = 0.20 x 25 = 5.0 kg m/s.
2. The force is F = delta p / delta t = 5.0 / 0.050.
3. The average force is 100 N, directed opposite to the motion of the ball.

**Example 3: A rebound.** A 0.060 kg tennis ball arrives at 20 m/s and leaves in the opposite direction at 30 m/s. The contact lasts 0.010 s. Find the average force.
1. Take the arrival direction as positive, so vi = +20 m/s and vf = -30 m/s.
2. The change in velocity is -30 - 20 = -50 m/s, so delta p = 0.060 x (-50) = -3.0 kg m/s.
3. The force is F = 3.0 / 0.010 = 300 N in size, pointing along the new direction of motion.

## Common mistakes

A frequent error is subtracting the speeds in a rebound problem, when the reversal of direction makes them add. Another is forgetting that impulse is a vector and dropping its sign. Students also confuse impulse (N s) with force (N) by leaving out the time. Finally, do not think that an airbag reduces the change in momentum; it cannot, since the passenger still stops. It reduces the force by increasing the time over which the momentum is lost.
