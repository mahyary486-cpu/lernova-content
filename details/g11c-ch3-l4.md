# Osmosis and Osmotic Pressure

If you put a slice of fresh cucumber in salty water, it shrinks and becomes soft, because water flows out of its cells. If you put a dried raisin in pure water, it swells up. Both effects are examples of osmosis, a process that is essential for every living cell, and one that engineers use to make drinking water from the sea. This lesson explains the direction of the flow, how to calculate the pressure that it produces, and how it is used.

## Key ideas

Osmosis is the movement of solvent through a semipermeable membrane, which lets the solvent pass but blocks the solute. The solvent moves toward the side with the higher solute concentration, which tends to equalize the concentrations on the two sides. It is the solvent that moves, and not the solute. Water is lost from the side where it is more abundant, and gained where there is more dissolved material.

The osmotic pressure is the pressure that must be applied to the more concentrated solution to stop the flow of solvent into it. It is given by pi = i M R T. Here M is the molarity, R = 0.0821 L atm/(mol K) when the pressure is in atmospheres, T is the temperature in kelvins and i is the van't Hoff factor. The formula looks like the ideal gas law, because the dissolved particles in a dilute solution behave in a similar way to the particles of a gas, and the pressure depends on the number of particles per liter. Even a small difference in concentration gives a large pressure, and a 0.10 M solution already gives more than 2 atm.

To compare two solutions, we use special words. Solutions with the same total concentration of particles are isotonic. A solution with a lower concentration than the cell is hypotonic, and one with a higher concentration is hypertonic. A cell placed in a hypotonic solution takes in water and swells, and it may burst. In a hypertonic solution it loses water and shrinks. Hospitals therefore use isotonic saline for intravenous fluids, to protect the blood cells. The same principle explains why salt and sugar preserve food: bacteria lose water to the concentrated surroundings and cannot grow.

Reverse osmosis uses the reverse process. If a pressure greater than the osmotic pressure is applied to the salty side, water is forced through the membrane to the pure side, and in this way fresh water is obtained from seawater in desalination plants.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Osmotic pressure.** Find the osmotic pressure of a 0.10 M sugar solution at 300 K, using R = 0.0821.
1. Sugar does not dissociate, so i = 1.
2. pi = i M R T = 1 x 0.10 x 0.0821 x 300.
3. pi = 2.46 atm.

**Example 2: Direction of flow.** Pure water and a salt solution are separated by a semipermeable membrane. In which direction does the water move?
1. The solvent moves toward the higher solute concentration.
2. The water flows from the pure water into the salt solution.

**Example 3: Red blood cells.** A red blood cell is placed in pure water.
1. The water is hypotonic compared with the inside of the cell, which holds dissolved particles.
2. Water enters the cell, which swells and may burst.

## Common mistakes

A very common error is to think that the solute moves in osmosis, but it is the solvent that moves through the membrane. Another is to use Celsius for the temperature in the formula, since T must be in kelvins. Students also mix up the words hypotonic and hypertonic. A simple memory aid is that hypo means lower, so a hypotonic solution has a lower concentration of solute than the cell, and hyper means higher. Finally, do not forget the van't Hoff factor for ionic solutes, such as NaCl, which doubles the pressure compared with a nonelectrolyte at the same molarity.
