# Scalars and Vectors

Some quantities are fully described by a number and a unit. Others need a direction as well. If someone says that a room is 5 m wide, nothing more is needed. But if someone says that a ship sails 20 km, you still cannot tell where it ends up until you know in which direction it went. Physics sorts quantities into scalars and vectors, and this sorting decides how we add them, compare them and draw them.

## Key ideas

A scalar has magnitude (size) only. Distance, speed, time, mass, temperature and energy are scalars. They add by ordinary arithmetic: 3 kg plus 4 kg is always 7 kg. A vector has both magnitude and direction. Displacement, velocity, acceleration and force are vectors. Their addition depends on direction, so 3 m east plus 4 m north is not 7 m.

The pairs distance and displacement, and speed and velocity, show the difference clearly. Distance is the total path length and is a scalar; displacement is the straight-line change in position from start to finish and is a vector. Speed is distance divided by time and is a scalar; velocity is displacement divided by time and is a vector. For this reason a runner who finishes a lap has a large speed but zero average velocity.

In diagrams a vector is drawn as an arrow. The length of the arrow shows the magnitude, and the way the arrow points shows the direction. Two vectors are equal when they have the same magnitude and the same direction, no matter where they are drawn. The vector -A has the same magnitude as A but points the opposite way. Multiplying a vector by a positive number changes only its magnitude, and multiplying by -1 reverses it.

In one dimension a plus or minus sign is enough to show the direction, for example +20 m/s for east and -20 m/s for west. In two dimensions a single sign is not enough. When two vectors are perpendicular, the magnitude of their sum is found with the Pythagorean theorem, and the direction angle comes from tan(theta) = opposite / adjacent. This is the first step toward the component method of the next lesson.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Perpendicular displacements.** A student walks 6.0 m east and then 8.0 m north. Find the distance, the displacement and its direction.
1. The distance is the sum of the legs: 6.0 + 8.0 = 14.0 m.
2. The two legs are perpendicular, so the displacement magnitude is sqrt(6.0^2 + 8.0^2) = sqrt(100) = 10.0 m.
3. The direction is theta = tan^-1(8.0 / 6.0) = 53 degrees north of east.

**Example 2: A round trip.** A jogger runs once around a 400 m track and stops at the starting line after 80 s. Find the average speed and the average velocity.
1. The distance is 400 m, and the displacement is 0 m because the final position equals the start.
2. Average speed = 400 / 80 = 5.0 m/s.
3. Average velocity = 0 / 80 = 0 m/s. The two quantities differ because one ignores direction and the other does not.

**Example 3: Back and forth.** A hiker walks 5.0 km east and then 8.0 km west. Find the distance and the displacement.
1. The distance adds the sizes: 5.0 + 8.0 = 13.0 km.
2. Take east as positive. The displacement is +5.0 + (-8.0) = -3.0 km.
3. The hiker ends 3.0 km west of the starting point, even though 13.0 km were walked.

## Common mistakes

Students often say that distance and displacement are the same, but displacement can be smaller than the distance or even zero. Another error is to read a negative sign as "slower"; the sign only shows direction, and a velocity of -20 m/s is just as fast as +20 m/s. A third error is to call speed a vector, when speed has no direction at all. The most costly mistake is adding perpendicular vectors by plain arithmetic (6 + 8 = 14) instead of using the Pythagorean theorem, which gives 10.
