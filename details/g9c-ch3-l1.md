# Solids, Liquids and Gases

Ice, liquid water and steam are the same substance in three different states. What changes from one to the other is not the kind of particles, which are always water molecules, but how those particles are arranged and how they move. Understanding the states of matter is the first step toward explaining why materials behave as they do, from a gas filling a balloon to a solid keeping its shape.

## Key ideas

A solid has a definite shape and a definite volume. Its particles are packed closely together in fixed positions, held by strong attractions. They are not at rest; they vibrate back and forth around their positions, but they cannot move from place to place. This is why a solid keeps its shape.

A liquid has a definite volume but no definite shape. Its particles are still close together, but they have enough energy to slide past one another, so a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container. The volume stays the same, since the particles remain in contact. A gas has neither a definite shape nor a definite volume. Its particles are far apart, move quickly in all directions, and have only very weak attractions, so a gas spreads out to fill any container completely.

The spacing of the particles explains how easily each state can be compressed. A gas has a lot of empty space between its particles, so it can be squeezed into a smaller volume, which is why a bicycle pump works and why air can be stored in a tank. The particles of solids and liquids are already almost touching, so they can hardly be compressed. This idea is used in hydraulic machines, which use liquids to transmit force.

There is also a fourth state, plasma, which is a very hot gas whose atoms have lost some electrons. It is found in stars, lightning and neon signs, and it is actually the most common state of visible matter in the universe, even though it is rare on Earth.

Remember that gases are matter, so they have mass. A balloon filled with air has more mass than an empty, flat one. Air seems weightless because we live at the bottom of a huge ocean of it and do not feel it, but a closed container of air placed on a sensitive balance does show its mass.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Sorting states.** Classify a rock, juice and air.
1. The rock keeps its own shape and volume, so it is a solid.
2. Juice takes the shape of the glass but keeps its volume, so it is a liquid.
3. Air spreads out and fills any container, so it is a gas.

**Example 2: Compression.** Why can the air in a syringe be pushed in, but water cannot?
1. The particles of air are far apart, with a lot of empty space between them, so they can be pushed closer.
2. The particles of water are already close together, so there is hardly any space to push them into, and water is almost incompressible.

**Example 3: Particle motion.** Do the particles of a solid move?
1. They cannot move from one place to another, because they are held in fixed positions.
2. They vibrate around those positions, so they are still moving. When the solid is heated, the vibrations get faster.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to think that the particles in a solid do not move at all. They vibrate constantly. Another mistake is to think that a gas weighs nothing, but a gas has mass like any other matter. Finally, students sometimes think that a liquid can be compressed as easily as a gas, but its particles are as close together as those of a solid.
