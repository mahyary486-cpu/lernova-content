# Valence Electrons and the Octet Rule

Atoms bond with each other to reach more stable arrangements of electrons. Only the outermost electrons, the valence electrons, take part in this process, so counting them is the first step in understanding any bond. The octet rule then tells us what arrangement the atoms are aiming for, and it lets us predict which ions an element will form.

## Key ideas

The valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost energy level. For main-group elements the count follows from the group number: group 1 has 1 valence electron, group 2 has 2, group 13 has 3, group 14 has 4, group 15 has 5, group 16 has 6, group 17 has 7 and group 18 has 8, with helium as the exception, having 2. The number can also be read from the electron configuration, by adding the electrons in the highest value of n. Inner electrons are held tightly and do not take part in bonding.

The octet rule states that atoms tend to gain, lose or share electrons until they have 8 valence electrons, the arrangement of a noble gas. Hydrogen and helium follow the duet rule, since their first energy level holds only 2 electrons. The rule explains why metals and nonmetals behave as they do. Metals have few valence electrons, so they lose them to form cations, which are positive ions. Nonmetals have many, so they gain electrons to form anions, which are negative ions. Atoms that become ions only move electrons. The number of protons never changes.

An ion with the same number of electrons as a noble gas is said to be isoelectronic with it. To count the electrons in an ion, use the atomic number and the charge: electrons = Z - charge. For a cation this means subtracting the size of the charge, and for an anion it means adding it.

An electron-dot (Lewis) symbol shows the valence electrons as dots around the symbol of the element, placing one dot on each side before pairing any. These symbols are used to show how atoms share or transfer electrons.

Some molecules do not follow the octet rule. Boron in BF3 has only 6 electrons around it, and elements in period 3 or below, such as sulfur in SF6, can hold more than 8 electrons, because they have empty d orbitals available. The rule is a very good guide for the first two periods, and a useful one beyond them.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Valence electrons of sulfur (Z = 16).** Find the number of valence electrons and the ion formed.
1. The configuration is 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p4.
2. The outer level, n = 3, holds 2 + 4 = 6 electrons.
3. Sulfur has 6 valence electrons (group 16), and it gains 2 electrons to form S 2-.

**Example 2: Electrons in an aluminum ion.** How many electrons are in Al 3+?
1. Aluminum has Z = 13 and 3 valence electrons (3s2 3p1).
2. It loses these 3 electrons, and the second level, with its octet, becomes the outer one.
3. The ion has 13 - 3 = 10 electrons, the same number as neon.

**Example 3: A noble gas for Cl-.** Which noble gas is isoelectronic with Cl-?
1. Chlorine has Z = 17, so Cl- has 17 + 1 = 18 electrons.
2. Argon has 18 electrons.
3. Cl- is isoelectronic with argon.

## Common mistakes

Students often count all the electrons instead of only the outermost ones. Another mistake is to forget that a cation has fewer electrons than the neutral atom, and an anion has more. Some think that atoms gain or lose protons when they form ions, but only electrons move. Finally, remember that the octet rule has exceptions, such as BF3 and SF6.
