# Acceleration and Changing Velocity

Whenever the velocity of an object changes, we say that the object accelerates. In everyday speech the word means only speeding up, but in physics it is much wider. Pressing the gas pedal, pressing the brake, and turning the steering wheel all make a car accelerate, because each one changes the velocity, either its size or its direction. This lesson shows how to measure acceleration, how to read its sign, and why the sign alone does not tell you whether an object is speeding up.

## Key ideas

Average acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time it takes: a = delta v / delta t = (v - v0) / t. It is a vector, so it has a direction. The SI unit is meters per second squared (m/s^2). This unit is easy to understand if you read it slowly: an acceleration of 4 m/s^2 means that the velocity changes by 4 m/s during every second. When the acceleration is constant, this rearranges into v = v0 + at, which gives the velocity at any later time.

To know what the acceleration does to the motion, compare its direction with the direction of the velocity. If the two point the same way, the object speeds up. If they point in opposite ways, the object slows down. This comparison is the real rule, and it replaces the tempting but wrong idea that positive means faster and negative means slower. When a car moves in the negative direction and has a negative acceleration, both point the same way, so the car speeds up even though the acceleration is negative.

Zero velocity does not mean zero acceleration. A ball thrown straight up is momentarily at rest at the top of its flight, yet its velocity is still changing, because gravity keeps pulling it down with 9.8 m/s^2. The velocity is simply passing through zero on its way from upward to downward. Acceleration depends on how the velocity changes, not on the value of the velocity at one instant.

Finally, always subtract in the same order: final velocity minus initial velocity. This order automatically produces the correct sign. If an object reverses direction, the change in velocity is larger than either speed alone, because the velocity must first drop to zero and then grow in the opposite direction.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Speeding up.** A cyclist goes from 5.0 m/s to 25 m/s in 4.0 s. What is the acceleration?
1. The change in velocity is 25 - 5.0 = 20 m/s.
2. Divide by the time: a = 20 / 4.0.
3. The acceleration is +5.0 m/s^2, so the cyclist gains 5.0 m/s every second.

**Example 2: Braking to rest.** A car slows from 20 m/s to rest in 5.0 s. What is the acceleration?
1. The change in velocity is 0 - 20 = -20 m/s.
2. Divide by the time: a = -20 / 5.0 = -4.0 m/s^2.
3. The negative sign shows that the acceleration points opposite to the motion, which is why the car slows down.

**Example 3: Reversing direction.** A ball hits a wall. Its velocity changes from +8.0 m/s to -6.0 m/s in 2.0 s. What is the average acceleration?
1. The change in velocity is -6.0 - 8.0 = -14 m/s. Notice that the sizes of the two velocities add, because the ball must stop and then go back.
2. Divide by the time: a = -14 / 2.0.
3. The acceleration is -7.0 m/s^2, directed away from the wall.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to subtract in the wrong order, using initial minus final; this flips the sign and the direction of the acceleration. Another is to believe that a negative acceleration always means slowing down, when the answer depends on the sign of the velocity too. A third mistake is to say that an object with zero velocity must have zero acceleration, which fails at the top of a throw. Finally, students sometimes put km/h and seconds in the same formula. A car going from 0 to 100 km/h in 8.0 s has first to be converted: 100 km/h is 27.8 m/s, and then a = 27.8 / 8.0, which is about 3.5 m/s^2.
