# Water Chemistry, Green Chemistry and Sustainability

Chemistry helps to clean water, and through green chemistry it helps to design processes that make less waste in the first place. Clean water is essential, but its quality is affected by minerals, nutrients and pollutants. At the same time, the chemical industry itself is under pressure to use fewer resources and to produce less harm. This final lesson looks at both ideas, and ends with a measure that lets us compare reactions fairly.

## Key ideas

Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium ions, Ca 2+ and Mg 2+. It makes soap form a scum, and it leaves scale in kettles and pipes. Temporary hardness is caused by hydrogen carbonates, and it is removed by boiling, which decomposes them: Ca(HCO3)2 -> CaCO3 + H2O + CO2. The calcium carbonate precipitates as scale. Permanent hardness is caused by sulfates and chlorides, which are not removed by boiling, and it needs treatment such as ion exchange.

Eutrophication is the enrichment of water with nutrients, usually nitrates and phosphates from fertilizers and sewage. The nutrients make algae grow quickly. When the algae die, bacteria decompose them and use up the dissolved oxygen, so fish and other organisms die. The biochemical oxygen demand, BOD, measures the oxygen that microorganisms need to break down the organic matter in water, and a high BOD signals pollution. Heavy metals such as mercury and lead are another danger, because they are not broken down, and they accumulate in living things, so the concentration rises along the food chain.

The treatment of drinking water has several stages. Coagulation adds a salt, such as aluminum sulfate, which makes small particles clump together. Filtration removes the clumps through sand. Chlorination kills bacteria and viruses.

Green chemistry is a set of principles for reducing the harm of chemical processes. They include preventing waste, using safer solvents, using catalysts instead of large amounts of reagents, using renewable raw materials, and saving energy. A useful measure is the atom economy: atom economy = mass of the desired product / total mass of the reactants x 100 percent. It differs from the percent yield. The percent yield tells how well an experiment performed, and the atom economy shows how much of the starting material ends up in the product, even in a perfect experiment. An addition reaction has an atom economy of 100 percent, because all atoms are used. A reaction that makes a by-product has a lower one.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Atom economy of hydration.** In C2H4 + H2O -> C2H5OH, the reactants have masses 28 + 18 = 46 and the product has mass 46.
1. All the atoms of the reactants end up in the single product.
2. Atom economy = 46 / 46 x 100 percent.
3. The atom economy is 100 percent.

**Example 2: Atom economy of limestone decomposition.** CaCO3 (100) -> CaO (56) + CO2 (44). Find the atom economy for the production of CaO.
1. The desired product has a mass of 56, out of 100 for the reactant.
2. Atom economy = 56 / 100 x 100 percent = 56 percent.
3. The other 44 percent is carbon dioxide, which is a by-product.

**Example 3: How is temporary hardness removed?**
1. The water is boiled, and calcium hydrogen carbonate decomposes.
2. The equation is Ca(HCO3)2 -> CaCO3 + H2O + CO2.
3. The calcium carbonate is insoluble, so it precipitates and can be removed.

## Common mistakes

Students often confuse percent yield with atom economy, but the first depends on the experiment and the second on the equation. Another error is to say that boiling removes permanent hardness, which it does not. The third mistake is to forget that eutrophication causes oxygen depletion, since it is the death of the oxygen-using organisms, and not the algae themselves, that harms the water.
