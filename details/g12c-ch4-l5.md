# Nucleic Acids and Metabolism

The four-letter code of DNA stores the instructions for making every protein in the body. Nucleic acids are the third family of biological polymers, after carbohydrates and proteins, and they carry information instead of providing energy or structure. In this lesson we look at the structure of DNA and RNA, at the way the code becomes a protein, and at the way cells handle energy in metabolism.

## Key ideas

A nucleotide has three parts: a sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogen base. Nucleotides join through the sugar and phosphate to make a long chain, which is the backbone of the nucleic acid, with the bases pointing out to the side. DNA has the sugar deoxyribose, and the bases adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C). RNA has the sugar ribose, and uracil (U) in place of thymine. DNA is a double helix of two strands, held together by hydrogen bonds between the bases. A pairs with T, with two hydrogen bonds, and G pairs with C, with three. Because of these fixed pairs, one strand determines the other, which is what allows DNA to be copied.

The sequence of bases is a code. A group of three bases, called a codon, specifies one amino acid. In transcription, a section of DNA is copied into a strand of RNA. In translation, the RNA is read by the ribosome, and amino acids are joined in the order of the codons to make a protein. Because three bases code for one amino acid, a protein of 100 amino acids needs at least 300 bases, plus a stop codon.

Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions of a cell. The energy currency is ATP. When ATP is hydrolyzed to ADP and phosphate, it releases about 30 kJ per mole, which drives reactions that need energy. Cells then regenerate ATP from food. Respiration oxidizes glucose: C6H12O6 + 6 O2 -> 6 CO2 + 6 H2O, and the energy released is used to make ATP. Photosynthesis is the reverse reaction. Plants use light energy to build glucose from carbon dioxide and water: 6 CO2 + 6 H2O -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2. In this way, the oxygen that animals use and the glucose that they eat are produced by plants, and the cycle of carbon and oxygen is closed.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Base pairing.** One DNA strand reads A-T-G-C. What is the complementary strand?
1. A always pairs with T, and G always pairs with C.
2. The partners of A, T, G and C are T, A, C and G.
3. The complementary strand reads T-A-C-G.

**Example 2: Number of bases for a protein.** How many bases code for a protein of 100 amino acids? Ignore the stop codon.
1. Each amino acid needs one codon, and each codon has 3 bases.
2. The number of bases is 100 x 3 = 300.

**Example 3: Oxygen from photosynthesis.** How many moles of oxygen form when 1 mol of glucose is made?
1. The balanced equation is 6 CO2 + 6 H2O -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2.
2. The ratio of O2 to glucose is 6 to 1.
3. One mole of glucose is accompanied by 6 mol of O2, which is 192 g.

## Common mistakes

Students often pair A with G, but A pairs with T, and G pairs with C. Another is to write uracil in DNA, when uracil belongs to RNA and thymine belongs to DNA. The third mistake is to confuse transcription with translation. Transcription copies DNA into RNA, in the nucleus, and translation reads RNA to build a protein.
