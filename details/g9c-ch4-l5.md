# Separating Mixtures

Chemists, recyclers and water treatment plants all depend on separating mixtures. Sea water must be turned into drinking water, crude oil must be split into fuels, and metals must be sorted from rubbish. The right method depends on one question: in which physical property do the substances in the mixture differ? Since the substances in a mixture are not chemically combined, each one keeps its own properties, and these differences can be used to pull them apart.

## Key ideas

Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid. The mixture is poured through filter paper, which has tiny holes. The liquid, and anything dissolved in it, passes through, while the solid is caught. The property that differs is the particle size, so filtration works for suspensions such as sand in water, and it does not work for dissolved substances.

Evaporation recovers a dissolved solid from a solution. The solution is heated until the solvent turns to vapor, and the solid is left behind. If the solvent is also wanted, distillation is used. In distillation the solution is boiled, the vapor is passed through a cooled tube, a condenser, and the liquid that collects is the pure solvent. Distillation can also separate two liquids that mix, because they have different boiling points. The liquid with the lower boiling point turns to vapor first and is collected first. This is used to obtain pure water from sea water, and to separate fuels from crude oil.

Chromatography separates substances that are dissolved in the same solvent, such as the dyes in ink. A spot of the mixture is placed on absorbent paper, and the edge of the paper is placed in a solvent, which rises up the paper and carries the dyes with it. Each substance moves at its own speed, because they differ in how strongly they stick to the paper and how well they dissolve, so they end up at different heights.

Other methods use other properties. A magnet separates magnetic materials, like iron, from nonmagnetic ones. A separating funnel separates two liquids that do not mix, such as oil and water, because one forms a layer on top of the other, and the lower one can be drained off. Decanting pours off a liquid from a settled solid.

Complex mixtures need a combination of methods, in a sensible order. The usual approach is to first remove the insoluble solid by filtration, and then separate the dissolved parts by evaporation, distillation or chromatography.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Sand and salt.** How can a mixture of sand and salt be separated?
1. Add water and stir. The salt dissolves, but the sand does not.
2. Filter the mixture to catch the sand, and then evaporate the water from the filtered liquid to get the salt.

**Example 2: Ink colors.** How can the dyes in black ink be separated?
1. Place a drop of ink near the bottom of a strip of filter paper, and put the end of the strip in water so that the water moves up.
2. Each dye moves at a different rate, so the colors separate into bands. This method is called chromatography.

**Example 3: Alcohol and water.** How can alcohol be separated from water?
1. Alcohol boils at a lower temperature than water, about 78 degrees C compared to 100 degrees C.
2. Heat the mixture gently, and collect and condense the alcohol vapor that comes off first. This is distillation.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to use filtration for dissolved substances, which pass through the filter paper. Another is to use distillation for liquids with the same, or nearly the same, boiling point. Finally, students do the steps in the wrong order, such as evaporating the water before filtering out the sand.
