# Colligative Properties: Vapor Pressure, Boiling Point and Freezing Point

Salt spread on an icy road makes the ice melt even when the air is below 0 degrees C, and antifreeze keeps a car engine from freezing in winter and from boiling in summer. In both cases, a dissolved substance changes the physical properties of a liquid. These changes are called colligative properties, and their special feature is that they depend on the number of dissolved particles, and not on what the particles are.

## Key ideas

A colligative property depends on how many solute particles are present in a given amount of solvent. The main ones are the lowering of the vapor pressure, the rise of the boiling point and the fall of the freezing point. The reason is the same for all of them. Solute particles occupy part of the surface of the liquid and get in the way of the solvent molecules, so fewer solvent molecules escape as vapor. The vapor pressure is lower, the liquid must be heated more to boil, and it must be cooled more to freeze.

Raoult's law describes the vapor pressure: P = X_solvent x P0, where X is the mole fraction of the solvent and P0 is the vapor pressure of the pure solvent. Because X is less than 1 in a solution, P is lower than P0.

The changes in boiling and freezing points are calculated with delta T_b = i K_b m and delta T_f = i K_f m. Here m is the molality, K_b and K_f are constants of the solvent, and i is the van't Hoff factor. For water, K_b = 0.512 degrees C kg/mol and K_f = 1.86 degrees C kg/mol. The molality is used, and not the molarity, because it does not change with temperature.

The van't Hoff factor is the number of particles that one formula unit gives in solution. Sugar does not break into ions, so i = 1. Sodium chloride gives two ions, so i = 2, and calcium chloride gives three, so i = 3. This is why salt melts ice better than sugar at the same molality. In practice the real value of i is a little smaller, because ions attract each other, but the whole numbers are used in this course. The boiling point rises, so delta T_b is added to the normal boiling point, and the freezing point falls, so delta T_f is subtracted from the normal freezing point.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Freezing point of a sugar solution.** A 1.0 m solution of sugar in water has i = 1.
1. delta T_f = i K_f m = 1 x 1.86 x 1.0 = 1.86 degrees C.
2. The freezing point is 0 - 1.86 = -1.86 degrees C.

**Example 2: Freezing point of a salt solution.** A 1.0 m solution of NaCl has i = 2.
1. delta T_f = 2 x 1.86 x 1.0 = 3.72 degrees C.
2. The freezing point is -3.72 degrees C, twice as low as for sugar.

**Example 3: Boiling point.** A 2.0 m solution of sugar in water.
1. delta T_b = 1 x 0.512 x 2.0 = 1.02 degrees C.
2. The boiling point is 100 + 1.02 = 101.02 degrees C.

## Common mistakes

The most frequent mistake is to forget the van't Hoff factor for an ionic compound, which gives an answer that is too small by half or by two thirds. Another is to use the molarity instead of the molality in the formulas. Students also add delta T to the freezing point, when the freezing point is lowered and the change must be subtracted. Finally, remember that the size of the effect depends on the number of particles, so 1.0 m CaCl2 lowers the freezing point three times as much as 1.0 m sugar.
