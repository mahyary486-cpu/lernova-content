# Useful elements: nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine and fluorine

This topic describes five important nonmetal elements, where they are found and how people use them.

## Key ideas

Nitrogen (N): Nitrogen makes up about 78% of air as N2 gas. The N2 molecule has a very strong triple bond, so nitrogen gas is unreactive. Plants need nitrogen to make proteins, but most plants cannot use N2 from the air. Industry makes ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen (N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3), and ammonia is used to make fertilizers such as urea and ammonium nitrate. Liquid nitrogen is very cold (about -196 °C) and is used to freeze and store materials.

Phosphorus (P): Phosphorus is found in rocks as phosphates. It is needed for DNA, for energy molecules in cells, and for bones and teeth. Phosphate fertilizers help plants grow strong roots. White phosphorus catches fire easily in air, and red phosphorus is used on the striking strip of matchboxes.

Sulfur (S): Sulfur is a yellow solid found near volcanoes and in underground deposits. Its most important use is to make sulfuric acid, which is used in fertilizers, batteries and many industries. Sulfur is also used in vulcanizing rubber, which makes rubber stronger. Burning fuels that contain sulfur forms sulfur dioxide (SO2), a gas that causes acid rain.

Chlorine (Cl): Chlorine is a yellow-green, poisonous gas (Cl2). It is a reactive halogen. It is used to kill germs in drinking water and swimming pools, to make bleach, and to make plastics such as PVC. Chlorine is found in nature mainly as sodium chloride (table salt) in seawater and salt deposits.

Fluorine (F): Fluorine is the most reactive nonmetal. It is found in minerals such as fluorite (CaF2). Small amounts of fluoride compounds in toothpaste make tooth enamel stronger against decay. Fluorine compounds are also used to make non-stick coatings such as Teflon.

Safety: Chlorine gas and many sulfur compounds are harmful. Substances must be used in the right amounts. Too much fertilizer can wash into rivers and harm water life.

## Examples

Example 1: Nitrogen percentage in urea, CO(NH2)2.
- Molar mass: C 12 + O 16 + 2 × (N 14 + 2 × H 1) = 12 + 16 + 2 × 16 = 60 g/mol.
- Mass of nitrogen: 2 × 14 = 28 g.
- Percentage = 28 ÷ 60 × 100 ≈ 46.7%.
- So 100 kg of urea contains about 46.7 kg of nitrogen.

Example 2: Comparing two fertilizers.
- Ammonium nitrate, NH4NO3: molar mass = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80 g/mol. Nitrogen = 28 g.
- Percentage = 28 ÷ 80 × 100 = 35%.
- Urea has 46.7% and ammonium nitrate has 35%, so urea gives more nitrogen per kilogram.

Example 3: Ammonia from nitrogen.
- Equation: N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3.
- Molar mass of N2 = 28 g/mol. Molar mass of NH3 = 14 + 3 = 17 g/mol.
- 1 mol N2 (28 g) gives 2 mol NH3 = 34 g.
- So 56 g of nitrogen gives 68 g of ammonia.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that plants can use nitrogen gas directly from the air. Most need nitrogen compounds in the soil.
- Mixing up the formulas Cl (an atom) and Cl2 (the gas molecule).
- Forgetting to multiply by the number of atoms when working out percentage mass, for example using 14 instead of 28 for urea.
- Believing that all fertilizer is harmless. Excess fertilizer pollutes water.

## Remember

- Nitrogen and phosphorus are key elements in fertilizers; sulfur makes sulfuric acid; chlorine disinfects water; fluoride protects teeth.
- N2 is unreactive; fluorine is the most reactive nonmetal.
- Percentage of an element = (mass of the element in the formula ÷ molar mass) × 100.
