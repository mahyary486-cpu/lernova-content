# Ionic Bonds and Compounds

An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It usually forms when a metal transfers electrons to a nonmetal, as in table salt. The properties of ionic compounds, from their hardness to their behavior in water, follow directly from the way the ions are packed together, and they are different from the properties of the molecular substances that we meet later.

## Key ideas

When a metal atom reacts with a nonmetal atom, the metal loses its valence electrons and becomes a cation, and the nonmetal gains them and becomes an anion. The two kinds of ions attract each other strongly. In the solid, they do not form separate molecules. Instead, each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge in a repeating three-dimensional pattern called a crystal lattice. For this reason, the formula of an ionic compound is a formula unit, the lowest whole-number ratio of ions, such as NaCl or MgCl2. It does not describe a molecule.

The compound as a whole is neutral, so the total positive charge equals the total negative charge. The criss-cross method gives the formula quickly: the size of the charge of one ion becomes the subscript of the other ion, and then the ratio is reduced. Al 3+ and O 2- give Al2O3.

The lattice explains the properties. Ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points, because a lot of energy is needed to pull the ions apart. They are hard but brittle: if a layer shifts, ions of the same charge come face to face and repel, and the crystal shatters. Solids do not conduct electricity, because the ions are fixed in place, but molten salts and water solutions do, because the ions are then free to move.

The strength of the lattice is measured by the lattice energy, the energy released when gaseous ions come together to form a solid lattice. It is larger when the charges of the ions are larger and when the ions are smaller, because the attraction is stronger. Magnesium oxide, made of 2+ and 2- ions, has a much higher melting point than sodium chloride, made of 1+ and 1- ions.

Calculations with ionic compounds use the mole. The molar mass of a formula unit is the sum of the molar masses of all the atoms in it, and one mole of a compound contains 6.02 x 10^23 formula units.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Formula of magnesium chloride.** Find the formula.
1. Magnesium forms Mg 2+, and chlorine forms Cl-.
2. Two Cl- are needed to cancel one Mg 2+, because 2+ and 2 x (1-) add up to zero.
3. The formula is MgCl2.

**Example 2: Mass of 0.50 mol of CaCl2.** Find the mass.
1. The molar mass is 40.08 + 2 x 35.45 = 110.98 g/mol.
2. The mass is 0.50 mol x 110.98 g/mol = 55.5 g.
3. With two significant figures, this is 55 g.

**Example 3: Moles in 117 g of NaCl.** Find the amount and the number of formula units.
1. The molar mass is 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol.
2. The moles are 117 g / 58.44 g/mol = 2.00 mol.
3. The formula units number 2.00 x 6.02 x 10^23 = 1.20 x 10^24.

## Common mistakes

Students often call NaCl a molecule, but it is a lattice of ions. Another mistake is to write the charges inside the formula, such as Mg2Cl2 or Mg2+Cl-2. Some say that ionic solids conduct electricity, but the ions are fixed in place until the solid melts or dissolves. Finally, always reduce the ratio: Mg2O2 must be written MgO.
