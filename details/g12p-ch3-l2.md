# Magnetic Force on Currents and Magnetic Fields of Currents

A current in a coil of wire behaves like a bar magnet, and an iron core makes it much stronger. This discovery, that electric currents create magnetic fields and that magnetic fields push on currents, joined electricity and magnetism into a single subject. It is the basis of electromagnets, electric motors, loudspeakers and many measuring instruments.

## Key ideas

A current is a stream of moving charges, so a magnetic field pushes on a wire that carries current. For a straight wire of length L carrying a current I in a field B, the force is F = I L B sin(theta), where theta is the angle between the wire and the field. The force is perpendicular to both, and its direction follows the right-hand rule used for moving charges, with the fingers pointing along the current. The force is largest when the wire is perpendicular to the field.

In the opposite direction, a current produces a magnetic field. Around a long straight wire, the field lines are circles centered on the wire, and their direction is given by the right-hand grip rule: point the thumb along the current, and the fingers curl in the direction of the field. The field at a distance r from the wire is B = mu_0 I / (2 pi r), where mu_0 = 4 pi x 10^-7 T m/A is the permeability of free space. The field weakens in proportion to 1 / r, and the distance is measured from the wire.

Bending the wire into a circular loop of radius R concentrates the field: at the center, B = mu_0 I / (2 R). A long coil of many loops, called a solenoid, has a nearly uniform field inside, B = mu_0 n I, where n is the number of turns per unit length, not the total number of turns. Outside, the field is weak. The field of a solenoid looks like that of a bar magnet, with a north and a south end, and putting a piece of iron inside increases it greatly.

The two effects combine in the force between two parallel wires. Each wire creates a field at the position of the other, and each feels a force. Parallel currents in the same direction attract each other, and currents in opposite directions repel. This effect is used to define the ampere.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Force on a wire.** A 0.50 m wire carrying 4.0 A is perpendicular to a field of 0.20 T. Find the force on it.
1. Use F = I L B = 4.0 x 0.50 x 0.20, since sin(90) = 1.
2. The force is F = 0.40 N.

**Example 2: Field of a wire.** Find the magnetic field 0.050 m from a long straight wire carrying 10 A.
1. Use B = mu_0 I / (2 pi r) = 4 pi x 10^-7 x 10 / (2 pi x 0.050).
2. The factor pi cancels, leaving B = 2 x 10^-7 x 10 / 0.050.
3. The field is B = 4.0 x 10^-5 T, about the same as Earth's field.

**Example 3: A solenoid.** A solenoid has 1000 turns per meter and carries 2.0 A. Find the field inside.
1. Use B = mu_0 n I = 4 pi x 10^-7 x 1000 x 2.0.
2. The field is B = 2.5 x 10^-3 T.
3. Doubling the current would double the field.

## Common mistakes

Students often use a wrong value for mu_0, forgetting the factor 10^-7. Another mistake is to measure the distance from something other than the wire, or to forget that the field of a wire falls as 1 / r. A third is to use the total number of turns instead of the turns per meter in the solenoid formula. Finally, remember that currents in the same direction attract, which is the opposite of the rule for like electric charges.
