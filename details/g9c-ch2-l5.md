# Periodic Families and Trends

Knowing a few families on the periodic table lets you predict the behavior of many elements at once. If you know how sodium behaves, you can guess a lot about potassium, because they are in the same family. This lesson looks at the most important families and at the general difference between metals and nonmetals, and it shows how an atom's electron arrangement leads to the kind of ion it forms.

## Key ideas

The alkali metals are the elements of Group 1, such as lithium, sodium and potassium. They are soft enough to be cut with a knife and are very reactive, and they must be stored under oil to keep them from reacting with air and moisture. Each one has a single valence electron, which it loses easily, forming an ion with a 1+ charge. They get more reactive going down the group, because the outer electron is farther from the nucleus and easier to remove.

The alkaline earth metals are in Group 2, such as magnesium and calcium. They have 2 valence electrons, lose both of them and form 2+ ions. They are less reactive than the alkali metals, but they are still reactive metals. The halogens are in Group 17, including fluorine, chlorine and bromine. They are reactive nonmetals with 7 valence electrons. Each needs just one more electron to fill its outer shell, so it gains one and forms a 1- ion. The noble gases, in Group 18, such as helium, neon and argon, have full outer shells and are almost completely unreactive.

These examples show a general rule. Metals tend to lose electrons, and form positive ions. Nonmetals tend to gain electrons, and form negative ions. In both cases the atom ends up with a full outer shell, like that of a noble gas. The charge of the ion can be predicted from the group: Group 1 gives 1+, Group 2 gives 2+, and Group 17 gives 1-.

Metalloids, such as silicon, lie between metals and nonmetals and have some properties of each. Silicon conducts electricity better than a nonmetal but worse than a metal, and this makes it a semiconductor, which is the base of computer chips. Finally, it is important to remember that reactivity is not the same for all metals. Sodium and potassium react violently with water, while gold and platinum hardly react at all.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Family names.** Name the family of sodium and the family of chlorine.
1. Sodium is in Group 1, which is the family of the alkali metals.
2. Chlorine is in Group 17, which is the family of the halogens.

**Example 2: Why helium is unreactive.** Helium has 2 electrons. Why does it not react?
1. Its only shell can hold a maximum of 2 electrons, so it is full.
2. A full outer shell makes the atom stable. It has no reason to gain, lose or share electrons, so helium is unreactive.

**Example 3: Predicting an ion.** What ion does potassium form?
1. Potassium is in Group 1, so it has 1 valence electron (its arrangement is 2, 8, 8, 1).
2. It loses that electron to reach a full outer shell and forms the ion K+, with a charge of 1+.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to think that all metals are equally reactive. The alkali metals are very reactive, but metals such as gold are not. Another is to think that halogens are metals, when they are nonmetals. Finally, students forget the reason that noble gases are unreactive: their outer shells are already full, so they have no tendency to react.
