# Heating and Cooling Curves

A graph of temperature against time is like a fingerprint of how a substance changes state. If you heat a block of ice at a steady rate and record the temperature every minute, the graph has a very characteristic shape, with sloping parts and flat parts. Reading this graph tells you the melting and boiling points, which states are present and how much energy each change needs. This lesson explains how to read a heating curve and why the flat parts appear.

## Key ideas

On a heating curve, time is on the horizontal axis and temperature is on the vertical axis. The energy is supplied at a steady rate, so time is a measure of the energy added. For water, the curve starts with ice at a low temperature. The first sloping section shows the temperature of the solid rising. Then there is a flat section at 0 degrees C, where the ice is melting. Next, a second sloping section shows the temperature of the liquid water rising, followed by another flat section at 100 degrees C, where the water is boiling, and finally a third sloping section for the steam.

The sloping sections are the parts where the substance stays in one state and the temperature rises. The added energy increases the kinetic energy of the particles, and temperature is a measure of average kinetic energy. The flat sections are the changes of state, and the temperature does not change while they happen, although energy is still being added. The reason is that during melting or boiling the energy goes into overcoming the attractions between the particles, which increases their potential energy and not their kinetic energy. Since the average kinetic energy stays the same, the temperature stays the same. Only when all the substance has changed state does the temperature begin to rise again.

The flat sections also show the melting point and the boiling point. For pure water they are at 0 degrees C and 100 degrees C. During a flat section, both states are present together: ice and water while melting, and water and steam while boiling. The boiling plateau is much longer than the melting plateau, because separating particles completely to form a gas requires much more energy than loosening them to form a liquid.

A cooling curve is the reverse of a heating curve. The substance releases energy, the temperature falls, and flat sections appear at the freezing point and the condensation point, which are the same temperatures as the melting and boiling points. During these plateaus, energy is released as the attractions form again.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Reading a curve.** A heating curve of a solid is flat at 0 degrees C for 3 minutes. What is happening?
1. A flat section means a change of state, and the temperature stays constant.
2. At 0 degrees C the substance is ice melting to liquid water, and both are present during these 3 minutes.

**Example 2: Why flat?** Why does the temperature stay constant while water boils, even though it is still being heated?
1. The added energy is used to break the attractions between the water molecules, so that they can escape as a gas.
2. It does not increase the average kinetic energy of the particles, so the temperature does not rise.

**Example 3: The longest plateau.** Which plateau of water is longer, melting or boiling?
1. Vaporization needs much more energy than melting, because the particles must be completely separated.
2. At a steady heating rate this takes more time, so the boiling plateau is longer.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to think that the temperature must rise whenever heat is added. During a change of state it stays constant. Another is to mix up the flat and sloping sections: flat means a change of state, and sloping means a change in temperature within one state. Finally, students forget that a cooling curve has the same plateaus, at the same temperatures, as the heating curve.
