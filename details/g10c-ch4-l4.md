# Types of Chemical Reactions

The number of possible reactions is enormous, but most of them follow a few patterns. Recognizing the pattern from the reactants alone lets you classify a reaction, and later it lets you predict what forms. There are five main types: synthesis, decomposition, single replacement, double replacement and combustion. Each has a general form that you can recognize at a glance.

## Key ideas

In a synthesis reaction, which is also called combination, two or more substances combine to form one product: A + B -> AB. The formation of table salt is an example: 2Na + Cl2 -> 2NaCl. The reverse pattern is decomposition, in which one compound breaks into two or more simpler substances: AB -> A + B. An example is 2HgO -> 2Hg + O2. Compounds are usually stable, so decomposition normally needs an input of energy such as heat, light or electricity. This is why a synthesis has one product, and a decomposition has one reactant.

In a single replacement reaction, one element takes the place of another element in a compound: A + BC -> AC + B. An example is Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2, where zinc replaces hydrogen. The pattern always has one element and one compound on each side. In a double replacement reaction, two compounds exchange their ions: AB + CD -> AD + CB. These reactions occur in solution and usually produce a precipitate, which is an insoluble solid, or a gas, or water. For example, AgNO3 + NaCl -> AgCl + NaNO3 forms solid silver chloride. A neutralization is a double replacement between an acid and a base, and it produces a salt and water.

Combustion is the rapid reaction of a substance with oxygen, releasing heat and light. When a hydrocarbon burns completely, the products are carbon dioxide and water. If the oxygen supply is poor, combustion is incomplete, and carbon monoxide or soot forms instead, which is why a poorly ventilated heater is dangerous.

A good way to classify is to count the reactants and see which are elements and which are compounds. One element and one compound points to single replacement, two compounds points to double replacement, oxygen with a fuel points to combustion, and a single reactant points to decomposition.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Classify Cl2 + 2KBr -> 2KCl + Br2.**
1. The reactants are an element, Cl2, and a compound, KBr.
2. The chlorine takes the place of bromine in the compound, and bromine is released as an element.
3. The type is single replacement.

**Example 2: Classify Pb(NO3)2 + 2KI -> PbI2 + 2KNO3.**
1. Both reactants are compounds, and they swap partners: lead pairs with iodide, and potassium pairs with nitrate.
2. The type is double replacement.
3. PbI2 is a yellow solid, so a precipitate forms and the reaction proceeds.

**Example 3: Combustion of ethane.**
1. The reactants are the hydrocarbon C2H6 and oxygen, so this is combustion, and the products are CO2 and H2O.
2. The balanced equation is 2C2H6 + 7O2 -> 4CO2 + 6H2O.
3. Check the oxygen: 7 x 2 = 14 on the left, and 4 x 2 + 6 x 1 = 14 on the right.

## Common mistakes

Students often call every reaction with oxygen a combustion, but 2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO is better described as synthesis, because the metal does not burn as a fuel to give CO2 and H2O. Another error is to confuse single and double replacement. The way to avoid it is to count how many reactants are compounds. Some students forget that complete combustion gives CO2 and not CO. Finally, a decomposition can be mistaken for a synthesis when the direction of the arrow is misread, so always check which side has the single substance.
