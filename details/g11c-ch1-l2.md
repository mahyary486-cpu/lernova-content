# The Mole and Molar Mass

Atoms and molecules are far too small to count one by one, so chemists count them in groups called moles and weigh them in grams. The mole is the link between the invisible world of particles and the masses we measure on a balance. Almost every quantitative problem in chemistry, from the amount of product in a reaction to the concentration of a solution, begins with converting a mass into moles, so this idea deserves careful practice.

## Key ideas

One mole is 6.02 x 10^23 particles, a number called Avogadro's number. The particles may be atoms, molecules, formula units or ions, depending on the substance. The size of the number was chosen so that the mass of one mole in grams is equal to the mass of one particle in atomic mass units. Because of this, the molar mass, which is the mass of one mole in g/mol, can be found from the periodic table. For an element it is the atomic mass. For a compound it is the sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms in the formula, and each subscript multiplies the atoms before it. Water has a molar mass of 2 x 1 + 16 = 18 g/mol.

Two relations do most of the work. The amount in moles is found from the mass with n = mass / molar mass, and the number of particles from the moles with particles = n x 6.02 x 10^23. Moles are always the middle step, since the molar mass connects grams to moles and Avogadro's number connects moles to particles. A direct conversion from grams to particles is not possible.

For gases there is a third connection. At standard temperature and pressure, STP, which means 273 K and 1 atm, one mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L. This works because equal volumes of different gases hold equal numbers of particles under the same conditions, and so it gives a fast way to convert between moles and volume. It must be used only at STP. Away from STP the ideal gas law is needed, which we study in chapter 2.

Percent composition tells what fraction of the mass of a compound comes from each element. It is the mass of the element in one mole of the compound divided by the molar mass of the compound, multiplied by 100 percent. It is useful for comparing fertilizers, ores and foods, and the percentages of all the elements add up to 100 percent.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Mass to moles.** How many moles are in 36 g of water, whose molar mass is 18 g/mol?
1. The mass is given and moles are wanted, so n = mass / molar mass = 36 g / 18 g/mol.
2. The amount is n = 2 mol, and the units of grams cancel correctly.

**Example 2: Number of particles.** How many molecules are in 0.5 mol of water?
1. The number of particles equals the moles multiplied by Avogadro's number: N = 0.5 x 6.02 x 10^23.
2. N = 3.01 x 10^23 molecules.
3. Since each molecule has 3 atoms, there are 9.03 x 10^23 atoms in this sample.

**Example 3: Percent composition.** Find the percent of oxygen in water.
1. In one mole of water, which has a mass of 18 g, the oxygen accounts for 16 g.
2. The percent is 16 / 18 x 100 percent = 88.9 percent.
3. The hydrogen is the remaining 11.1 percent, and the two values add up to 100 percent.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to use the atomic mass instead of the molar mass of a molecule, which counts the atoms of only one element. For example, using 16 g/mol for water would be wrong. Students also forget to multiply the atoms by their subscripts, so that the molar mass of CO2 is calculated as 12 + 16 = 28 g/mol instead of 12 + 2 x 16 = 44 g/mol. Finally, the value of 22.4 L per mole holds only at STP, and using it at room temperature or at another pressure gives an incorrect volume.
