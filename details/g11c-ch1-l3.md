# Stoichiometry: Mole Ratios and Mass Calculations

A recipe tells you how many eggs you need for each cup of flour. A balanced chemical equation does the same thing for a reaction. It tells you how many moles of one substance react with or form from a given number of moles of another. This kind of calculation is called stoichiometry, and it lets a chemist decide in advance how much of each reactant to weigh out and how much product to expect.

## Key ideas

The coefficients of a balanced equation give the mole ratio between any two substances. In 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O, two moles of hydrogen react with one mole of oxygen to give two moles of water. The ratio is a statement about moles, and it is not true for grams, because one mole of hydrogen has a very different mass from one mole of oxygen. This is the idea that the whole lesson depends on.

Because balances measure mass, and not moles, most problems follow a four-step path: mass of A, to moles of A, to moles of B, to mass of B. The first step uses the molar mass of A, the second uses the mole ratio, and the last uses the molar mass of B. The mole ratio is written as the coefficient of the wanted substance divided by the coefficient of the given substance, which puts the unit that you want on top so that the units cancel. If the equation is not balanced, the ratio is wrong, and so is the whole answer.

For gases there is a useful shortcut. At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of moles, so gas volumes react in the same ratio as the coefficients. Two liters of hydrogen need one liter of oxygen to form water vapor. When a gas volume is wanted at STP, convert the moles with 22.4 L per mole. Away from STP, use the ideal gas law from chapter 2.

Finally, always ask whether the answer is reasonable. The mass of a product cannot be greater than the total mass of the reactants, and a result that comes out very small or very large usually means that the ratio was inverted or the molar mass was forgotten.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Mass to mass.** How many grams of water form from 4 g of H2, according to 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O?
1. The moles of H2 are 4 g / 2 g/mol = 2 mol. The mole ratio of water to hydrogen is 2 to 2, so 2 mol of H2O form.
2. The mass of water is 2 mol x 18 g/mol = 36 g.
3. This is reasonable, because the 36 g of water contain the 4 g of hydrogen plus 32 g of oxygen that joined it.

**Example 2: Oxygen needed.** How many moles of O2 react with 3 mol of H2?
1. The ratio of O2 to H2 is 1 to 2, which means one mole of oxygen for every two moles of hydrogen.
2. The moles of O2 are 3 x 1 / 2 = 1.5 mol.

**Example 3: Gas volume.** What volume of CO2 at STP forms when 1 mol of CH4 burns?
1. The equation is CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O, so one mole of CH4 gives one mole of CO2.
2. The volume at STP is 1 mol x 22.4 L/mol = 22.4 L.

## Common mistakes

The first mistake is to use an unbalanced equation to find the ratio, which gives numbers that do not match the real reaction. The second is to convert grams of one substance directly into grams of another, because the coefficients refer to moles, and each substance has its own molar mass. The third is to use the mole ratio upside down. A simple check is to ask which substance should be in larger amount. In Example 2 the answer must be smaller than 3 mol, since the equation uses only half as much oxygen as hydrogen.
