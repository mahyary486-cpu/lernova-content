# Alkenes, Alkynes and Aromatic Compounds

Unsaturated compounds are more reactive than alkanes because the double or triple bond between carbon atoms can open up and allow new atoms to be added. This is the basis of the plastic industry, of the ripening of fruit, and of many medicines. In this lesson we look at alkenes, which have a double bond, alkynes, which have a triple bond, and benzene, which has a ring with a special kind of bonding.

## Key ideas

Alkenes contain at least one carbon-carbon double bond, and their general formula is CnH2n. Alkynes contain a triple bond, with the formula CnH2n-2. Both families are unsaturated, because they have fewer hydrogen atoms than the alkane with the same number of carbons. The names end in -ene and -yne, and the chain is numbered so that the double or triple bond receives the lowest possible number. Thus CH3-CH=CH-CH3 is but-2-ene, and not but-3-ene.

The typical reaction is addition. One of the bonds in the double bond is weaker, and it breaks to join two new atoms, one on each carbon, so that no atom is lost. Alkenes add hydrogen, in the presence of a nickel catalyst, to give alkanes. They add bromine or chlorine to give dihalides, they add hydrogen bromide to give a bromoalkane, and they add water, with an acid catalyst, to give an alcohol. A simple test makes use of this. When an alkene is shaken with bromine water, the orange color disappears, because the bromine is used up in the addition. An alkane does not react in this way.

Rotation around a double bond is not possible without breaking the bond. As a result, the groups attached to the carbons stay fixed, and when each carbon has two different groups, two different isomers can exist. In the cis isomer the matching groups are on the same side, and in the trans isomer they are on opposite sides. They have different properties, such as boiling point.

Benzene, C6H6, is the main aromatic compound. Its six carbon atoms form a flat ring, and the electrons of the three double bonds are spread over the whole ring. This delocalization makes the ring unusually stable. Because of that, benzene does not easily undergo addition, which would destroy the stable ring. It reacts mainly by substitution, in which a hydrogen atom is replaced, for example in nitration or in the reaction with chlorine and a catalyst.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Complete CH2=CH2 + Br2 ->.**
1. The double bond opens, and one bromine atom adds to each carbon.
2. The product is CH2Br-CH2Br, which is 1,2-dibromoethane.
3. No atom is lost, which is typical of an addition reaction.

**Example 2: How many hydrogens are in an alkene with 5 carbons?**
1. Use the general formula CnH2n with n = 5.
2. The formula is C5H10, so there are 10 hydrogens.
3. The alkane C5H12 has 2 more, which is the difference caused by the double bond.

**Example 3: How can you tell an alkene from an alkane?**
1. Add a few drops of bromine water to each sample and shake.
2. The alkene decolorizes the bromine water, because the bromine adds across the double bond.
3. The alkane does not react in the dark, so the orange color stays.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that benzene undergoes addition easily, as an alkene does, but the delocalized ring is stable and favors substitution. Another error is to forget cis-trans isomers when a double bond has two different groups on each carbon. The third mistake is to use the alkane formula for an alkene, which gives two hydrogens too many.
