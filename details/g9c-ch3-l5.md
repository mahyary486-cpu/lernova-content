# Thermal Energy, Temperature and Heat

A cup of hot tea has a higher temperature than a bathtub of warm water, yet the bathtub contains far more thermal energy. Everyday language mixes up temperature, thermal energy and heat, but in science they are three different things. This lesson separates them, shows how heat travels, and introduces a formula for calculating how much heat is needed to change the temperature of a substance.

## Key ideas

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance. Thermal energy is the total energy of all the particles in the sample, so it depends on both the temperature and the amount of matter. This is why the bathtub has more thermal energy than the cup, even though the tea is hotter. Heat is the energy that is transferred from a hotter object to a colder one because of the difference in temperature. Heat always flows from hot to cold, and it continues until both are at the same temperature. There is no flow of cold; a cold object just receives less energy than it loses.

Heat is transferred in three ways. Conduction is the transfer by direct contact, as when a metal spoon in hot soup gets hot. Convection is the transfer by the movement of a fluid, as when warm air rises and cool air sinks. Radiation is the transfer by electromagnetic waves, and it needs no medium, which is how the Sun warms the Earth through empty space. Metals are good conductors of heat, while air, wood and plastic are insulators, and they slow the flow of heat.

Different substances need different amounts of energy to change their temperature. The specific heat capacity, c, of a substance is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of it by 1 degree C. Water has a very high value, 4200 J/(kg C), so it warms up and cools down slowly, which is why oceans moderate the climate of coastal cities. Metals have low values, such as 900 J/(kg C) for aluminum, so they heat up fast. The heat needed is given by Q = m c delta T, where m is the mass in kilograms and delta T is the change in temperature.

When two samples of the same liquid at different temperatures are mixed, the hot one loses the same amount of heat as the cold one gains, as long as no energy escapes. If the masses are equal, the final temperature is the average of the two.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Heating water.** How much heat is needed to raise the temperature of 2.0 kg of water by 5.0 degrees C? Use c = 4200 J/(kg C).
1. Use Q = m c delta T = 2.0 x 4200 x 5.0.
2. The heat needed is Q = 42 000 J, or 42 kJ.

**Example 2: Finding specific heat.** A 0.50 kg piece of metal absorbs 4500 J of heat and warms up by 10 degrees C. Find its specific heat capacity.
1. Rearrange the formula: c = Q / (m delta T) = 4500 / (0.50 x 10).
2. The specific heat is c = 900 J/(kg C), which is the value for aluminum.

**Example 3: Mixing water.** 100 g of water at 80 degrees C is mixed with 100 g of water at 20 degrees C. Find the final temperature.
1. The masses are equal and the liquid is the same, so the heat lost by the hot water equals the heat gained by the cold water.
2. The final temperature is the average: (80 + 20) / 2 = 50 degrees C.

## Common mistakes

Students often confuse heat with temperature. Temperature is the average kinetic energy, and heat is energy in transfer. Another mistake is to use grams in Q = m c delta T when c is given per kilogram, which makes the answer wrong by a factor of 1000. Finally, remember that heat flows from the hot object to the cold one, and that cold is not a substance that flows.
