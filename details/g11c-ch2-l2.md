# Boyle's, Charles's and Gay-Lussac's Laws

When you pump a bicycle tire, you squeeze the air into a smaller volume, and the pressure goes up. When you leave a basketball outside on a cold night, it goes soft. These everyday observations are examples of the gas laws, which describe how pressure, volume and temperature are related for a fixed amount of gas. Each law holds one variable constant and shows how the other two change, and together they can be combined into one equation.

## Key ideas

Boyle's law connects pressure and volume at constant temperature. They are inversely proportional, so when the volume is halved, the pressure doubles: P1 V1 = P2 V2. The kinetic theory explains it. In a smaller volume the particles hit the walls more often, so the pressure rises, while the average energy of the particles stays the same.

Charles's law connects volume and temperature at constant pressure. The volume is directly proportional to the temperature in kelvins: V1 / T1 = V2 / T2. When the gas is heated the particles move faster, and the volume must grow if the pressure is to stay the same. Gay-Lussac's law connects pressure and temperature at constant volume: P1 / T1 = P2 / T2. In a rigid container the particles cannot spread out, so faster particles hit the walls harder and more often, and the pressure rises. This is why a sealed can must never be heated in a fire.

When all three variables change together, the laws combine into one equation: P1 V1 / T1 = P2 V2 / T2. Each of the three laws can be obtained from it by cancelling the quantity that is held constant. In every one of these equations the temperature must be in kelvins. The units of pressure and volume are not important, as long as they are the same on both sides, because the units cancel in the ratio.

These laws apply only when the amount of gas does not change. If gas leaks out of a tire, or more is pumped in, the number of moles changes, and a different equation is needed. That is the subject of the next lesson.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Boyle's law.** A gas at 2.0 atm and 3.0 L expands to 6.0 L at constant temperature. Find the new pressure.
1. Solve for the unknown: P2 = P1 V1 / V2.
2. P2 = 2.0 atm x 3.0 L / 6.0 L = 1.0 atm.
3. The volume doubled, and the pressure fell to half, as the law predicts.

**Example 2: Charles's law.** A gas of volume 4.0 L at 250 K is heated to 500 K at constant pressure. Find the new volume.
1. Solve for the unknown: V2 = V1 T2 / T1.
2. V2 = 4.0 L x 500 K / 250 K = 8.0 L.

**Example 3: Gay-Lussac's law.** A gas at 1.0 atm and 300 K is heated to 450 K in a rigid vessel. Find the new pressure.
1. The volume is constant, so P2 = P1 T2 / T1.
2. P2 = 1.0 atm x 450 K / 300 K = 1.5 atm.

## Common mistakes

The commonest mistake is to use Celsius temperatures in these equations, which always gives a wrong answer. Another is to forget which variable is held constant, and so to use the wrong law, for example Boyle's law in a problem where the temperature changes. Students also mix up direct and inverse relations, writing the temperature in the wrong place. A quick reasoning check helps: if the gas is heated or compressed, the pressure must rise. Finally, remember that these laws apply only when the amount of gas stays the same.
