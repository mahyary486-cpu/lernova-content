# Solubility

Why does sugar dissolve faster in hot tea, and why does a warm bottle of soda lose its fizz faster than a cold one? The answers lie in solubility. Solubility tells how much of a substance can dissolve, and several factors, such as temperature, pressure and the size of the particles, affect both the amount and the speed of dissolving.

## Key ideas

Solubility is the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature. It is often given in grams of solute per 100 g of water. At 20 degrees C, about 36 g of table salt dissolves in 100 g of water. A solution can be described by how much solute it holds compared to this limit. An unsaturated solution contains less than the maximum, so more solute can still dissolve. A saturated solution contains exactly the maximum, and any extra solute stays undissolved at the bottom. A supersaturated solution holds more than the normal maximum. It is unstable, and a small disturbance may make the extra solute crystallize.

Temperature changes solubility. For most solids, solubility increases as the temperature rises, so more sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold. For gases, the opposite is true: they are less soluble in warm liquids. When a bottle of soda warms up, the carbon dioxide escapes, and the drink goes flat. This is also the reason why fish struggle in warm water, which holds less dissolved oxygen. Pressure affects the solubility of gases, too. More gas dissolves at higher pressure, and this is why soda is bottled under pressure and fizzes when the cap is opened.

Solubility, the maximum amount, must be kept apart from the rate of dissolving, how fast it happens. The rate increases with stirring, which brings fresh solvent to the solute, with a higher temperature, which makes the particles move faster, and with smaller particles, which have a larger surface area exposed to the solvent. Stirring or crushing makes a solute dissolve faster, but does not change the total amount that can dissolve.

A solubility curve is a graph that shows solubility against temperature. A point on the line means a saturated solution at that temperature, a point below the line means an unsaturated solution, and a point above it indicates a supersaturated one. Reading the curve lets us predict how much solid will crystallize when a hot saturated solution is cooled.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: A saturated solution.** At 20 degrees C, 36 g of salt dissolves in 100 g of water. How much dissolves in 250 g of water at the same temperature?
1. The amount of water is 250 / 100 = 2.5 times larger.
2. The salt that can dissolve is 36 x 2.5 = 90 g.

**Example 2: Gas in warm soda.** Why does warm soda go flat faster than cold soda?
1. Carbon dioxide is less soluble in water at higher temperatures.
2. The gas leaves the solution more quickly, so the bubbles escape and the drink goes flat.

**Example 3: Crushed sugar.** Why do sugar crystals dissolve faster than a sugar cube of the same mass?
1. Crystals have a much larger total surface area exposed to the water.
2. More contact between solute and solvent means a faster dissolving, although the same total amount can dissolve.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that stirring changes how much can dissolve in total, but it only speeds up the process. Another mistake is to think that all solutes are more soluble when hot. Gases are less soluble. Finally, saturated and concentrated are not the same: a solution can be saturated and still contain little solute if the solute has a low solubility.
