# Electromagnetic Induction and Faraday's Law

Moving a magnet into a coil makes a current flow in the coil, although there is no battery. This surprising effect, discovered by Faraday, is called electromagnetic induction, and it is how almost all of the world's electricity is generated. In a power station, something turns a coil in a magnetic field, and the changing field through the coil produces a voltage that drives current to our homes.

## Key ideas

The key quantity is the magnetic flux, which measures how much magnetic field passes through a loop. For a flat loop of area A in a uniform field B, the flux is Phi = B A cos(theta), where theta is the angle between the field and the line perpendicular to the loop. The unit is the weber (Wb), equal to 1 T m^2. The flux is largest when the field is perpendicular to the plane of the loop.

Faraday's law states that a changing flux induces an emf in the loop: emf = -N delta Phi / delta t, where N is the number of turns. The emf depends on how fast the flux changes, not on its value. A strong but constant flux induces nothing, while a small flux that changes quickly induces a lot. A flux can change in three ways: by changing the field, by changing the area, or by changing the angle.

The minus sign carries the content of Lenz's law: the induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change in flux that produces it. If a magnet's north pole is pushed toward a coil, the coil's current creates a field that pushes back, resisting the approach. This is a consequence of energy conservation. If the induced current helped the change, energy would be created from nothing. Instead, work must be done to move the magnet, and this work becomes electrical energy.

A rod of length L moving at speed v perpendicular to a field B has an induced emf of B L v. This is a special case of Faraday's law, because the moving rod changes the area of the circuit. A generator uses a coil that rotates in a field. The angle changes continually, so the flux changes, and the emf follows a sine wave, emf = N B A omega sin(omega t), which is alternating current. Where the flux does not change, no emf appears.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: An induced emf.** A coil of 100 turns experiences a flux change from 0.040 Wb to 0.010 Wb in 0.10 s. Find the size of the induced emf.
1. The change in flux is 0.040 - 0.010 = 0.030 Wb.
2. emf = N delta Phi / delta t = 100 x 0.030 / 0.10.
3. The emf is 30 V.

**Example 2: A moving rod.** A rod 0.40 m long moves at 5.0 m/s perpendicular to a field of 0.30 T. Find the emf.
1. Use emf = B L v = 0.30 x 0.40 x 5.0.
2. The emf is 0.60 V.

**Example 3: Flux through a loop.** A loop of area 0.20 m^2 is perpendicular to a field of 0.50 T. Find the flux, and the flux when the loop is turned by 60 degrees.
1. At first, Phi = B A cos(0) = 0.50 x 0.20 = 0.10 Wb.
2. After the turn, Phi = 0.50 x 0.20 x cos(60) = 0.050 Wb.
3. This change in flux would induce an emf while the loop turns.

## Common mistakes

Students often ignore the number of turns, but each turn contributes to the emf. Another mistake is to think that a constant flux, even a large one, induces an emf; only a change does. Many forget that Lenz's law, with the minus sign, gives the direction of the induced current, and the size comes from the rest of the formula. Finally, measure the angle in the flux formula from the line perpendicular to the loop, not from the plane of the loop.
