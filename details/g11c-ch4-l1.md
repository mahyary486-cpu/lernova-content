# Energy, Heat and Enthalpy

A chemical hand warmer gets hot when it is opened, and an instant cold pack gets cold when it is squeezed. Both are chemical reactions, and they differ in the direction in which energy flows. Almost every reaction involves a change in energy, and measuring and predicting it is what thermochemistry is about. The central quantity is the enthalpy change, which is the heat exchanged at constant pressure, and it tells us whether a reaction releases or absorbs energy.

## Key ideas

In thermochemistry we divide the world into the system, which is the reaction we study, and the surroundings, which is everything else. Energy passes between them as heat. An exothermic reaction releases heat to the surroundings, so the surroundings get warmer. An endothermic reaction absorbs heat from the surroundings, which get colder. The combustion of fuels is exothermic, and the dissolving of ammonium nitrate in a cold pack is endothermic.

The enthalpy change is written delta H, and it is defined as delta H = H(products) - H(reactants). For an exothermic reaction the products have less enthalpy than the reactants, so delta H is negative. For an endothermic reaction it is positive. The sign is given from the point of view of the system: a negative sign means that energy leaves the system. Delta H is usually quoted in kJ per mole of reaction as the equation is written.

Because enthalpy is a property of the substances involved, two rules follow. If a reaction is reversed, the sign of delta H changes, since the energy that was released must now be supplied. If the equation is multiplied by a number, delta H is multiplied by the same number, because twice the amount of material exchanges twice the energy. A thermochemical equation therefore has to be read with its coefficients, which mean moles in this context.

Even an exothermic reaction does not always start by itself. The reactants must first collide with enough energy to break the first bonds, and this minimum energy is the activation energy. A match is needed to start the burning of paper, although the burning then releases much more energy than the match supplied. The activation energy determines the speed of a reaction, while delta H depends only on the starting and ending states, and the two are not related to each other.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: The sign of delta H.** The burning of 1 mol of methane releases 890 kJ. Write delta H.
1. Heat leaves the system, so the reaction is exothermic.
2. delta H = -890 kJ/mol.

**Example 2: Reversing a reaction.** The reaction A -> B has delta H = -50 kJ. What is delta H for B -> A?
1. Reversing the reaction changes the sign.
2. delta H = +50 kJ, and the reverse reaction is endothermic.

**Example 3: Scaling.** How much heat is released when 2 mol of CH4 burn?
1. Each mole releases 890 kJ.
2. The heat released is 2 x 890 = 1780 kJ, so delta H = -1780 kJ for this amount.

## Common mistakes

The most frequent mistake is to mix up the sign of delta H, for example by writing a positive value for a reaction that releases heat. A helpful rule is that a negative sign means that the system loses energy. Another is to forget to multiply delta H when the equation is multiplied, or to forget to change its sign when it is reversed. Finally, students often confuse the activation energy with delta H. The first is the barrier that must be crossed, and the second is the difference between the final and the initial energy.
