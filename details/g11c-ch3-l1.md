# Solution Concentration Units

A solution is a homogeneous mixture, and the amount of solute dissolved in it can be stated in several ways. Different jobs need different units. A chemist who plans a reaction wants molarity, because it links volume to moles. A chemist who studies the freezing point wants molality, because it does not change with temperature. Someone who measures a pollutant in drinking water wants parts per million. This lesson explains each unit, the reason it exists, and how to convert and dilute.

## Key ideas

Molarity, M, is the number of moles of solute per liter of solution: M = mol solute / L solution. Note that the volume is that of the whole solution, and not of the solvent. When a solid dissolves, the final volume is not simply the volume of the water plus the volume of the solid, so solutions are prepared by dissolving the solute in some solvent and then adding solvent up to the final mark of a volumetric flask. Molarity changes slightly with temperature, because the volume of a liquid expands when it is heated.

Molality, m, is the number of moles of solute per kilogram of solvent: m = mol solute / kg solvent. It uses mass, and mass does not change with temperature, which is why molality is used for properties that depend on temperature, such as the freezing and boiling points. Here the mass is that of the solvent alone, in kilograms.

Mass percent is the mass of solute divided by the mass of solution, multiplied by 100 percent. The mole fraction, X, is the moles of one component divided by the total moles of all components, and the mole fractions in a mixture add up to 1. Parts per million, ppm, is the mass of solute divided by the mass of solution, multiplied by 10^6. For very dilute water solutions, 1 ppm is about 1 mg of solute per liter.

Dilution is the addition of solvent to lower the concentration. The amount of solute does not change, so the moles before and after are the same, and M1 V1 = M2 V2. In this equation V1 and V2 can be in any unit, provided they are the same on both sides. To make 500 mL of 0.1 M solution from a 1.0 M stock solution, the volume needed is V1 = M2 V2 / M1 = 0.1 x 500 / 1.0 = 50 mL, and this is then diluted to the final volume.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Molarity.** A student dissolves 11.7 g of NaCl, whose molar mass is 58.5 g/mol, to make 500 mL of solution.
1. The moles are n = 11.7 g / 58.5 g/mol = 0.200 mol.
2. The volume in liters is 500 mL = 0.500 L.
3. M = 0.200 mol / 0.500 L = 0.400 M.

**Example 2: Molality.** A sample of 0.200 mol of solute is dissolved in 400 g of water.
1. Convert the mass of solvent to kilograms: 400 g = 0.400 kg.
2. m = 0.200 mol / 0.400 kg = 0.500 mol/kg.

**Example 3: Mole fraction.** A mixture contains 1 mol of ethanol and 3 mol of water.
1. The total number of moles is 1 + 3 = 4 mol.
2. X(ethanol) = 1 / 4 = 0.25, and X(water) = 3 / 4 = 0.75.
3. The two mole fractions add up to 1, as they should.

## Common mistakes

The most common error in molarity is to use the volume of the solvent instead of the volume of the whole solution. Another is to use grams instead of kilograms in molality, which makes the result a thousand times too large. Students also assume that a solute added to 1 L of water gives 1 L of solution, but the final volume is usually different. Finally, do not mix the units of the volumes in a dilution problem. Any unit works, but both volumes must use the same one.
