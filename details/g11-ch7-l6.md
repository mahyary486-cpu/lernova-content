# The Binomial Distribution

## What is a binomial experiment?
A binomial experiment has:
1. A **fixed number of trials** n.
2. Each trial has **two outcomes**: success or failure.
3. The trials are **independent**.
4. The probability of success **p** is the same every time.
Examples: flipping a coin 10 times and counting heads; guessing the answers on a multiple-choice test.

## The binomial probability formula
The probability of exactly k successes in n trials:
**P(X = k) = nCk * p^k * (1 - p)^(n - k)**
Here nCk counts the ways to place the k successes among the n trials.

## Examples
**Three coin flips, exactly 2 heads.** 3C2 * (1/2)^2 * (1/2)^1 = 3 * 1/8 = **3/8**.

**Five coin flips, exactly 3 heads.** 5C3 * (1/2)^5 = 10/32 = **5/16**.

**n = 3, p = 0.2, exactly 1 success.** 3C1 * 0.2 * 0.8^2 = 3 * 0.2 * 0.64 = **0.384**.

**At least one success.** Use the complement. For n = 3 and p = 1/2: 1 - P(0 successes) = 1 - 1/8 = **7/8**.

**Guessing on a test.** There are 10 questions, each with 4 choices, so p = 1/4. The probability of getting none right: (3/4)^10, about **0.056**.

## Mean and standard deviation
- **Mean (expected number of successes): μ = np**
- **Standard deviation: σ = sqrt(np(1 - p))**

For n = 100 and p = 0.3: μ = **30** and σ = sqrt(100 * 0.3 * 0.7) = sqrt(21), about 4.58.

## Common mistakes
- Forgetting the nCk factor.
- Using p for the failure probability. The exponent of (1 - p) is n - k.
- Using the binomial formula when the trials are not independent (for example, drawing without replacement from a small group).
- Forgetting that "at least one" is easiest with the complement.
