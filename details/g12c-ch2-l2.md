# Balancing Redox Equations

Some redox equations can be balanced by inspection, but many cannot. When oxygen, hydrogen and charges are all involved, as in reactions of permanganate or dichromate, trial and error fails. The half-reaction method is a systematic procedure that works for every redox equation, however complicated. It treats oxidation and reduction as two separate processes, balances each one, and then joins them in a way that gives the same number of electrons in both.

## Key ideas

The first step is to separate the equation into two half-reactions, one for the species that is oxidized and one for the species that is reduced. Each half-reaction is balanced on its own, in a fixed order. First balance all the atoms except oxygen and hydrogen. Then balance oxygen by adding H2O where it is needed. Then balance hydrogen by adding H+. At this point both the atoms and the oxygen and hydrogen are in order, but the charges are not. Finally balance the charge by adding electrons to the side with more positive charge. Electrons appear on the left in a reduction half-reaction and on the right in an oxidation half-reaction.

The next step is to multiply each half-reaction by an integer so that the number of electrons is the same in both. The electrons lost in the oxidation must equal the electrons gained in the reduction, because electrons are not created or destroyed. Then add the half-reactions. The electrons cancel, and so do any species that appear on both sides, such as H+ and H2O, so the final equation should be simplified.

The method is for an acidic solution. For a basic solution, first balance as if the solution were acidic. Then add to both sides as many OH- ions as there are H+ ions. The H+ and OH- on the same side combine to form H2O, and the excess water is cancelled. The result has OH- in place of H+, which is correct for a basic medium.

The last step is always to check. Count the atoms of every element on both sides, and then check that the total charge is the same on both sides. A balanced equation must satisfy both conditions, and the charge test is a quick way to find a mistake.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: A simple redox reaction.** Balance Fe 2+ + Ce 4+ -> Fe 3+ + Ce 3+.
1. The oxidation half-reaction is Fe 2+ -> Fe 3+ + e-, and the reduction half-reaction is Ce 4+ + e- -> Ce 3+.
2. Each half-reaction already involves one electron, so the numbers are equal and no multiplication is needed.
3. Adding gives Fe 2+ + Ce 4+ -> Fe 3+ + Ce 3+, with a charge of +6 on each side.

**Example 2: Permanganate and iron(II) in acid.** Balance MnO4- + Fe 2+ -> Mn 2+ + Fe 3+.
1. For the reduction, balance oxygen with water and hydrogen with H+: MnO4- + 8 H+ + 5 e- -> Mn 2+ + 4 H2O. The charge is -1 + 8 - 5 = +2 on the left and +2 on the right.
2. The oxidation is Fe 2+ -> Fe 3+ + e-, and it is multiplied by 5 to give 5 electrons.
3. Adding gives MnO4- + 8 H+ + 5 Fe 2+ -> Mn 2+ + 4 H2O + 5 Fe 3+.

**Example 3: Check the charge of the last equation.**
1. The left side has -1 + 8 + 5 x 2 = -1 + 8 + 10 = +17.
2. The right side has +2 + 5 x 3 = +2 + 15 = +17.
3. The charges are equal. The atoms also match: 1 Mn, 4 O, 8 H and 5 Fe on each side.

## Common mistakes

A frequent error is to add the half-reactions before the number of electrons has been made equal, so that electrons remain in the final equation. Another is to forget to add H+ or OH- according to the medium, which leaves an equation that is balanced in atoms but not in charge. The third error is to skip the final check of charge, which is the quickest way to find a mistake.
