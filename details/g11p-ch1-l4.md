# Gravitational Fields and Weight

The same astronaut weighs much less on the Moon than on Earth, yet she is the same person with the same amount of matter. The explanation is that weight is not a fixed property of an object. It depends on the gravitational field of the place where the object is. This lesson separates the idea of mass from the idea of weight and shows how to compute the field strength of any planet.

## Key ideas

A gravitational field is the region of space around a mass in which other masses feel a gravitational force. The field strength at a point is the force per unit mass, g = F / m. Combining this with the law of gravitation gives g = G M / r^2, where M is the mass of the planet and r is the distance from its center. The unit of g can be written as newtons per kilogram or as meters per second squared, and the two are the same. The field strength does not depend on the mass of the object that feels it: a small stone and a large rock fall with the same acceleration in the same place.

Mass and weight are different quantities. Mass, in kilograms, measures the amount of matter and is constant everywhere. Weight is the gravitational force on the object, W = m g, measured in newtons, and it changes from place to place. On Earth g is 9.8 N/kg, and on the Moon it is 1.6 N/kg, so a 50 kg astronaut weighs 490 N on Earth and only 80 N on the Moon, while her mass is 50 kg in both places.

The field strength also changes with height, and it follows the same inverse-square rule as the force: far from the planet it is weaker. It also depends on the planet itself, being proportional to M / R^2, where R is the planet's radius. A planet with more mass has a stronger field, but a larger radius weakens the field at the surface.

A common misunderstanding concerns astronauts in orbit. They appear to float, so people think that they have left Earth's gravity. In fact the field at the height of the space station is still about 90 percent of its value on the ground. The astronauts feel weightless because they and the station are in free fall around the Earth, accelerating together, so nothing pushes on them to give the sensation of weight.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Weight.** A 50 kg person stands on Earth where g = 9.8 N/kg. Find the weight.
1. Use W = m g = 50 x 9.8.
2. The weight is 490 N.

**Example 2: Checking the field of Earth.** Find g at the surface of the Earth, using M = 5.97 x 10^24 kg and R = 6.37 x 10^6 m.
1. Use g = G M / R^2, with G M = 6.67 x 10^-11 x 5.97 x 10^24 = 3.98 x 10^14.
2. The radius squared is (6.37 x 10^6)^2 = 4.06 x 10^13.
3. Then g = 3.98 x 10^14 / 4.06 x 10^13 = 9.8 N/kg, in agreement with the measured value.

**Example 3: Another planet.** A planet has twice the mass of Earth and twice the radius of Earth. Find g at its surface.
1. Because g is proportional to M / R^2, the factor is 2 / 2^2 = 2 / 4 = 1/2.
2. The field is half of Earth's: g = 0.5 x 9.8 = 4.9 m/s^2.

## Common mistakes

Many students believe that astronauts in orbit are beyond the reach of Earth's gravity; they are in free fall instead. Another mistake is to mix up mass and weight, for instance by saying that mass changes on the Moon. A third error is to use the height above the surface as r in g = G M / r^2; the distance must be measured from the center of the planet. Finally, when comparing planets, remember to square the radius but not the mass.
