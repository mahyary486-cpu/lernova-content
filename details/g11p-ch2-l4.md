# Buoyancy and Archimedes' Principle

Why does a steel ship weighing thousands of tons float, while a small steel nail sinks at once? The answer is not the material but the shape. A ship pushes aside a very large amount of water, and the water pushes back with a large upward force. This push, the buoyant force, acts on every object placed in a fluid, and Archimedes found exactly how large it is.

## Key ideas

Archimedes' principle states that the buoyant force on an object that is fully or partly submerged equals the weight of the fluid that the object displaces: F_b = rho_fluid g V_displaced. The force points upward, and it depends on the density of the fluid and on the volume of fluid pushed aside, not on the weight or the material of the object. A kilogram of lead and a kilogram of wood, both fully under water, feel different buoyant forces, because the wood is much bigger and displaces more water.

The behavior of an object follows from comparing the buoyant force with the weight. A fully submerged object sinks if its density is greater than the density of the fluid, and it rises if its density is smaller. When an object floats, it is in equilibrium, so the buoyant force equals its weight. The object sinks until the displaced fluid has exactly the weight of the object. This leads to a handy rule: the fraction of the volume that is submerged equals rho_object / rho_fluid. A block of density 500 kg/m^3 in water floats with half of its volume under the surface.

A ship floats because its hollow shape displaces a huge volume of water, so that the average density of the ship, steel and air together, is less than the density of water. A nail, by contrast, is solid steel, with a density about eight times that of water, so it sinks.

Buoyancy also changes what a scale shows. The apparent weight of an object in a fluid is its true weight minus the buoyant force. The loss of weight when an object is lowered into water equals the buoyant force, which gives a method for finding the volume and density of irregular objects. This is the famous story of the crown that Archimedes tested.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: A buoyant force.** An object of volume 0.0020 m^3 is fully under water. Use g = 9.8 m/s^2. Find the buoyant force.
1. The displaced volume equals the object's volume, 0.0020 m^3.
2. F_b = rho g V = 1000 x 9.8 x 0.0020.
3. The buoyant force is 19.6 N, directed upward.

**Example 2: A floating block.** A wooden block floats in water with half of its volume submerged. Find the density of the wood.
1. For a floating object, the fraction submerged is rho_wood / rho_water = 0.50.
2. So rho_wood = 0.50 x 1000.
3. The density of the wood is 500 kg/m^3.

**Example 3: Apparent weight.** A rock weighs 30 N in air and 20 N when fully submerged in water. Find the buoyant force, the volume and the density of the rock.
1. The weight lost equals the buoyant force: F_b = 30 - 20 = 10 N.
2. The volume is V = F_b / (rho g) = 10 / (1000 x 9.8) = 1.0 x 10^-3 m^3.
3. The mass is 30 / 9.8 = 3.06 kg, so the density is 3.06 / 1.0 x 10^-3 = 3000 kg/m^3 (the same as 1000 x 30 / 10).

## Common mistakes

Students often use the volume of the object above the water as the displaced volume, but it is the submerged part that counts. Another widespread belief is that heavy objects always sink; a heavy ship floats because of the water it displaces. A third mistake is to think that the buoyant force depends on the weight of the object, when it depends on the displaced fluid. Finally, do not mix up the density of the object and the density of the fluid in the formula for the buoyant force; the fluid's density is the one that appears.
