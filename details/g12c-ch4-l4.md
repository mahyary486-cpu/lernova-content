# Proteins and Amino Acids

The shape of a protein decides what it does, and the shape comes from the order of its amino acids. Proteins make muscles, carry oxygen in blood, defend the body against infection and, as enzymes, speed up almost every chemical reaction in a cell. They are all built from the same set of about twenty amino acids, joined in different orders and in chains of different lengths. This lesson follows the path from the amino acid to the folded protein.

## Key ideas

An amino acid has a central carbon atom bonded to an amino group, -NH2, a carboxyl group, -COOH, a hydrogen atom and a side chain R. Its general formula is H2N-CH(R)-COOH. The side chain is different in each of the twenty amino acids, and it determines whether the amino acid is polar, nonpolar, acidic or basic. In water, an amino acid exists mainly as a zwitterion, in which the carboxyl group has lost a proton and the amino group has gained one. The ion has a positive and a negative charge, and a net charge of zero.

Amino acids join in a condensation reaction. The -COOH group of one reacts with the -NH2 group of the next, with the loss of a molecule of water, and the new -CO-NH- link is a peptide bond. Two amino acids give a dipeptide, and many give a polypeptide. A chain of N amino acids has N - 1 peptide bonds, because each bond joins two neighboring units.

Four levels describe the structure of a protein. The primary structure is the sequence of the amino acids. The secondary structure is the regular local folding, as an alpha helix or a beta sheet, held by hydrogen bonds between the peptide groups. The tertiary structure is the full three-dimensional fold of one chain, which is held by interactions between the side chains, such as hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions, disulfide bridges and hydrophobic effects. The quaternary structure exists when several chains join, as in hemoglobin.

Enzymes are proteins that act as catalysts. A small part of the molecule, called the active site, fits the reactant, which is the substrate, and lowers the activation energy. Because the active site depends on the exact shape, any change in shape can stop the enzyme working. Denaturation is the loss of the natural shape, caused by heat, extreme pH or some chemicals. It breaks the weak interactions of the secondary and tertiary structures, but not the peptide bonds, and so the primary structure is unchanged.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Form a dipeptide.** Two glycine molecules, H2N-CH2-COOH, form a dipeptide. What else is formed?
1. The -COOH group of one molecule reacts with the -NH2 group of the other.
2. The -OH from the acid group and one H from the amino group leave together.
3. One molecule of water is formed, and the product is H2N-CH2-CO-NH-CH2-COOH.

**Example 2: Peptide bonds in a polypeptide.** A polypeptide has 50 amino acids. How many peptide bonds does it contain?
1. Each peptide bond joins two neighboring amino acids.
2. A chain of 50 units has 49 places where two units meet.
3. The number of peptide bonds is 50 - 1 = 49.

**Example 3: Why does cooking an egg change the white?**
1. The heat breaks the hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions that hold the folded shape.
2. The protein unfolds, and the chains clump together, so the clear liquid becomes a white solid.
3. The protein is denatured. The peptide bonds are not broken, and the change cannot be reversed.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that the number of peptide bonds equals the number of amino acids, but it is one less. Another error is to say that denaturation breaks peptide bonds, while it affects only the folded shape. The third mistake is to confuse the four levels of structure, so the sequence, the helix, the fold and the group of chains need to be kept separate.
