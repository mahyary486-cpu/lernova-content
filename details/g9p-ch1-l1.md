# The Scientific Method and Lab Safety

Science is a way of answering questions about the natural world by testing ideas against evidence. Instead of simply trusting what sounds reasonable, a scientist asks how an idea could be checked, designs a fair test, and lets the results decide. The set of steps that people follow is called the scientific method. It is not a rigid recipe that every investigation must follow in exactly the same order. It is a flexible path: scientists often go back to an earlier step when a result is surprising, and a single experiment often raises new questions that start the cycle again.

## Key ideas

A scientific investigation usually begins with a question, for example "Why do some plants grow taller than others?" The scientist then researches what is already known, so that the new work builds on earlier results instead of repeating them. Next comes a hypothesis, which is a testable explanation or prediction. A good hypothesis is often written in the form "If ... then ...", such as "If a plant receives more light, then it will grow taller." It must be testable, which means that an experiment could show that it is wrong. A statement such as "plants are happier in the sun" cannot be tested, so it is not a useful hypothesis.

To test a hypothesis fairly, scientists run a controlled experiment. In a controlled experiment only one factor is changed on purpose; this factor is the independent variable. The factor that is measured to see the effect is the dependent variable. Everything else that could influence the result, such as the amount of water, the type of soil or the temperature, is kept the same, and these are the controlled variables. If more than one thing changed at once, you could not tell which one caused the result. Many experiments also include a control group, which receives no treatment or the standard treatment, so that the results of the changed group can be compared with something.

After the experiment, the scientist analyzes the data, often with tables and graphs, and draws a conclusion. The data either support the hypothesis or fail to support it. Data never "prove" a hypothesis for every possible case, because the experiment covered only certain conditions. Scientists then share their results so that others can repeat the work and check it. Repeating an experiment and getting the same result is one of the strongest ways to build confidence in a finding.

It helps to know the difference between a theory and a law. In everyday speech "theory" means a guess, but in science a theory is a well-tested explanation that is supported by a large amount of evidence from many investigations. A law describes a pattern in nature, often with an equation, but it does not explain why the pattern exists. Theories do not turn into laws; they are different kinds of knowledge.

Safety is part of doing science correctly. In the laboratory you wear safety goggles, tie back long hair and keep the work area tidy. You never taste chemicals and you never smell them directly; instead you gently waft the vapor toward your nose if the teacher tells you to. You must know where the exits, the fire blanket and the eyewash station are, and you report every spill, breakage or injury to the teacher immediately, even a small one. Following directions exactly is part of safety, because many accidents happen when someone changes a procedure without asking.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Naming the variables.** A student grows bean plants under different amounts of light each day and measures the height of each plant after two weeks. Identify the variables.
1. The student decides how much light each plant gets, so the amount of light is the independent variable.
2. The height of the plants is what is measured as the result, so it is the dependent variable.
3. The amount of water, the type of soil, the size of the pot and the temperature must be the same for all plants, so these are the controlled variables.
4. A plant that gets the normal amount of light can serve as the control group for comparison.

**Example 2: Writing a hypothesis.** A student asks, "Does water temperature affect how fast sugar dissolves?" Write a hypothesis and plan a fair test.
1. A testable prediction is: "If the water is hotter, then the sugar will dissolve faster."
2. To make the test fair, use the same mass of sugar, the same volume of water and the same amount of stirring in every trial.
3. Change only the temperature, for example 10 C, 30 C and 50 C.
4. Measure the time the sugar needs to dissolve completely in each trial, and repeat each trial more than once.

**Example 3: Drawing a conclusion.** In the sugar experiment the sugar dissolves in 60 s at 10 C, in 30 s at 30 C and in 15 s at 50 C. What can the student conclude?
1. As the temperature rises, the dissolving time becomes shorter each time.
2. This agrees with the prediction, so the data support the hypothesis that sugar dissolves faster in warmer water.
3. The conclusion applies only to these conditions. It does not prove that every substance dissolves faster in warm water.

## Common mistakes

A very common mistake is to change more than one variable at a time. For example, if a student uses a different amount of sugar in each cup and also a different temperature, the result cannot be traced to a single cause. Another mistake is to call a hypothesis "just a guess"; a hypothesis is a testable prediction that is based on research. Students also like to say that the data "prove" the hypothesis, but data can only support it or fail to support it, because a later experiment might give a different result. Many people think that a theory is weaker than a law, while a theory is in fact a strongly supported explanation. Finally, never ignore a safety rule because an experiment looks harmless; many accidents happen with ordinary materials.
