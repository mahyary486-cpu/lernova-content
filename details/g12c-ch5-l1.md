# Nuclear Reactions and Radioactive Decay

Nuclear equations are balanced like chemical ones, but with nucleons in place of atoms. In a chemical reaction the nucleus stays the same and only the electrons move. In a nuclear reaction the nucleus itself changes, so a different element may be formed. This is the process behind radioactive dating, medical imaging, nuclear power and the heat inside the Earth, and the balancing rules are simple enough that you can write any decay equation once you know the main types of radiation.

## Key ideas

A nuclide is written with its mass number A, which is the number of protons and neutrons, and its atomic number Z, which is the number of protons. For example Ra-226 has A = 226 and Z = 88. In every nuclear equation, two quantities must be conserved: the sum of the mass numbers is the same on both sides, and so is the sum of the atomic numbers, counting charges.

There are several types of decay. In alpha decay, the nucleus emits a helium nucleus, He-4, with A = 4 and Z = 2, so the mass number falls by 4 and the atomic number falls by 2. In beta-minus decay, a neutron changes into a proton and an electron, and the electron is emitted. The mass number stays the same, and the atomic number rises by 1. In positron emission, a proton changes to a neutron and a positron is emitted, so Z falls by 1 and A is unchanged. Electron capture has the same effect on the nucleus. In gamma emission, the nucleus releases energy as a photon, and neither A nor Z changes. Gamma rays often accompany the other types.

The penetrating power is different for each. Alpha particles are heavy and doubly charged, and they are stopped by paper or by skin. Beta particles pass through paper but are stopped by a thin sheet of aluminum. Gamma rays are very penetrating, and they need thick lead or concrete. The order of penetration is alpha, then beta, then gamma, and the order of ionizing power is the reverse.

Which type of decay occurs depends on the ratio of neutrons to protons. Light stable nuclei have about equal numbers, and heavy stable nuclei have more neutrons, up to about 1.5 neutrons per proton. A nucleus with too many neutrons tends to emit a beta-minus particle, which changes a neutron into a proton. A nucleus with too few neutrons tends to undergo positron emission or electron capture. Very heavy nuclei, above bismuth, usually decay by emitting alpha particles.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Alpha decay of radium-226 (Z = 88).**
1. An alpha particle takes away A = 4 and Z = 2, so the new nucleus has A = 226 - 4 = 222 and Z = 88 - 2 = 86.
2. Element 86 is radon.
3. The equation is Ra-226 -> Rn-222 + He-4. Check: 222 + 4 = 226 and 86 + 2 = 88.

**Example 2: Beta-minus decay of carbon-14 (Z = 6).**
1. A beta-minus decay leaves A unchanged at 14 and raises Z from 6 to 7.
2. Element 7 is nitrogen.
3. The equation is C-14 -> N-14 + e-. Check the charge: 6 on the left, and 7 + (-1) = 6 on the right.

**Example 3: Find the missing particle in Pu-239 -> U-235 + X.**
1. The mass number of X is 239 - 235 = 4.
2. The atomic number of X is 94 - 92 = 2.
3. A particle with A = 4 and Z = 2 is an alpha particle, He-4.

## Common mistakes

A frequent error is to balance only the mass number or only the atomic number, while both must be balanced. Another is to think that beta decay changes the mass number, since it changes only the atomic number. The third mistake is to mix up the penetrating powers, because alpha is the least penetrating, even though it is the heaviest.
