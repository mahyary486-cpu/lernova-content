# Limiting Reactant and Percent Yield

If you have 10 wheels and 3 car bodies, the number of complete cars is set by the bodies, however many wheels you have. The same thing happens in chemical reactions. Reactants are rarely mixed in exactly the ratio of the equation, so one of them is used up first and the reaction stops. In addition, real experiments never give exactly the amount of product that a calculation predicts. This lesson shows how to deal with both facts.

## Key ideas

The limiting reactant is the reactant that is used up first, and it determines how much product can form. The reactant that is not used up completely is in excess, and some of it is left over at the end. The limiting reactant is not necessarily the one with the smaller mass, because the equation compares moles, and one mole of different substances has different masses.

A reliable way to find it is to divide the moles of each reactant by its coefficient in the balanced equation. The reactant with the smallest result is limiting. This works because the quotient tells how many times the reaction can occur with that amount of reactant, and the reaction can occur only as many times as the scarcest reactant allows. Another method is to calculate the product that each reactant could give, and to choose the smaller answer. Either way, the amount of product must always be calculated from the limiting reactant, never from the excess one.

The theoretical yield is the maximum mass of product, calculated from the limiting reactant. The actual yield is the mass that is really obtained, and it is measured in the laboratory. The percent yield compares them: percent yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) x 100 percent. In practice, it is always less than 100 percent. Some of the material is lost when it is transferred between vessels, some reacts in unwanted side reactions, and some reactions do not go to completion. A value above 100 percent is a warning that the product is wet or impure.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Finding the limiting reactant.** A mixture of 3 mol H2 and 2 mol O2 reacts according to 2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O.
1. Divide each amount by its coefficient: for H2, 3 / 2 = 1.5, and for O2, 2 / 1 = 2.
2. Hydrogen has the smaller value, so H2 is the limiting reactant.
3. The hydrogen needs only 1.5 mol of O2, so 2 - 1.5 = 0.5 mol of O2 is left over, which is 16 g.

**Example 2: Theoretical yield.** How much water can form in the reaction above?
1. The calculation uses the limiting reactant, 3 mol of H2. The ratio of H2O to H2 is 2 to 2, so 3 mol of water can form.
2. The mass is 3 mol x 18 g/mol = 54 g, and this is the theoretical yield.

**Example 3: Percent yield.** In the experiment only 45 g of water is obtained.
1. The percent yield is actual / theoretical x 100 percent = 45 / 54 x 100 percent.
2. The percent yield is 83.3 percent, which is a reasonable value for a laboratory experiment.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to choose the limiting reactant by the smaller mass. In this example the hydrogen has a mass of 6 g and the oxygen has a mass of 64 g, but the comparison must be made in moles after applying the ratio. Another mistake is to forget to divide by the coefficient, which gives a wrong comparison when the coefficients are different. Students also calculate the yield from the excess reactant, which predicts more product than can ever form. Finally, do not turn the percent yield upside down, because dividing the theoretical by the actual yield would give a value above 100 percent.
