# Atomic Number, Mass Number and Isotopes

Every atom is described by two whole numbers, and atoms of a single element can come in different forms called isotopes. These ideas let a chemist count the protons, neutrons and electrons of any atom or ion, and they explain why the atomic mass printed on the periodic table is a decimal and not a whole number.

## Key ideas

The atomic number, Z, is the number of protons in the nucleus. It defines the element: every atom with 6 protons is carbon, whatever else it contains. The mass number, A, is the total of protons and neutrons, so the number of neutrons is N = A - Z. In a neutral atom the number of electrons equals the number of protons. For an ion, the electrons are found from Z and the charge: electrons = Z - charge. A 3+ ion has lost three electrons, so it has three fewer electrons than protons, and a 2- ion has two more.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element, with the same Z, that have different numbers of neutrons and therefore different mass numbers. They are written with the mass number, as in carbon-14, or as 14C. Isotopes of an element have almost identical chemical behavior, because chemistry is controlled by electrons, but their masses differ, and some are radioactive. The proton number never changes within an element. If it did, the atom would belong to another element.

Because an element is a mixture of its natural isotopes, the atomic mass on the periodic table is an average. It is a weighted average: multiply the mass of each isotope by its fractional abundance, and add. A simple average of the masses would be wrong unless every isotope was equally common. This is the reason why the number on the table is a decimal, such as 10.81 for boron, and not a whole number.

The average can also be used backward. If the average mass and the masses of two isotopes are known, we can find the abundance of each by writing one equation with a single unknown fraction x for one isotope and (1 - x) for the other. The abundances must add up to 100 percent.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Particles in aluminum-27, 3+.** Find the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in 27Al 3+ (Z = 13).
1. The protons equal Z, so there are 13.
2. The neutrons are A - Z = 27 - 13 = 14.
3. The electrons are Z - charge = 13 - 3 = 10.

**Example 2: Average atomic mass of boron.** Boron-10 has a mass of 10.013 amu and an abundance of 19.9 percent, and boron-11 has a mass of 11.009 amu and an abundance of 80.1 percent.
1. The contribution of boron-10 is 0.199 x 10.013 = 1.993 amu.
2. The contribution of boron-11 is 0.801 x 11.009 = 8.818 amu.
3. The sum is 10.81 amu, the value shown on the periodic table.

**Example 3: Finding an abundance.** Copper has two isotopes, Cu-63 (62.93 amu) and Cu-65 (64.93 amu), and its average mass is 63.55 amu. Let x be the fraction of Cu-63.
1. Write 62.93 x + 64.93 (1 - x) = 63.55.
2. Simplify to 64.93 - 2.00 x = 63.55, so x = 1.38 / 2.00 = 0.69.
3. Cu-63 is 69 percent and Cu-65 is 31 percent.

## Common mistakes

Students often take a simple average of the isotope masses instead of a weighted one. Another mistake is to add the atomic number and the mass number to find the neutrons, when the neutrons come from subtraction. For a positive ion, some add electrons, but a positive ion has lost electrons. Many think that isotopes have different numbers of protons, which would make them different elements. Finally, do not mix up the atomic number, a whole number, with the average atomic mass, a decimal.
