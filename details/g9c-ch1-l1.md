# Classifying Matter

Everything around you is made of matter, from the air you breathe to the water you drink and the rock you walk on. Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. With so many different materials in the world, scientists need a way to sort them into groups, because a good classification lets us predict how a material will behave and how it can be separated or used. This lesson shows the main groups of matter and the questions that decide where a sample belongs.

## Key ideas

The first question to ask about a sample is whether it has a fixed composition. A pure substance has the same composition everywhere in the sample and every time you make it. Water, for example, is always made of hydrogen and oxygen in the same proportion. Pure substances are of two kinds. An element contains only one kind of atom, such as gold, oxygen or iron, and it cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical methods. A compound contains two or more elements that are chemically combined in a fixed ratio, such as water or table salt. A compound has properties that are different from the properties of its elements. Sodium is a soft metal that reacts violently, and chlorine is a poisonous gas, yet together they form the harmless salt that we put on food.

A mixture is made of two or more substances that are only physically mixed. They are not chemically combined, so each part keeps its own properties, and the amounts can vary. Sand and salt can be mixed in any proportion. Mixtures are divided by how evenly they are mixed. A homogeneous mixture looks the same throughout, and you cannot see the separate parts; salt water, air and brass are examples. A heterogeneous mixture has parts that you can tell apart, such as muddy water, a salad or granite.

Because the parts of a mixture are not chemically bonded, a mixture can be separated by physical methods, which do not change what the substances are. Filtration catches solid pieces in a filter, evaporation removes a liquid and leaves the dissolved solid, distillation uses the different boiling points of liquids, and a magnet pulls out magnetic materials such as iron. Compounds cannot be separated by these methods; they need a chemical reaction.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Sorting examples.** Classify gold, water, air and muddy water.
1. Gold contains only one kind of atom, so it is an element.
2. Water is made of hydrogen and oxygen chemically combined in a fixed ratio, so it is a compound.
3. Air is a mixture of gases that looks the same throughout, so it is a homogeneous mixture, and muddy water has visible particles, so it is a heterogeneous mixture.

**Example 2: Separating sand and water.** How can sand be separated from water?
1. Sand does not dissolve in water, so its pieces are much larger than the water particles.
2. Pour the mixture through a filter. The sand stays on the filter paper and the water passes through. This is filtration, a physical method.

**Example 3: Salt water.** How can pure water be obtained from salt water?
1. Heat the salt water until it boils. The water turns to steam, but the salt stays behind.
2. Cool the steam so that it condenses back into liquid water in a clean container. This process is called distillation.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to call a mixture a compound. In a mixture, the substances are not chemically combined and keep their own properties, while in a compound the elements are bonded in a fixed ratio and the properties change. Students also think that a compound keeps the properties of its elements, which is wrong, because salt is very different from sodium and chlorine. Finally, many students think that air is a single pure substance, but it is a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen and other gases, which can be separated by physical means.
