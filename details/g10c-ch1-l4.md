# Electron Configuration

An electron configuration shows how the electrons of an atom are arranged in shells and subshells. It is the key to understanding the periodic table, because atoms with similar outer configurations behave in similar ways. Writing a configuration is a matter of following three rules and a fixed order, and with a little practice it becomes routine.

## Key ideas

Electrons occupy shells, numbered n = 1, 2, 3 and so on, and each shell is divided into subshells named s, p, d and f. A subshell contains orbitals, and each orbital holds at most two electrons. The s subshell has one orbital and holds 2 electrons, the p subshell has three orbitals and holds 6, the d subshell has five and holds 10, and the f subshell has seven and holds 14. A whole shell holds 2n^2 electrons, so n = 1 holds 2, n = 2 holds 8 and n = 3 holds 18.

Three rules decide how electrons are placed. The Aufbau principle says that electrons fill the lowest-energy subshell first, in the order 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s. Notice that 4s comes before 3d, because it has a lower energy. The Pauli exclusion principle says that an orbital holds at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins. Hund's rule says that when electrons enter a subshell with several orbitals, they occupy the orbitals one at a time, with parallel spins, before any orbital gets a second electron. Electrons repel each other, so they spread out when they can.

The valence electrons are those in the outermost shell, and they decide the chemical behavior. A shorthand uses the previous noble gas to stand for the inner electrons: iron is written [Ar] 4s2 3d6. There are a few exceptions to the order, caused by the extra stability of half-filled and filled d subshells. Chromium is [Ar] 4s1 3d5, not 4s2 3d4, and copper is [Ar] 4s1 3d10, not 4s2 3d9.

The configuration also tells how many unpaired electrons an atom has. Unpaired electrons make an atom magnetic, and they show how many bonds an atom might form. For ions, electrons are removed from the outermost shell first, so Fe 2+ loses its two 4s electrons before any 3d electron.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Oxygen (8 electrons).** Write the configuration and count the valence electrons.
1. Fill 1s with 2 electrons, then 2s with 2 more, for 4 in total.
2. Place the remaining 4 electrons in the 2p subshell.
3. The configuration is 1s2 2s2 2p4, and the valence electrons are 2 + 4 = 6.

**Example 2: Iron (26 electrons).** Write the configuration.
1. The sequence 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2 uses 20 electrons.
2. The remaining 6 electrons go into the 3d subshell.
3. The configuration is 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2 3d6, or [Ar] 4s2 3d6.

**Example 3: Unpaired electrons in oxygen.** How many unpaired electrons does oxygen have?
1. The 2p subshell has three orbitals and holds 4 electrons.
2. By Hund's rule, each of the three orbitals receives one electron, and the fourth electron pairs up with one of them.
3. Two orbitals contain a single electron, so oxygen has 2 unpaired electrons.

## Common mistakes

A common mistake is to fill 3d before 4s, while the Aufbau order puts 4s first. Another is to pair electrons in a p subshell before each orbital has one. Students also count all electrons as valence electrons, but only the outermost shell counts. Finally, when forming a positive ion, remember that electrons leave the outermost shell first, not the last subshell that was filled.
