# Magnetic Materials, Motors and Applications

Loudspeakers, electric motors, MRI scanners and hard drives all rely on magnetic materials and on the force that a magnetic field exerts on a current. This lesson brings the earlier ideas together. It explains why some materials are strongly magnetic, how a motor converts electrical energy into rotation, and how a mass spectrometer uses the circular motion of charged particles to weigh atoms.

## Key ideas

Most materials respond only weakly to a magnetic field, but ferromagnetic materials, mainly iron, nickel and cobalt, respond strongly. Inside them, atoms act like tiny magnets, and they group into small regions called domains in which all the atomic magnets point the same way. In an unmagnetized piece the domains point in random directions and cancel. In an external field the domains line up, and the material becomes a strong magnet. This is why a nail is attracted to a magnet, and why an iron core inside a coil makes an electromagnet much stronger.

Heating disturbs the alignment. Above a certain temperature, called the Curie temperature, thermal motion destroys the domains and the material loses its ferromagnetism. For iron this is about 770 degrees Celsius, well below its melting point. Not every metal is attracted to magnets, either: copper and aluminum are not ferromagnetic.

An electric motor uses the force on a current. A coil of N turns, with area A and carrying a current I, placed in a field B, feels a torque that tends to turn it: tau = N I A B sin(theta). The torque is largest when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field. To keep the coil turning, a DC motor uses a commutator, a split ring that reverses the current each half turn, so the torque keeps the same direction. Without it, the coil would just swing back and forth. Increasing the current, the number of turns, the area or the field increases the torque.

A mass spectrometer uses the circular path of ions in a magnetic field. Ions with the same speed and charge move on circles of radius r = m v / (q B), so heavier ions travel on larger circles. Measuring where the ions land lets scientists find their masses and identify substances, from drugs to the isotopes of an element.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Torque on a coil.** A coil of 50 turns and area 0.020 m^2 carries 2.0 A in a 0.40 T field, with sin(theta) = 1. Find the torque.
1. Use tau = N I A B = 50 x 2.0 x 0.020 x 0.40.
2. 50 x 2.0 = 100, and 0.020 x 0.40 = 0.0080.
3. The torque is tau = 0.80 N m.

**Example 2: A mass spectrometer.** Ions with m = 3.3 x 10^-26 kg and q = 1.6 x 10^-19 C move at 4.0 x 10^5 m/s in a field of 0.50 T. Find the radius of their path.
1. Use r = m v / (q B) = 3.3 x 10^-26 x 4.0 x 10^5 / (1.6 x 10^-19 x 0.50).
2. The numerator is 1.32 x 10^-20 and the denominator is 8.0 x 10^-20.
3. The radius is r = 0.165 m.

**Example 3: Heating a magnet.** A bar magnet is heated strongly in a furnace and then cooled. Explain why it loses its strength.
1. Heating makes the atoms vibrate more and the domains become random.
2. Above the Curie temperature (770 degrees Celsius for iron), the alignment is lost completely.
3. After cooling, the domains point in random directions, so there is no net magnetism until it is magnetized again.

## Common mistakes

Students often think that all metals are attracted to magnets, but only ferromagnetic ones are. Another mistake is to forget the commutator in a DC motor, which would otherwise reverse the torque every half turn. Many confuse the Curie temperature with the melting point; a magnet loses its magnetism long before it melts. Finally, in the spectrometer formula, remember that a heavier ion of the same speed and charge follows a larger circle.
