# Kinetic Energy and the Work-Energy Theorem

Moving objects can do work on whatever they hit. The energy an object has because of its motion is called kinetic energy.

## Key ideas
- **KE = 1/2 m v^2**, measured in joules. Mass is in kilograms and speed in meters per second.
- Kinetic energy depends on the square of the speed. Doubling the speed makes KE four times larger.
- **Work-energy theorem: W_net = KE_final - KE_initial.**
- Positive net work increases the speed, negative net work decreases it, and zero net work leaves it unchanged.
- The theorem is useful when time is not given, such as in stopping-distance problems.

## Worked examples
**Example 1: Finding kinetic energy.** A 1500 kg car moves at 20 m/s.
1. KE = 1/2 m v^2.
2. KE = 0.5 x 1500 x 20^2 = 0.5 x 1500 x 400.
3. KE = 300000 J.

**Example 2: Finding speed.** Net work of 40 J is done on a 2 kg object at rest.
1. W_net = KE_final - 0, so 40 = 1/2 x 2 x v^2.
2. v^2 = 40, so v = 6.3 m/s.

**Example 3: Stopping distance.** A 800 kg car at 15 m/s is stopped by a constant 4000 N braking force.
1. KE_initial = 0.5 x 800 x 15^2 = 0.5 x 800 x 225 = 90000 J.
2. KE_final = 0, so W_net = -90000 J.
3. The braking force is opposite to the motion: -4000 x d = -90000.
4. d = 22.5 m.

## Common mistakes
- Forgetting to square the speed, or forgetting the factor 1/2.
- Mixing up the change in kinetic energy with the final kinetic energy.
- Using only one force when the theorem needs the net work of all forces.
- Forgetting that stopping distance grows with the square of the speed: twice as fast needs four times the distance.
