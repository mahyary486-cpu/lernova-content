# Kinetic Energy and the Work-Energy Theorem

A moving object can do work on whatever it hits: a hammer drives a nail, a moving car crumples a fence, and a flowing river turns a wheel. The energy that an object has because of its motion is called kinetic energy. This lesson shows how to calculate it and how it connects directly to work through the work-energy theorem, a shortcut that solves many problems without any need for the time.

## Key ideas

The kinetic energy of an object with mass m and speed v is KE = 1/2 m v^2. It is measured in joules when the mass is in kilograms and the speed in meters per second. Kinetic energy is a scalar and is never negative, because the speed is squared. It does not depend on direction: a car going north at 20 m/s has the same kinetic energy as a car going south at 20 m/s.

The dependence on the square of the speed has a practical meaning. If the speed doubles, the kinetic energy becomes four times larger, and if the speed triples, it becomes nine times larger. This is why a collision at high speed is so much more destructive than one at low speed, and why braking distances grow quickly with speed.

The work-energy theorem states that the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy: W_net = KE_final - KE_initial. The reasoning is simple. A net force produces an acceleration, which changes the speed, and the work of that force over the displacement measures exactly the energy gained or lost. Positive net work increases the speed, negative net work decreases it, and zero net work leaves the speed unchanged.

The theorem is especially useful when the time is not given or not needed. Stopping-distance problems are the best example: if you know the initial speed and the braking force, you can find the distance directly, without finding the acceleration or the time. The only requirement is to use the net work of all the forces, and to pay attention to the signs.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Finding kinetic energy.** A 1500 kg car moves at 20 m/s. Find its kinetic energy.
1. Use KE = 1/2 m v^2.
2. Substitute: KE = 0.5 x 1500 x 20^2 = 0.5 x 1500 x 400.
3. The kinetic energy is 300 000 J, or 300 kJ.

**Example 2: Finding speed.** A net work of 40 J is done on a 2.0 kg object that starts at rest. Find its final speed.
1. The theorem gives W_net = KE_final - 0, so 40 = 1/2 x 2.0 x v^2.
2. This simplifies to v^2 = 40.
3. The speed is v = sqrt(40) = 6.3 m/s.

**Example 3: Stopping distance.** An 800 kg car moving at 15 m/s is stopped by a constant braking force of 4000 N. Find the stopping distance.
1. The initial kinetic energy is 0.5 x 800 x 15^2 = 0.5 x 800 x 225 = 90 000 J, and the final value is zero.
2. The net work is W_net = 0 - 90 000 = -90 000 J.
3. The braking force is opposite to the motion, so -4000 x d = -90 000.
4. The distance is d = 22.5 m.

## Common mistakes

A common error is to forget to square the speed, or to forget the factor 1/2 in the formula. Another is to confuse the change in kinetic energy with the final kinetic energy; the theorem uses the difference. Students also use the work of only one force when the theorem needs the net work of all forces together. Finally, remember that stopping distance grows with the square of the speed: a car moving twice as fast needs four times the distance to stop with the same braking force.
