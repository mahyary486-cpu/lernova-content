# Molecular Shapes and Polarity

The shape of a molecule decides many of its properties, from its boiling point to the way it dissolves. Water is bent, and that is why it is a liquid at room temperature, while carbon dioxide is linear, and it is a gas. The shape is predicted with a simple idea, the valence shell electron pair repulsion theory, usually called VSEPR. A second idea, polarity, combines the shape with the pull of each atom on the shared electrons.

## Key ideas

VSEPR theory says that the electron pairs around a central atom repel each other and spread as far apart as possible. To find the shape, count the electron groups on the central atom, which means bonds (a double or triple bond counts as one group) and lone pairs. Lone pairs take up more room than bonding pairs, and so they squeeze the bond angles together.

The common shapes are these. Two groups with no lone pairs give a linear shape, with an angle of 180 degrees, as in CO2. Three groups with no lone pairs give a trigonal planar shape, 120 degrees, as in BF3. Four groups with no lone pairs give a tetrahedral shape, 109.5 degrees, as in CH4. Four groups with one lone pair give a trigonal pyramidal shape, about 107 degrees, as in NH3. Four groups with two lone pairs give a bent shape, about 104.5 degrees, as in H2O. Six groups with no lone pairs give an octahedral shape, 90 degrees, as in SF6. The name of the shape describes only the atoms, not the lone pairs, which is why NH3 is pyramidal and not tetrahedral.

Bond polarity comes from the electronegativity difference. A difference below 0.4 gives a nonpolar covalent bond, one from 0.4 to 1.7 gives a polar covalent bond, and one above 1.7 gives an ionic bond. In a polar bond, the more electronegative atom carries a partial negative charge. A molecule is polar only when it has polar bonds and its shape is asymmetric, so that the bond dipoles do not cancel. Carbon dioxide has two polar bonds, but they point in opposite directions, so the molecule is nonpolar. Polar molecules attract each other more strongly, and they dissolve in polar solvents such as water, which follows the rule that like dissolves like.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Shape of H2O.** Predict the shape and polarity.
1. The valence electrons are 2 x 1 + 6 = 8, so oxygen has 4 pairs around it.
2. Two are bonding pairs and two are lone pairs.
3. The shape is bent, with an angle of about 104.5 degrees, and the molecule is polar.

**Example 2: Bond type in HCl.** Use EN of H = 2.1 and of Cl = 3.0.
1. The difference is 3.0 - 2.1 = 0.9.
2. This lies between 0.4 and 1.7, so the bond is polar covalent.
3. Chlorine carries the partial negative charge.

**Example 3: Is CCl4 polar?** Use EN of C = 2.5 and of Cl = 3.0.
1. Each C-Cl bond is polar, since the difference is 0.5.
2. The molecule is tetrahedral and symmetric, so the four dipoles cancel.
3. CCl4 is nonpolar, while CHCl3 is polar, because the hydrogen breaks the symmetry.

## Common mistakes

Students often use the flat drawing on paper as the shape, but a flat drawing of CH4 does not show the tetrahedron. Another mistake is to say that every molecule with polar bonds is polar. Some ignore lone pairs when naming the shape, so they call NH3 trigonal planar, which is wrong. Finally, remember that a double bond counts as one electron group when predicting the shape.
