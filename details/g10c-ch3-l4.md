# Molecular Shapes and Polarity

The shape of a molecule decides many of its properties. **VSEPR theory** (valence shell electron pair repulsion) says that electron pairs around a central atom repel each other and spread as far apart as possible.

## Key ideas
Count electron groups (bonds and lone pairs) on the central atom. Lone pairs take more room than bonding pairs.
- 2 groups, no lone pairs: **linear**, 180 degrees (CO2, BeCl2).
- 3 groups, no lone pairs: **trigonal planar**, 120 degrees (BF3).
- 4 groups, no lone pairs: **tetrahedral**, 109.5 degrees (CH4).
- 4 groups, 1 lone pair: **trigonal pyramidal**, about 107 degrees (NH3).
- 4 groups, 2 lone pairs: **bent**, about 104.5 degrees (H2O).
- 6 groups, no lone pairs: **octahedral**, 90 degrees (SF6).

**Electronegativity** is the pull of an atom on shared electrons. Use the difference between the two atoms:
- below 0.4: nonpolar covalent
- 0.4 to 1.7: polar covalent
- above 1.7: ionic

A **polar molecule** needs polar bonds and an asymmetric shape so that the bond dipoles do not cancel.

## Worked examples
**Example 1: Shape of H2O.**
1. Valence electrons: 2 x 1 + 6 = 8, so 4 pairs on O.
2. Two bonding pairs and two lone pairs.
3. Shape: bent, about 104.5 degrees. The molecule is polar.

**Example 2: Bond type in HCl (H = 2.1, Cl = 3.0).**
1. Difference = 3.0 - 2.1 = 0.9.
2. 0.9 lies between 0.4 and 1.7, so the bond is polar covalent. Chlorine carries the partial negative charge.

**Example 3: Is CCl4 polar?**
1. Each C-Cl bond is polar (3.0 - 2.5 = 0.5).
2. The shape is tetrahedral and symmetric, so the four dipoles cancel.
3. CCl4 is nonpolar, but CHCl3 is polar because H breaks the symmetry.

## Common mistakes
- Using the Lewis drawing on paper as the shape. A flat drawing of CH4 does not show the tetrahedron.
- Saying every molecule with polar bonds is polar.
- Ignoring lone pairs when naming the shape: NH3 is not trigonal planar.
