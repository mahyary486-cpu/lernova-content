# Magnetic Fields and Forces on Moving Charges

Charged particles streaming from the Sun are caught by Earth's magnetic field and guided along its field lines toward the poles. There they strike the atmosphere and create the glowing lights of the aurora. The same kind of force steers electron beams in old television tubes and in particle accelerators. This lesson explains how a magnetic field acts on a moving charge, and why the motion it produces is circular.

## Key ideas

A magnetic field, B, is a vector field around magnets and electric currents. Its SI unit is the tesla (T). Outside a magnet, the field lines run from the north pole to the south pole. Earth's field is about 5 x 10^-5 T, while a strong laboratory magnet reaches several teslas. A magnetic field acts only on charges that are moving; a charge at rest feels no magnetic force.

For a charge q moving with speed v at an angle theta to the field, the magnetic force has the size F = q v B sin(theta). The direction is perpendicular to both the velocity and the field, found with the right-hand rule: point the fingers along v, curl them toward B, and the thumb gives the force on a positive charge. For a negative charge the force is reversed. If the velocity is parallel to the field (theta = 0), the force is zero, and it is largest when the velocity is perpendicular to the field.

Because the force is always perpendicular to the velocity, it does no work on the particle. It changes the direction of the motion but never the speed or the kinetic energy. When the velocity is perpendicular to a uniform field, the magnetic force plays the role of the centripetal force, and the particle moves in a circle. Setting q v B = m v^2 / r gives the radius r = m v / (q B). A faster or heavier particle moves in a larger circle, and a stronger field or larger charge makes the circle smaller. The time for one revolution is the period T = 2 pi m / (q B), which does not depend on the speed. This remarkable fact is what makes the cyclotron accelerator work.

If the velocity has a component along the field as well, the particle moves in a spiral, a circle combined with steady motion along the field. This is exactly what the charged particles near Earth's poles do.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Force on a proton.** A proton (charge 1.6 x 10^-19 C) moves at 2.0 x 10^6 m/s perpendicular to a 0.50 T field. Find the force.
1. Use F = q v B, since sin(90) = 1.
2. F = 1.6 x 10^-19 x 2.0 x 10^6 x 0.50.
3. The force is F = 1.6 x 10^-13 N, perpendicular to both v and B.

**Example 2: Radius of the path.** An electron (mass 9.1 x 10^-31 kg) moves at 1.0 x 10^7 m/s in a field of 1.0 x 10^-3 T, perpendicular to the field. Find the radius.
1. Use r = m v / (q B) = 9.1 x 10^-31 x 1.0 x 10^7 / (1.6 x 10^-19 x 1.0 x 10^-3).
2. The numerator is 9.1 x 10^-24 and the denominator is 1.6 x 10^-22.
3. The radius is r = 0.057 m, or 5.7 cm.

**Example 3: Period of a proton.** Find the period of a proton (mass 1.67 x 10^-27 kg) in a field of 1.0 T.
1. Use T = 2 pi m / (q B) = 6.28 x 1.67 x 10^-27 / (1.6 x 10^-19 x 1.0).
2. The numerator is 1.05 x 10^-26.
3. The period is T = 6.6 x 10^-8 s, whatever the speed of the proton.

## Common mistakes

A common error is to think that the magnetic force speeds up the particle; it only bends its path, because it does no work. Another is to ignore the sign of the charge, which reverses the direction of the force. Students also mix up the angle: the formula needs the angle between the velocity and the field, and the force is zero when they are parallel. Finally, remember that a charge at rest feels no magnetic force at all.
