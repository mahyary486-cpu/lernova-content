# Position, Distance and Displacement

Physics begins its study of motion by asking a simple question: where is the object, and how does that change? Motion in one dimension happens along a straight line, such as a car on a straight road. Because the path is a line, one number with a sign is enough to say where the object is. Position, distance and displacement look similar at first, but they answer three different questions, and mixing them up is the most common source of errors in kinematics.

## Key ideas

Position, written x, is the location of an object measured from a chosen origin on a number line. You are free to choose where the origin is and which direction counts as positive, but you must choose before you start and keep the choice until the end of the problem. Position x = -3 m means that the object is 3 m from the origin on the negative side.
Distance, written d, is the total length of the path that the object actually traveled. It adds up every meter of the trip, including any part where the object turned around and came back. For this reason distance is a scalar: it has size but no direction, and it can never be negative or decrease.
Displacement, written delta x, is the change in position, found with delta x = x_final - x_initial. It cares only about where you started and where you ended, not about the path in between. It is a vector, so its sign gives the direction: a positive displacement points toward the positive side, and a negative one points toward the negative side. This is why you must subtract the initial position from the final position and not the other way round. The order matters, because reversing it flips the sign and gives the wrong direction.

The two quantities are related, but they are not the same. The distance is always greater than or equal to the size of the displacement. They are equal only when the object moves in a straight line without ever reversing. As soon as the object turns around, the distance keeps growing while the displacement can shrink. In the extreme case of a full lap on a track, the runner covers a large distance but ends at the starting line, so the displacement is exactly zero. Both quantities are measured in meters (m) in the SI system.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Out and back.** A hiker starts at x = 0, walks 40 m east (the positive direction) and then turns around and walks 15 m west. Find the displacement and the distance.
1. Write each move with its sign: the first leg is +40 m and the second leg is -15 m.
2. The displacement is the sum of the signed moves: delta x = 40 + (-15) = +25 m, so the hiker ends 25 m east of the start.
3. The distance adds the sizes of the legs and ignores the signs: d = 40 + 15 = 55 m.

**Example 2: Crossing the origin.** A cart moves along a track from x = +12 m to x = -5 m without turning around. Find its displacement and distance.
1. Use delta x = x_final - x_initial, so delta x = -5 - 12.
2. The result is delta x = -17 m. The negative sign tells us that the cart moved 17 m in the negative direction.
3. The cart never reversed, so the distance equals the size of the displacement: d = 17 m.

**Example 3: A three-leg trip.** A bus starts at x = -2 m, drives to x = +10 m, and then returns to x = +4 m. Find the displacement and the distance.
1. The displacement depends only on the endpoints: delta x = 4 - (-2) = +6 m.
2. The first leg is 10 - (-2) = 12 m long, and the second leg is 10 - 4 = 6 m long.
3. The distance is the total path length: d = 12 + 6 = 18 m, which is three times the displacement.

## Common mistakes

Students often add the sizes of the moves to find the displacement. This is wrong whenever the object reverses, because a move in the opposite direction must subtract; only the distance adds every move. A second mistake is to treat displacement as if it were always positive. A negative displacement is a perfectly good answer, since it tells you that the motion pointed toward the negative side. A third mistake is to use the distance when the problem asks for displacement, which gives the right size only for motion that never turns back. Finally, many errors come from forgetting to name the origin and the positive direction at the start, so the signs of later numbers have no clear meaning.
