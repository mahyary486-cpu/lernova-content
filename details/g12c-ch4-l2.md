# Condensation Polymers

Clothes made of polyester or nylon are condensation polymers, built with the release of a small molecule, usually water, at every link. The same kind of reaction builds the molecules of life: proteins, starch, cellulose and DNA. In this process the monomers do not need a double bond. Instead, each monomer has two functional groups, one at each end, and this allows the chain to grow in both directions.

## Key ideas

In condensation polymerization, two monomers join with the loss of a small molecule. Because of this loss, the polymer is lighter than the total mass of the monomers. The reaction is similar to esterification, where an acid and an alcohol give an ester and water. For a long chain to form, each monomer must have two reactive groups, since a monomer with only one group would join once and stop the growth of the chain. A molecule with a single functional group acts as a chain stopper.

A polyester forms from a dicarboxylic acid, which has a -COOH group at each end, and a diol, which has an -OH group at each end. The link between the units is an ester link. Terylene, known as PET, is a polyester used for clothing and drink bottles. A polyamide forms from a dicarboxylic acid and a diamine, which has an -NH2 group at each end. The link is an amide link, -CO-NH-. Nylon-6,6 is a polyamide, made from hexanedioic acid and 1,6-diaminohexane, and each of the two monomers has six carbon atoms, which explains the name. Another way to form a polyamide is to join amino acids, and the amide link in a protein is called a peptide link.

Condensation polymers can be broken down again by hydrolysis, which is the reverse of their formation, since water splits the ester or amide links. This is one reason why polyesters and polyamides are more easily degraded than addition polymers, whose carbon-carbon backbone is very stable. It also explains why a strong acid or base can damage clothing made of nylon or polyester.

Natural condensation polymers are very important. Proteins are polyamides of amino acids, starch and cellulose are polymers of glucose joined with the loss of water, and DNA is built from nucleotides. These natural polymers follow the same chemistry as the synthetic ones.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Water released.** How many water molecules are released when a polyester chain of 100 monomer units forms, 50 of the diacid and 50 of the diol?
1. Each link between two neighboring units releases one water molecule.
2. A chain of 100 units has 99 links.
3. Therefore 99 molecules of water are released, which is about 100 for a long chain.

**Example 2: What monomers form nylon-6,6?**
1. The polymer has amide links, so the monomers must be an acid with two -COOH groups and an amine with two -NH2 groups.
2. Each contains six carbon atoms in the chain, as the name 6,6 says.
3. The monomers are hexanedioic acid and 1,6-diaminohexane.

**Example 3: Which link joins two amino acids?**
1. The -NH2 group of one amino acid reacts with the -COOH group of the other.
2. Water is lost, and a -CO-NH- link is made.
3. The link is an amide link, and in proteins it is called a peptide link.

## Common mistakes

Students often forget that a small molecule is lost in every link, so that the mass of the polymer is less than the sum of the masses of the monomers. Another error is to mix up the ester link, which is -COO-, with the amide link, which is -CO-NH-. The third mistake is to choose monomers with only one functional group, because such a molecule can form only one link and cannot make a chain.
