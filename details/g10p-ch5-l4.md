# Elastic and Inelastic Collisions

In every collision within an isolated system, the total momentum is conserved. What differs from one collision to another is what happens to the kinetic energy. Two steel balls bounce apart almost without losing energy, while a car crash crumples metal and turns most of the motion into heat, sound and deformation. Sorting collisions by their treatment of kinetic energy tells us which equations we may use.

## Key ideas

In an elastic collision both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. The objects bounce apart, and no energy is lost to heat or deformation. Collisions between hard steel balls, and between gas molecules, are nearly elastic. In an inelastic collision momentum is still conserved, but some kinetic energy is changed into other forms such as heat, sound or permanent deformation. Most collisions in daily life are of this kind.

A perfectly inelastic collision is the extreme case: the objects stick together and move off with one common velocity. Kinetic energy is lost in the greatest amount that momentum allows, although not all of it, unless both objects end at rest. Momentum conservation gives the common velocity directly: v = (m1 v1 + m2 v2) / (m1 + m2).

For an elastic collision where the second object starts at rest, combining the conservation of momentum and of kinetic energy gives two useful results: v1' = (m1 - m2) v1 / (m1 + m2) and v2' = 2 m1 v1 / (m1 + m2). When the masses are equal, the first object stops and the second one leaves with the velocity of the first; the two exchange velocities. When a light object hits a heavy one, it bounces back.

The key habit is to remember that momentum is always conserved, in every collision, while kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic ones. If a problem says that objects stick together, you know at once that the collision is perfectly inelastic, and the kinetic energy equation must not be used across the collision. A special case combines the two ideas: in a ballistic pendulum, the collision is solved with momentum, and the swing that follows is solved with energy.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Carts that stick.** A 2.0 kg cart moving at 4.0 m/s hits a stationary 3.0 kg cart, and they couple together. Find the final velocity and the energy lost.
1. The momentum before is 2.0 x 4.0 = 8.0 kg m/s.
2. The common velocity is v = 8.0 / (2.0 + 3.0) = 1.6 m/s.
3. The kinetic energy before is 0.5 x 2.0 x 4.0^2 = 16 J, and after it is 0.5 x 5.0 x 1.6^2 = 6.4 J.
4. The energy lost is 16 - 6.4 = 9.6 J.

**Example 2: An elastic collision.** A 2.0 kg ball moving at 3.0 m/s strikes a stationary 1.0 kg ball elastically. Find both final velocities.
1. The first ball: v1' = (2.0 - 1.0) x 3.0 / 3.0 = 1.0 m/s.
2. The second ball: v2' = 2 x 2.0 x 3.0 / 3.0 = 4.0 m/s.
3. Check the momentum: 2.0 x 1.0 + 1.0 x 4.0 = 6.0, equal to 2.0 x 3.0. Check the kinetic energy: 1.0 + 8.0 = 9.0 J, equal to the 9.0 J at the start.

**Example 3: A ballistic pendulum.** A 0.010 kg bullet at 400 m/s embeds itself in a 1.990 kg block that hangs at rest. How high does the block swing?
1. The collision uses momentum: 0.010 x 400 = 2.0 x v, so v = 2.0 m/s.
2. The swing uses energy: 1/2 v^2 = g h, so h = v^2 / (2 g) = 4.0 / 19.6.
3. The height is h = 0.20 m. Most of the bullet's energy was lost in the collision, so energy conservation applies only after it.

## Common mistakes

Students often use conservation of kinetic energy in a collision where objects stick together or deform. Another error is to forget that momentum is conserved in every collision, even when kinetic energy is not. In the ballistic pendulum, many apply the energy method across the collision instead of after it. Finally, do not average the two initial speeds to find the final speed; the momentum equation weights each speed by its mass.
