# What Is a Force?

Every change in motion has a cause. Objects start moving, stop, speed up, slow down and turn because something pushes or pulls on them. In physics that push or pull is called a force. To predict what an object will do, we first find all the forces that act on it and then combine them.

## Key ideas

A force is a push or a pull. It has a size and a direction, so it is a vector quantity. The SI unit of force is the newton (N), defined as the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s^2, so 1 N = 1 kg x m/s^2. A small apple weighs about 1 N.

An object usually has more than one force acting on it. The combined effect of all of them is called the net force. To find it, add the forces that point in the same direction and subtract the forces that point in opposite directions. When forces act along a line, choose one direction as positive and the other as negative, and then add with their signs.

The net force decides what happens to the motion. If the net force is zero, the forces are balanced, and the motion does not change: an object at rest stays at rest and an object moving at constant velocity keeps moving that way. If the net force is not zero, the forces are unbalanced, and the object accelerates in the direction of the net force.

To keep track of all the forces in a problem, physicists draw a free-body diagram. It shows only one object, usually as a dot or a box, and an arrow for every force acting on that object. The arrow points in the direction of the force, and a longer arrow means a bigger force. Drawing this diagram first makes it much easier to add the forces correctly.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Opposite forces.** A box is pushed with 30 N to the right and with 10 N to the left. What is the net force?
1. The forces act in opposite directions, so subtract them: 30 - 10.
2. The result is 20 N.
3. The net force is 20 N to the right, so the box accelerates to the right.

**Example 2: Balanced forces.** Two students pull on a rope with 15 N each, in opposite directions. What happens?
1. Take the right as positive: +15 N and -15 N.
2. Add them: 15 - 15 = 0.
3. The forces are balanced. The rope does not accelerate; if it was at rest, it stays at rest.

**Example 3: Forces on a book.** A book has 12 N acting upward and 5 N acting downward. Find the net force.
1. Upward is positive: +12 N and -5 N.
2. Add: 12 - 5 = 7.
3. The net force is 7 N upward.

## Common mistakes

Students often add forces without paying attention to direction, so forces that point in opposite directions get added instead of subtracted. Another mistake is to think that balanced forces mean there are no forces; they mean the forces are present but cancel in their effect. Finally, many people believe that an object needs a net force to keep moving at constant velocity. In reality, only a change in the motion needs a net force.
