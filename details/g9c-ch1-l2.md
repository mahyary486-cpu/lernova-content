# Physical Properties

You can describe a block of copper without changing it in any way: it is reddish, shiny, heavy and a good conductor of electricity. These descriptions are physical properties. Chemists use physical properties all the time to recognize substances, to choose the right material for a job and to design ways of separating mixtures. In this lesson we see what counts as a physical property, which ones help us identify a substance, and how they can be used.

## Key ideas

A physical property is a characteristic that can be observed or measured without changing the identity of the substance. After you measure it, the substance is still the same substance. Common examples are color, shape, state, density, melting point, boiling point, hardness, solubility and electrical conductivity. Some of these, such as color, can be seen directly, but others need instruments. A balance is needed to measure mass, and a thermometer to find a melting point. For this reason, a physical property is not only something that can be seen with the eyes.

Physical properties are of two types. Some depend on the amount of matter in the sample, such as mass and volume: a large block of iron has more mass than a small one. Others do not depend on the amount, and they are called intensive properties. Density, melting point and boiling point are examples. Pure water melts at 0 degrees C whether you have a drop or a lake, and for this reason these properties are the best way to identify a substance. Mass alone cannot identify anything, because it changes with the size of the sample.

Physical properties also help us to classify materials. Metals are usually shiny, malleable (they can be hammered into thin sheets), ductile (they can be drawn into wires) and good conductors of heat and electricity. Nonmetals are usually dull, brittle when solid and poor conductors. This pattern explains why wires are made of copper and handles of cooking pots are made of plastic.

Finally, physical properties are the basis of many separation methods. Iron is magnetic and sand is not, so a magnet can separate them. Salt dissolves in water and sand does not, so water can separate them. Liquids with different boiling points can be separated by distillation. In each case, we choose a property in which the substances differ.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: Identifying a metal.** A shiny solid conducts electricity and can be hammered flat without breaking. What kind of material is it?
1. Shiny appearance, electrical conductivity and malleability are all physical properties of metals.
2. A nonmetal would be dull and would shatter when hit, so the solid is most probably a metal.

**Example 2: Separating by a property.** How can iron filings be separated from sand?
1. Iron is attracted to magnets and sand is not, so magnetism is the property that differs.
2. Move a magnet over the mixture, and the iron filings stick to it while the sand stays behind.

**Example 3: Using melting point.** A pure solid melts sharply at 0 degrees C. What could it be?
1. A pure substance has a fixed melting point, and this value does not depend on the amount.
2. A melting point of 0 degrees C is the property of ice, so the solid is probably frozen water. An impure sample would melt over a range of temperatures.

## Common mistakes

Some students think that a physical property can only be seen with the eyes, but many, such as density or melting point, must be measured. Another mistake is to think that mass identifies a substance. A gram of gold and a kilogram of gold have different masses but are the same substance, and the properties that do not depend on the amount, such as density, are the ones to use. Finally, students confuse physical properties with chemical ones. A physical property can be measured without changing the substance, while a chemical property describes how it changes into something new.
